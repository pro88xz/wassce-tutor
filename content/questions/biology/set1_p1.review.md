### 1. `bio-cl-01`

- **A.** Irritability (sensitivity)  ✅
- **B.** Growth
- **C.** Reproduction
- **D.** Excretion

Bending towards light is a response to a stimulus (light), so it shows irritability, also called sensitivity.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- **A.** decomposers  ✅
- **B.** producers
- **C.** herbivores
- **D.** parasites

Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, break down dead matter and release minerals for plants to use again.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- producers: Producers make food by photosynthesis.
- herbivores: Herbivores feed on living plants.
- parasites: Parasites feed on living hosts.

### 46. `bio-ec-04`

Carbon dioxide is returned to the atmosphere by all of the following EXCEPT

- **A.** respiration
- **B.** photosynthesis  ✅
- **C.** burning of fuels
- **D.** decay of dead organisms

Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the air; respiration, burning and decay return it.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- respiration: Respiration releases carbon dioxide.
- burning of fuels: Burning (combustion) releases carbon dioxide.
- decay of dead organisms: Decomposers respire as they break down dead matter, releasing carbon dioxide.

### 47. `bio-ec-05`

The flow of energy through an ecosystem begins with

- **A.** decomposers breaking down dead matter
- **B.** herbivores eating plants
- **C.** the sun's energy trapped by green plants  ✅
- **D.** carnivores eating herbivores

Green plants trap light energy in photosynthesis; it then passes along food chains, with some lost at each step.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- decomposers breaking down dead matter: Decomposers receive energy at the end of food chains.
- herbivores eating plants: Herbivores are the second step: they eat what plants have made.
- carnivores eating herbivores: Carnivores come later still in the chain.

### 48. `bio-ec-06`

*Rhizobium* bacteria live in the root nodules of legumes. The bacteria get food from the plant, and the plant gets nitrogen compounds from the bacteria. This relationship is

- **A.** mutualism  ✅
- **B.** parasitism
- **C.** commensalism
- **D.** predation

Mutualism (symbiosis) benefits both partners. Lichens (a fungus with an alga) and the bacteria in the rumen of cattle are other examples.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- parasitism: In parasitism one partner benefits and the other (the host) is harmed.
- commensalism: In commensalism one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
- predation: In predation one animal kills and eats another.

### 49. `bio-ec-07`

Bare land left after a farm is abandoned is colonised first by grasses and weeds, then by shrubs, and finally by forest trees. This gradual change in the community is called

- **A.** eutrophication
- **B.** ecological succession  ✅
- **C.** competition
- **D.** evolution

Each community changes the conditions (soil, shade, moisture) so that new species can establish, until a stable climax community, such as forest, develops.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- eutrophication: Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, which leads to algal blooms.
- competition: Competition between species is part of what drives succession, but it is not the name of the whole process.
- evolution: Evolution is the change in species over many generations, not the change of a community in one place.

_Syllabus: Genes and alleles; dominant and recessive; genotype and phenotype; monohybrid crosses; sex determination; inheritance of blood groups_

### 50. `bio-mg-01`

Two tall pea plants, both heterozygous (Tt), are crossed. What ratio of tall to short plants is expected among the offspring?

- **A.** 1 tall : 1 short
- **B.** 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt
- **C.** All tall
- **D.** 3 tall : 1 short  ✅

Tt × Tt gives TT, Tt, Tt and tt. Three have the dominant T, so 3 tall : 1 short.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- 1 tall : 1 short: A 1 : 1 ratio comes from crossing Tt with tt.
- 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt: That is the ratio of genotypes, not of tall to short plants.
- All tall: All tall offspring would come from crossing TT with any plant.

### 51. `bio-mg-02`

An organism with two identical alleles of a gene is said to be

- **A.** homozygous  ✅
- **B.** heterozygous
- **C.** dominant
- **D.** recessive

Homozygous: two identical alleles, such as TT or tt.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- heterozygous: Heterozygous means having two different alleles.
- dominant: Dominant describes an allele that shows its effect even with one copy.
- recessive: Recessive describes an allele that is hidden when a dominant allele is present.

### 52. `bio-mg-03`

The observable characteristics of an organism, produced by its genes and its environment, are called its

- **A.** genotype
- **B.** phenotype  ✅
- **C.** allele
- **D.** chromosome

The phenotype is what can be seen or measured, such as height or blood group.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- chromosome: A chromosome is a thread of DNA that carries genes.

### 53. `bio-mg-04`

In humans, the sex of a baby is decided by

- **A.** the sex chromosome carried by the mother's egg
- **B.** the number of eggs released
- **C.** the sex chromosome carried by the father's sperm  ✅
- **D.** the mother's diet during pregnancy

Half the sperm carry X and half carry Y: an X sperm gives a girl (XX), a Y sperm gives a boy (XY).

**Why the other options are wrong**

- the sex chromosome carried by the mother's egg: Every egg carries an X chromosome, so the egg cannot decide.
- the number of eggs released: The number of eggs affects twins, not sex.
- the mother's diet during pregnancy: Diet does not decide the sex of a baby.

### 54. `bio-mg-05`

A man of blood group AB and a woman of blood group O have a child. Which blood groups could the child have?

- **A.** AB or O
- **B.** O only
- **C.** A, B, AB or O
- **D.** A or B only  ✅

The father gives A or B; the mother gives O. The child is AO (group A) or BO (group B).

**Why the other options are wrong**

- AB or O: AB needs an A and a B allele, but the mother can give only O; O needs O from both parents, but the father gives A or B.
- O only: The father always passes on A or B, so the child cannot be group O.
- A, B, AB or O: All four groups are possible only when the parents are, for example, AO and BO.

### 55. `bio-mg-06`

A man and a woman both have the sickle cell trait (genotype AS). What is the chance that their child will also be a carrier (AS)?

- **A.** 0%
- **B.** 25%
- **C.** 50%  ✅
- **D.** 75%

AS × AS gives AA, AS, AS and SS. Two outcomes in four are AS, so the chance is 1 in 2 (50%). The chance of SS, sickle cell disease, is 1 in 4 (25%) for each child, in every pregnancy.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- 0%: Each parent can pass on either A or S, so a child can easily receive one of each.
- 25%: 25% is the chance that a child will have sickle cell disease (SS), or will be AA.
- 75%: 75% is the chance that a child will NOT have sickle cell disease (AA or AS).

### 56. `bio-mg-07`

To find out whether a tall pea plant is TT or Tt, it is crossed with a dwarf plant (tt). Some of the offspring are dwarf. The tall plant must be

- **A.** TT (homozygous tall)
- **B.** tt (homozygous dwarf)
- **C.** impossible to tell from this cross
- **D.** Tt (heterozygous)  ✅

In a test cross the unknown is crossed with a homozygous recessive. Tt × tt gives Tt and tt in a 1 : 1 ratio, so dwarf offspring prove that the tall parent carried t.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- TT (homozygous tall): TT × tt gives only Tt offspring, so all would be tall.
- tt (homozygous dwarf): A tt plant would itself be dwarf, not tall.
- impossible to tell from this cross: This cross, a test cross, is exactly how the genotype is found.
