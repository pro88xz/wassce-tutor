## Sense Organs

_Syllabus: Structure and functions of the eye; rods and cones; the pupil reflex and accommodation; defects of vision and their correction; the ear, hearing and balance_

### 1. `bio-so-01`

The cells in the retina that are sensitive to dim light but do not detect colour are the

- **A.** rods  ✅
- **B.** cones
- **C.** cells of the iris
- **D.** cells of the choroid

Rods are very sensitive and let us see in dim light, but only in shades of grey. Cones need brighter light and give colour vision and fine detail.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- cones: Cones work in bright light and are responsible for colour vision.
- cells of the iris: The iris is the coloured ring of muscle that controls the size of the pupil; it does not detect light.
- cells of the choroid: The choroid is a dark layer that absorbs stray light and supplies the retina with blood; it does not detect light.

### 2. `bio-so-02`

A pupil walks out of a dark classroom into bright sunlight. What happens to the pupils of the eyes?

- **A.** They become smaller, as the radial muscles of the iris contract
- **B.** They become smaller, as the circular muscles of the iris contract  ✅
- **C.** They become larger, as the circular muscles of the iris contract
- **D.** They become larger, to let in more light

This is the pupil reflex. In bright light the circular muscles contract and the radial muscles relax, so the pupil narrows; in dim light the reverse happens.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- They become smaller, as the radial muscles of the iris contract: When the radial muscles contract, they pull the pupil wider, not smaller.
- They become larger, as the circular muscles of the iris contract: Contracting the circular muscles closes the pupil down; it does not widen it.
- They become larger, to let in more light: In bright light the pupil narrows, to protect the retina from too much light.

### 3. `bio-so-03`

When the eye focuses on a near object,

- **A.** the ciliary muscles relax, the suspensory ligaments tighten and the lens becomes thinner
- **B.** the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments tighten and the lens becomes thinner
- **C.** the ciliary muscles relax, the suspensory ligaments slacken and the lens becomes thicker
- **D.** the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments slacken and the lens becomes thicker  ✅

This change of lens shape is called accommodation. A thicker, more rounded lens bends light more, which is needed to focus the spreading rays from a near object.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- the ciliary muscles relax, the suspensory ligaments tighten and the lens becomes thinner: This is how the eye focuses on a distant object.
- the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments tighten and the lens becomes thinner: When the ciliary muscles contract, the pull on the ligaments is reduced, so they slacken.
- the ciliary muscles relax, the suspensory ligaments slacken and the lens becomes thicker: Relaxed ciliary muscles let the eyeball pull the ligaments tight, which makes the lens thinner.

### 8. `bio-ns-01`

Which sequence shows the correct path of a nerve impulse in a reflex arc?

- **A.** Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector  ✅
- **B.** Receptor → motor neurone → relay neurone → sensory neurone → effector
- **C.** Effector → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → receptor
- **D.** Receptor → relay neurone → sensory neurone → motor neurone → effector

In a reflex such as pulling the hand away from a hot pot, the impulse goes from the receptor in the skin through the spinal cord to the muscle, without waiting for the brain to decide.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Receptor → motor neurone → relay neurone → sensory neurone → effector: The sensory neurone carries impulses from the receptor, so it comes first; the motor neurone carries them to the effector.
- Effector → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → receptor: The receptor detects the stimulus at the start, and the effector responds at the end.
- Receptor → relay neurone → sensory neurone → motor neurone → effector: The relay neurone in the spinal cord sits between the sensory and the motor neurone.

- **C.** the blood carrying the impulse across
- **D.** the release of a chemical transmitter that diffuses across the gap  ✅

**Why the other options are wrong**

- the blood carrying the impulse across: Blood does not carry nerve impulses; that would be far too slow.

## The Endocrine System

### 15. `bio-en-01`

After a meal rich in carbohydrate, the level of glucose in the blood rises. The pancreas responds by releasing

- **A.** glucagon, which makes the liver change glucose into glycogen
- **B.** insulin, which makes the liver change glycogen into glucose
- **C.** adrenaline, which makes the muscles store glucose
- **D.** insulin, which makes the liver change glucose into glycogen  ✅

Insulin lowers blood glucose: the liver and muscles take up glucose and store it as glycogen. When glucose falls, glucagon raises it again. This is negative feedback.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- glucagon, which makes the liver change glucose into glycogen: Glucagon does the opposite: it makes the liver change glycogen into glucose when blood glucose is low.
- insulin, which makes the liver change glycogen into glucose: Changing glycogen into glucose would raise blood glucose even further; that is the effect of glucagon.
- adrenaline, which makes the muscles store glucose: Adrenaline is made by the adrenal glands, not the pancreas, and it raises blood glucose.

- **C.** the young never need any parental care
- **D.** sperm are placed close to the eggs, so fewer gametes are wasted  ✅

Inside the female, the sperm do not dry out or get washed away, so the chance of fertilisation is high, and the fertilised egg can be protected inside the body or in a shell.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- it can take place only in water: Internal fertilisation is what lets animals such as reptiles, birds and mammals reproduce on dry land.
- the female must produce millions of eggs: With internal fertilisation far fewer eggs are needed.
- the young never need any parental care: Many animals with internal fertilisation, such as mammals, give a great deal of care.

## Human Reproduction and Development

_Syllabus: Male and female reproductive systems; the menstrual cycle and its hormones; fertilisation, implantation and the placenta; birth control; sexually transmitted infections_

### 36. `bio-hr-01`

In humans, fertilisation normally takes place in the

- **A.** oviduct (fallopian tube)  ✅
- **B.** uterus
- **C.** ovary
- **D.** vagina

The ovum is fertilised in the oviduct. The zygote divides as cilia and muscle move it to the uterus, where the embryo implants in the thickened lining.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- uterus: The embryo implants in the lining of the uterus, but fertilisation has already happened in the oviduct.
- ovary: The ovary releases the ovum; it is fertilised after it has entered the oviduct.
- vagina: Sperm are deposited in the vagina, then swim through the uterus to the oviducts.

### 37. `bio-hr-02`

Which substance passes across the placenta from the mother's blood into the blood of the fetus?

- **A.** Carbon dioxide
- **B.** Oxygen  ✅
- **C.** Urea
- **D.** Red blood cells

Oxygen, glucose, amino acids and antibodies pass from mother to fetus; carbon dioxide and urea pass from fetus to mother. The placenta also keeps the two blood systems separate.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Carbon dioxide: Carbon dioxide passes the other way, from the fetus to the mother, who breathes it out.
- Urea: Urea passes from the fetus to the mother, whose kidneys remove it.
- Red blood cells: The two bloods do not mix, and cells are too large to cross the placenta.

### 38. `bio-hr-03`

In a regular 28-day menstrual cycle, counting the first day of menstruation as day 1, ovulation usually takes place on about

- **A.** day 1
- **B.** day 5
- **C.** day 14  ✅
- **D.** day 28

Ovulation is triggered by a surge of LH from the pituitary, near the middle of the cycle. An ovum lives for only about a day after its release.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- day 1: Day 1 is the start of menstruation, when the lining of the uterus is being shed.
- day 5: Around day 5 menstruation is ending and the lining begins to rebuild.
- day 28: Day 28 is the end of the cycle, just before the next menstruation.

### 39. `bio-hr-04`

Which hormone, produced by the corpus luteum after ovulation, keeps the lining of the uterus thick and ready for implantation?

- **A.** Oestrogen
- **B.** Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)
- **C.** Progesterone  ✅
- **D.** Luteinising hormone (LH)

If no pregnancy occurs, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone falls and the lining is shed as menstruation.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Oestrogen: Oestrogen, made by the developing follicle before ovulation, rebuilds and thickens the lining; progesterone maintains it afterwards.
- Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH): FSH, from the pituitary, makes a follicle develop in the ovary.
- Luteinising hormone (LH): LH, from the pituitary, triggers ovulation.

### 40. `bio-hr-05`

In the human male, sperm are produced in the

- **A.** epididymis
- **B.** prostate gland
- **C.** urethra
- **D.** testes  ✅

Sperm are made in the seminiferous tubules of the testes, which hang outside the body in the scrotum because sperm develop best slightly below body temperature.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- epididymis: The epididymis stores sperm while they mature, but does not produce them.
- prostate gland: The prostate gland adds fluid to the semen.
- urethra: The urethra carries semen (and urine) out of the body.

### 41. `bio-hr-06`

Which sexually transmitted infection is caused by a bacterium and can be cured with antibiotics?

- **A.** Gonorrhoea  ✅
- **B.** HIV infection
- **C.** Genital herpes
- **D.** Hepatitis B

Gonorrhoea and syphilis are bacterial and can be treated with antibiotics. Antibiotics do not work against viruses.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- HIV infection: HIV is a virus; antiretroviral drugs control it, but antibiotics have no effect on it.

### 42. `bio-hr-07`

Which method of birth control also helps to protect against sexually transmitted infections?

- **A.** The contraceptive pill
- **B.** The condom  ✅
- **C.** The intra-uterine device (IUD)
- **D.** Vasectomy

A condom stops semen and body fluids passing between partners, so it reduces the risk of STIs, including HIV, if used correctly every time. Abstinence is the only fully certain protection.

- The intra-uterine device (IUD): An IUD prevents implantation in the uterus but does not stop infections passing between partners.
- Vasectomy: A vasectomy stops sperm leaving the testes, but infections can still pass in body fluids.

## Growth and Development

_Syllabus: Meaning of growth and development; measuring growth; growth curves; seed germination and its conditions; epigeal and hypogeal germination; tropisms and plant hormones_

### 43. `bio-gd-01`

Which conditions are needed for a seed to germinate?

- **A.** Water, light and soil
- **B.** Oxygen, carbon dioxide and light
- **C.** Water, oxygen and a suitable temperature  ✅
- **D.** Water, fertiliser and light

Water activates enzymes and softens the seed coat; oxygen is needed for respiration to release energy; a suitable temperature lets the enzymes work.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Water, light and soil: Most seeds germinate in the dark, and seeds will germinate on damp cotton wool without soil.
- Oxygen, carbon dioxide and light: Carbon dioxide and light are needed later, for photosynthesis by the seedling.
- Water, fertiliser and light: Fertiliser and light are not needed: the seed uses its own food store.

### 44. `bio-gd-02`

In epigeal germination, as in the bean,

- **A.** the cotyledons stay below the soil while the epicotyl lengthens
- **B.** the plumule appears above the soil before the radicle emerges
- **C.** the seed germinates without taking in any water
- **D.** the cotyledons are carried above the soil by the lengthening hypocotyl  ✅

Epigeal: the hypocotyl (stem below the cotyledons) grows and lifts the cotyledons into the light. Hypogeal: the epicotyl (stem above them) grows, and the cotyledons stay underground.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- the cotyledons stay below the soil while the epicotyl lengthens: This is hypogeal germination, as in maize and the pea.
- the plumule appears above the soil before the radicle emerges: The radicle always emerges first and grows downwards.
- the seed germinates without taking in any water: Every seed must take in water (imbibition) before it can germinate.

### 45. `bio-gd-03`

Dry mass is a more reliable measure of the growth of a plant than fresh mass because

- **A.** fresh mass changes with the amount of water the plant has taken in or lost  ✅
- **B.** dry mass can be measured without killing the plant
- **C.** dry mass includes the water stored in the cells
- **D.** fresh mass cannot be measured on a balance

Water content changes from hour to hour, but dry mass shows the permanent material actually built into the plant.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- dry mass can be measured without killing the plant: Measuring dry mass means drying the plant, which kills it; that is its main disadvantage.
- dry mass includes the water stored in the cells: Dry mass is measured after all the water has been removed.
- fresh mass cannot be measured on a balance: Fresh mass is easily measured on a balance; the problem is that it varies with water content.

### 46. `bio-gd-04`

In the S-shaped (sigmoid) growth curve of an annual plant, the rate of growth is fastest during the

- **A.** lag phase
- **B.** exponential (log) phase  ✅
- **C.** stationary (plateau) phase
- **D.** phase of decline

Lag (slow start), exponential (rapid growth), slowing, then stationary (no further growth) and, for many organisms, decline.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- lag phase: In the lag phase, at the start, growth is slow while the young plant becomes established.
- stationary (plateau) phase: In the stationary phase growth has almost stopped, as the plant reaches its full size.
- phase of decline: In the phase of decline the plant is ageing and may lose mass.

### 47. `bio-gd-05`

A young shoot lit from one side grows towards the light because auxin

- **A.** collects on the lit side, where it makes the cells lengthen more
- **B.** is destroyed on the shaded side, so that side grows less
- **C.** collects on the shaded side, where it makes the cells lengthen more  ✅
- **D.** makes the cells on the lit side divide faster

This is positive phototropism. Auxin moves to the shaded side, cells there elongate more, and the shoot curves towards the light, which helps the leaves photosynthesise.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- collects on the lit side, where it makes the cells lengthen more: If the lit side grew faster, the shoot would bend away from the light.
- is destroyed on the shaded side, so that side grows less: If the shaded side grew less, the shoot would bend towards the shaded side, away from the light.
- makes the cells on the lit side divide faster: The bending is caused by cells lengthening on the shaded side, not by faster division on the lit side.

### 48. `bio-gd-06`

The radicle of a germinating seed grows downwards whichever way the seed is placed. This response is

- **A.** negative geotropism
- **B.** positive phototropism
- **C.** hydrotropism
- **D.** positive geotropism  ✅

Roots are positively geotropic (they grow towards gravity), which anchors the plant and takes the root to water; shoots are negatively geotropic.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- negative geotropism: Negative geotropism is growth away from gravity, shown by shoots.
- positive phototropism: Phototropism is a response to light, not gravity; and roots are not lit underground.
- hydrotropism: Hydrotropism is growth in response to water, not gravity.

### 49. `bio-gd-07`

Development, as distinct from growth, is best described as

- **A.** the changes by which an organism becomes more complex and mature  ✅
- **B.** a permanent increase in size and dry mass
- **C.** an increase in the number of cells
- **D.** a temporary increase in mass from taking in water

Growth is an increase in size; development is a change in form and function, such as a seedling forming leaves and flowers, or a tadpole becoming a toad.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- a permanent increase in size and dry mass: This is the definition of growth.
- an increase in the number of cells: An increase in cell number is part of growth.
- a temporary increase in mass from taking in water: Taking in water is temporary and is not counted as growth or development.
