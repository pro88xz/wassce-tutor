## Phrases and Clauses

_Syllabus: Structure: phrases and clauses; noun, adjectival and adverbial clauses_

### 1. `eng-clause-01`

Which of these groups of words is a clause?

- **A.** when the bell rang  ✅
- **B.** after the long break
- **C.** in the school library
- **D.** running across the field

A clause has its own subject and finite verb: *the bell* (subject) *rang* (verb). The others are phrases.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- after the long break: This has no verb: it is a phrase (a preposition and a noun group).
- in the school library: This has no subject or verb: it is a prepositional phrase.
- running across the field: *Running* is not a finite verb and there is no subject, so this is a phrase.

### 2. `eng-clause-02`

Which of these groups of words is a phrase, not a clause?

- **A.** when the rain stopped
- **B.** under the mango tree  ✅
- **C.** because he was late
- **D.** that she would come

*Under the mango tree* has no subject and no finite verb: it is a phrase. Each of the others contains a subject and a finite verb, so each is a clause.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- when the rain stopped: *The rain stopped* has a subject and a finite verb, so this is a clause.
- because he was late: *He was* is a subject and a finite verb, so this is a clause.
- that she would come: *She would come* is a subject and a finite verb, so this is a clause.

### 3. `eng-clause-03`

- **A.** adjectival clause
- **B.** adverbial clause of time
- **C.** noun clause  ✅

The clause answers *I know what?* It does the job of a noun (the object of *know*), so it is a noun clause.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- main clause: It cannot stand alone as a sentence: it begins with *that* and depends on *I know*.

### 5. `eng-clause-05`

What is the grammatical name of the clause in bold?

We stayed at home **because it was raining heavily**.

- **A.** adverbial clause of reason  ✅
- **B.** adverbial clause of time
- **C.** adjectival clause

**Why the other options are wrong**

## Sentence Types

_Syllabus: Structure: simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences; sentence functions_

### 6. `eng-stype-01`

What type of sentence is this?

*Musa ran to the shop, but it was already closed.*

- **A.** simple sentence
- **B.** compound sentence  ✅
- **C.** complex sentence
- **D.** compound-complex sentence

Two independent clauses (*Musa ran to the shop* / *it was already closed*) joined by the coordinating conjunction *but* make a compound sentence.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simple sentence: A simple sentence has only one clause. This has two.
- complex sentence: A complex sentence has a dependent clause. Here both clauses can stand alone.
- compound-complex sentence: There is no dependent clause, so it is not compound-complex.

---

### 7. `eng-stype-02`

What type of sentence is this?

*Although she was tired, Hawa finished her homework.*

- **A.** simple sentence
- **B.** compound sentence
- **C.** complex sentence  ✅
- **D.** compound-complex sentence

One main clause (*Hawa finished her homework*) plus one dependent clause (*Although she was tired*) makes a complex sentence.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simple sentence: A simple sentence has only one clause. This has two.
- compound sentence: A compound sentence joins clauses that can each stand alone. *Although she was tired* cannot.
- compound-complex sentence: There is only one main clause, so it is not compound-complex.

---

### 8. `eng-stype-03`

What type of sentence is this?

*The pupils in the front row clapped loudly.*

- **A.** compound sentence
- **B.** complex sentence
- **C.** compound-complex sentence
- **D.** simple sentence  ✅

One subject (*the pupils in the front row*) and one finite verb (*clapped*): a single clause, so a simple sentence. *In the front row* is only a phrase.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- compound sentence: There is only one clause; nothing is joined by *and*, *but* or *or*.
- complex sentence: There is no dependent clause.
- compound-complex sentence: There is only one clause.

### 9. `eng-stype-04`

What is the function of this sentence?

*Close the door quietly.*

- **A.** imperative  ✅
- **B.** declarative
- **C.** interrogative
- **D.** exclamatory

A sentence that gives an order, request or instruction is imperative. The subject (*you*) is understood.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- declarative: A declarative sentence makes a statement. This gives an order.
- interrogative: An interrogative sentence asks a question.
- exclamatory: An exclamatory sentence expresses strong feeling, as in *What a lovely day!*

### 10. `eng-stype-05`

What type of sentence is this?

*When the rain stopped, we went outside, and the children played football.*

- **A.** simple sentence
- **B.** compound-complex sentence  ✅
- **C.** compound sentence

Two main clauses (*we went outside* / *the children played football*) plus a dependent clause (*When the rain stopped*) make a compound-complex sentence.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simple sentence: There are three clauses, not one.
- compound sentence: A compound sentence has no dependent clause, but *When the rain stopped* is dependent.
- complex sentence: A complex sentence has one main clause. This has two, joined by *and*.

## Punctuation

_Syllabus: Mechanics: apostrophes, colons, semicolons, commas and quotation marks_

### 11. `eng-punct-01`

Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** The school has it's own borehole.
- **B.** The school has its' own borehole.
- **C.** The school has its own borehole.  ✅
- **D.** The school has its own, borehole.

*Its* (no apostrophe) shows possession, like *his* and *hers*. *It's* is short for *it is* or *it has*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- The school has it's own borehole.  
  *It's* means *it is* or *it has*. The possessive *its* has no apostrophe.
- The school has its' own borehole.  
  There is no such form as *its'*.
- The school has its own, borehole.  
  No comma is needed between *own* and the noun it describes.

---

### 12. `eng-punct-02`

Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** Bring the following items; a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.
- **B.** Bring the following items: a ruler a pencil and an eraser.
- **C.** Bring, the following items: a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.
- **D.** Bring the following items: a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.  ✅

A colon introduces a list after a complete clause, and commas separate the items: *Bring the following items: a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.*

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Bring the following items; a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.  
  A semicolon joins two complete clauses. To introduce a list, use a colon.
- Bring the following items: a ruler a pencil and an eraser.  
  Items in a list are separated by commas.
- Bring, the following items: a ruler, a pencil and an eraser.  
  No comma is needed between the verb *bring* and its object.

---

### 13. `eng-punct-03`

Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** "We will win the match," said the captain.  ✅
- **B.** "We will win the match" said the captain.
- **C.** "We will win the match." said the captain.
- **D.** We will win the match, "said the captain."

Direct speech goes inside quotation marks, and when the reporting words follow, the spoken words end with a comma inside the closing mark.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- "We will win the match" said the captain.  
  When the spoken words come first, a comma goes inside the closing quotation mark.
- "We will win the match." said the captain.  
  A full stop would end the sentence too early. Use a comma before *said*.
- We will win the match, "said the captain.": Only the words actually spoken go inside the quotation marks.

---

### 14. `eng-punct-04`

Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** It was getting late, we decided to go home.
- **B.** It was getting late; we decided to go home.  ✅
- **C.** It was getting late; and we decided to go home.
- **D.** It was getting late we decided to go home.

Two closely related complete sentences can be joined with a semicolon: *It was getting late; we decided to go home.* (Or: *It was getting late, so we decided to go home.*)

**Why the other options are wrong**

- It was getting late, we decided to go home.  
  A comma alone cannot join two complete sentences (a comma splice).
- It was getting late; and we decided to go home.  
  Use either a semicolon or a comma with *and*, not a semicolon with *and*.
- It was getting late we decided to go home.  
  Two complete sentences run together with no punctuation (a run-on sentence).

### 15. `eng-punct-05`

Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** She asked me where the library was?
- **B.** She asked me, where the library was.
- **C.** She asked me where the library was.  ✅
- **D.** She asked me where was the library?

A reported (indirect) question is not itself a question: it takes statement word order and a full stop.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- She asked me where the library was?  
  A reported question is a statement, so it ends with a full stop, not a question mark.
- She asked me, where the library was.  
  No comma is needed before a reported question.
- She asked me where was the library?  
  A reported question uses statement word order (*where the library was*) and a full stop.

### 20. `eng-homo-05`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

The soldiers marched ____ the town at dawn.

- **A.** threw
- **B.** thru
- **C.** though
- **D.** through  ✅

*Through* (from one side to the other) and *threw* (past of *throw*) are homophones. Use the full spelling *through* in examinations.

## Figures of Speech

_Syllabus: Lexis: simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, alliteration_

### 21. `eng-fig-01`

Which figure of speech is used in this sentence?

*The classroom was as quiet as a graveyard.*

- **A.** simile  ✅
- **B.** metaphor
- **C.** personification
- **D.** hyperbole

A simile compares two different things using *like* or *as*: *as quiet as a graveyard*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- metaphor: A metaphor says one thing **is** another. This uses *as… as* to compare.
- personification: Nothing here is given human qualities.
- hyperbole: Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration; the key feature here is the comparison with *as*.

---

### 22. `eng-fig-02`

Which figure of speech is used in this sentence?

*The wind whispered through the palm trees.*

- **A.** simile
- **B.** personification  ✅
- **C.** metaphor
- **D.** alliteration

Personification gives human qualities or actions to things that are not human: the wind *whispered*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simile: There is no comparison with *like* or *as*.
- metaphor: The wind is not said to **be** something else; it is given a human action.
- alliteration: Alliteration is the repetition of the same first sound, which is not the point here.

---

### 23. `eng-fig-03`

Which figure of speech is used in this sentence?

*I have told you a million times to shut that door!*

- **A.** simile
- **B.** irony
- **C.** hyperbole  ✅
- **D.** personification

Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration for effect: nobody has really said it *a million times*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simile: There is no comparison with *like* or *as*.
- irony: Irony says the opposite of what is meant. The speaker means *many times*, exaggerated.
- personification: Nothing is given human qualities.

---

### 24. `eng-fig-04`

Which figure of speech is used in this sentence?

*Our principal is a lion when pupils break the rules.*

- **A.** simile
- **B.** personification
- **C.** euphemism
- **D.** metaphor  ✅

A metaphor says one thing **is** another to show a shared quality, without *like* or *as*: the principal is fierce, like a lion.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simile: A simile would use *like* or *as* (*like a lion*). This says he **is** a lion.
- personification: Personification gives human qualities to non-human things; here a person is compared to an animal.
- euphemism: A euphemism is a gentle word for something unpleasant.

---

### 25. `eng-fig-05`

Which figure of speech is used in this sentence?

*Fatu found five fresh fish at the fishing wharf.*

- **A.** alliteration  ✅
- **B.** simile
- **C.** metaphor
- **D.** hyperbole

Alliteration repeats the same first consonant sound in words close together: *Fatu found five fresh fish… fishing*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- simile: There is no comparison with *like* or *as*.
- metaphor: Nothing is said to **be** something else.
- hyperbole: There is no exaggeration.

### 26. `eng-form-01`

Without a ladder, it was ____ for Sorie to reach the top shelf.

- **A.** unpossible
- **B.** impossible  ✅

The negative prefix *in-* changes to *im-* before *p*, *b* and *m*: *impossible*, *imbalance*, *immature*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- unpossible: *Possible* does not take *un-*; its negative is *impossible*.
- inpossible: Before *p*, the prefix *in-* becomes *im-*: *impossible*.
- dispossible: *Dis-* does not combine with *possible*.
