## Approaching a Comprehension Passage

_Syllabus: Comprehension: reading in stages; literal, inferential, figurative, grammatical and vocabulary questions; answering exactly what is asked, from the passage_

### 1. `eng-ac-01`

*What does the writer mean by "the city never sleeps"?* This is

- **A.** a figurative question  ✅
- **B.** a literal question
- **C.** a grammatical question
- **D.** a vocabulary question

A figurative question asks you to explain an image or expression in plain words: here, that the city is busy and active day and night.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- a literal question: A literal question asks for a fact stated in the passage, such as *What…? Where…? When…?*
- a grammatical question: A grammatical question asks for the name and function of a phrase or clause.
- a vocabulary question: A vocabulary question asks for a word that could replace one in the passage.

### 2. `eng-ac-02`

Which is the best way to work through a comprehension test?

- **A.** Answer the questions first, then read the passage
- **B.** Read the passage quickly for the main idea, read all the questions, then reread carefully to find each answer  ✅
- **C.** Read only the first paragraph, then answer all the questions
- **D.** Read the questions and answer from general knowledge

Reading in stages works: first for the big picture, then the questions, then a slow hunt for each answer, marking the lines in the margin.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Answer the questions first, then read the passage: Without reading the passage, the answers can only be guesses.
- Read only the first paragraph, then answer all the questions: Answers may come from any part of the passage.
- Read the questions and answer from general knowledge: Every answer must come from the passage, not from general knowledge.

### 3. `eng-ac-03`

A question asks: *Why do you think the old man refused the money?* The passage never says why directly. The candidate should

- **A.** copy the sentence in which the money is mentioned
- **B.** give a personal opinion with no link to the passage
- **C.** work out the reason from clues in the passage  ✅
- **D.** explain the meaning of the word *refused*

This is an inferential question: the answer is suggested, not stated, so it must be built from details the passage gives.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- copy the sentence in which the money is mentioned: Copying the sentence does not give a reason; the question asks you to infer one.
- give a personal opinion with no link to the passage: Inferences must be supported by the passage, not invented.
- explain the meaning of the word *refused*: The question asks why, not what a word means.

### 4. `eng-ac-04`

In a vocabulary question (*find another word or phrase that could replace … as used in the passage*), the replacement must

- **A.** be any word with a similar spelling
- **B.** mean the opposite of the word in the passage
- **C.** be a long explanation of the word
- **D.** fit the sentence in the passage, with the same meaning and the same word class  ✅

Test your answer by putting it into the sentence: if it reads correctly and keeps the meaning, it is right. A noun must be replaced by a noun, a verb by a verb in the same form.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- be any word with a similar spelling: Spelling is irrelevant; meaning and word class are what matter.
- mean the opposite of the word in the passage: The replacement must mean the same, not the opposite.
- be a long explanation of the word: A replacement is a word or short phrase that could stand in the sentence.

### 15. `eng-fl-01`

- **B.** Your's faithfully,

*Yours faithfully,*: capital *Y*, small *f*, a comma. Then the signature and the full name.

- introduces a new character in the last line with no meaning: A random new character confuses rather than surprises.
- tells the reader that the story was a dream: The dream ending is a cliché, not a twist.

### 83. `eng-cw-06`

Telling a story in the third person (*he*, *she*, *they*) gives the writer

- **A.** a close view of one mind only
- **B.** no need to describe anything
- **C.** more distance and the freedom to show several characters  ✅
- **D.** permission to change point of view in every paragraph

First person is intimate and immediate; third person can move between characters.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- a close view of one mind only: A close view of one mind is the strength of the first person.
- no need to describe anything: Every point of view needs description.
- permission to change point of view in every paragraph: Point of view should stay consistent.

### 84. `eng-cw-07`

Which sentence brings a character to life most effectively?

- **A.** Pa Kamara was a nice old man.
- **B.** Pa Kamara was a man who was old and nice and good.
- **C.** Pa Kamara was a character in the story.
- **D.** Pa Kamara tapped his walking stick twice before every sentence, as if calling the room to order.  ✅

One telling habit or detail shows a character in a few strokes.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Pa Kamara was a nice old man.  
  *Nice* tells us nothing the reader can picture.
- Pa Kamara was a man who was old and nice and good.  
  A string of vague adjectives does not create a character.
- Pa Kamara was a character in the story.  
  This says nothing about him at all.
