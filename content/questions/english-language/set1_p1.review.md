## Nouns

_Syllabus: Structure: nouns (countable and uncountable nouns, irregular plurals, possessive forms)_

### 1. `eng-nouns-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

The school has ordered some new ____ for the staff room.

- **A.** furniture  ✅
- **B.** furnitures
- **C.** furniture's
- **D.** a furniture

*Furniture* is an uncountable noun: it never takes -s and never follows *a*. *Some new furniture* is correct.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- furnitures: *Furniture* is uncountable, so it has no plural form with -s.
- furniture's: The apostrophe shows possession, not a plural. Nothing here belongs to the furniture.
- a furniture: Uncountable nouns do not take *a* or *an*. Say *a piece of furniture*.

### 2. `eng-nouns-02`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Two ____ stopped to help the motorcyclist who had fallen at the roundabout.

- **A.** passer-bys
- **B.** passers-by  ✅
- **C.** passers-bys
- **D.** passer-by's

In compounds such as *passer-by*, *mother-in-law* and *commander-in-chief*, the main noun takes the plural: *passers-by*, *mothers-in-law*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- passer-bys: In a compound noun like this, the main word (*passer*) takes the plural ending, not *by*.
- passers-bys: Only one part of the compound takes the plural ending: *passers-by*.
- passer-by's: The apostrophe makes it possessive, not plural.

## Pronouns

_Syllabus: Structure: pronouns (case, relative, reflexive and possessive pronouns)_

### 6. `eng-pron-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Between you and ____, I do not think the bus will leave on time today.

- **A.** I
- **B.** me  ✅
- **C.** myself
- **D.** mine

A pronoun after a preposition takes the object form: *between you and me*, *for him and her*, *with us*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- I: After a preposition (*between*) we need the object form, *me*, not the subject form *I*.
- myself: *Myself* is reflexive: it is used when the subject is also *I*, as in *I hurt myself*.
- mine: *Mine* is a possessive pronoun (*the book is mine*). It cannot follow *between you and*.

### 7. `eng-pron-02`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

The girl ____ father owns the shop at Lumley is my classmate.

- **A.** who's
- **B.** whom
- **C.** whose  ✅
- **D.** which

*Whose* shows possession in a relative clause: *the girl whose father owns the shop* = the girl's father owns the shop.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- who's: *Who's* means *who is* or *who has*. *Who is father owns* makes no sense.
- whom: *Whom* is an object pronoun. Here we need possession: the father belongs to the girl.
- which: *Which* refers to things, not people, and does not show possession.

- **D.** herself  ✅

## Verbs and Verb Forms

_Syllabus: Structure: verbs (irregular forms, transitive and intransitive pairs, phrasal verbs, verb patterns)_

### 11. `eng-verbs-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

By the time we reached the gate, the bell had already ____.

- **A.** rang
- **B.** ringed
- **C.** rung  ✅
- **D.** ring

*Ring* is irregular: *ring, rang, rung*. The past perfect is *had* + past participle: *had rung*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- rang: *Rang* is the simple past. After *had* we need the past participle, *rung*.
- ringed: *Ringed* is the past of a different verb, *to ring* meaning to circle something, as in *ringed in red*.
- ring: After *had* we need the past participle, not the base form.

### 12. `eng-verbs-02`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Every morning the sun ____ in the east.

- **A.** raises
- **B.** rise
- **C.** arouses
- **D.** rises  ✅

*Rise* (rise, rose, risen) has no object; *raise* (raise, raised, raised) always has one. A habit or fact takes the present simple: *the sun rises*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- raises: *Raise* needs an object: you raise your hand. The sun does not raise anything.
- rise: With a singular subject (*the sun*) the present simple takes -s: *rises*.
- arouses: *Arouse* means to stir up a feeling, as in *arouse interest*. It does not describe the sun coming up.

### 13. `eng-verbs-03`

After the long journey from Kabala, my grandfather went to ____ down for an hour.

- **B.** lay
- **C.** laid
- **D.** lain

*Lie* (to rest flat) has no object: *lie, lay, lain*. *Lay* (to put down) has an object: *lay, laid, laid*. After *to*, use the base form: *to lie down*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- lay: *Lay* needs an object: you lay a mat or lay a table. Here nothing is being laid.
- laid: *Laid* is the past of *lay*. After *to* we need the base form.
- lain: *Lain* is the past participle of *lie*. After *to* we need the base form.

## Tenses

### 16. `eng-tense-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Kadiatu ____ in Bo since 2019.

- **A.** lives
- **B.** lived
- **C.** is living
- **D.** has lived  ✅

An action that started in the past and continues now, with *since* or *for*, takes the present perfect: *has lived* (or *has been living*).

**Why the other options are wrong**

- lives: *Since 2019* links the past to now, which needs the present perfect, not the present simple.
- lived: The simple past describes finished time. *Since 2019* means she still lives there.
- is living: The present continuous does not combine with *since* + a point in time.

For the earlier of two past actions, especially with *by the time*, use the past perfect: *had left*.

The new classroom block ____ by the Minister of Education next Friday.

- **A.** will be opened  ✅
- **B.** will open
- **C.** will opened
- **D.** is opening

The future passive is *will be* + past participle: *will be opened by the Minister*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- will open: *By the Minister* shows that someone else does the opening, so the verb must be passive.
- will opened: After *will* we need *be* + past participle in the passive: *will be opened*.

### 22. `eng-voice-02`

Which option is the correct passive form of this sentence?

*The teacher marked the scripts.*

- **A.** The scripts was marked by the teacher.
- **B.** The scripts were marked by the teacher.  ✅
- **C.** The scripts are marked by the teacher.
- **D.** The teacher was marked by the scripts.

To make the passive, the object (*the scripts*) becomes the subject, the verb becomes *be* in the same tense + past participle (*were marked*), and the doer follows *by*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

*Look forward to*, *object to* and *be used to* end in the preposition *to*, which takes a gerund: *looking forward to receiving*.

## Adjectives

### 31. `eng-adj-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Of the two sisters, Isatu is the ____.

- **A.** tallest
- **B.** more taller
- **C.** taller  ✅

When comparing exactly two, use the comparative: *the taller of the two*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

Adjectives usually follow this order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, then the noun: *a beautiful long red cotton dress*.

## Prepositions

Use *at* for clock times (*at 9 a.m.*), *on* for days and dates, and *in* for months, years and parts of the day.

- **C.** for
- **D.** to  ✅

*Be married to someone* is the standard pattern, like *engaged to* and *related to*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- with: In standard English the phrase is *married to*, not *married with*.
- by: *Married by* would mean the person who performed the wedding.
- for: *Married for* is followed by a length of time (*married for ten years*), not a person.

## Conjunctions

_Syllabus: Structure: conjunctions (contrast, condition, purpose, correlative pairs)_

### 41. `eng-conj-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

____ it was raining heavily, the inter-secondary sports went on.

- **A.** Although  ✅
- **B.** Because
- **C.** Despite
- **D.** Unless

*Although* (or *though*, *even though*) joins two contrasting clauses: *Although it was raining, the sports went on*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- Because: *Because* gives a reason. The rain is not the reason the sports went on; it is a contrast.
- Despite: *Despite* is followed by a noun or -ing form (*despite the rain*), not a full clause.
- Unless: *Unless* means *if not*, which does not fit two facts that both happened.

---

### 42. `eng-conj-02`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

You will fail the examination ____ you study harder.

- **A.** if
- **B.** unless  ✅
- **C.** although
- **D.** because

*Unless* means *if… not*: *You will fail unless you study harder* = *If you do not study harder, you will fail*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- if: *If you study harder, you will fail* says the opposite of what is meant.
- although: *Although* shows contrast; the sentence needs a condition.
- because: *Because* gives a reason; studying harder is not the reason for failing.

---

### 43. `eng-conj-03`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Neither the teacher ____ the pupils knew where the key was.

- **A.** or
- **B.** and
- **C.** nor  ✅
- **D.** but

Correlative conjunctions come in fixed pairs: *neither… nor*, *either… or*, *both… and*, *not only… but also*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- or: *Neither* pairs with *nor*; *either* pairs with *or*.
- and: *Neither… and* is not a correct pair.
- but: *Neither… but* is not a correct pair.

---

### 44. `eng-conj-04`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Not only did Hawa pass all her subjects, ____ she also won a scholarship.

- **A.** and
- **B.** so
- **C.** yet
- **D.** but  ✅

*Not only… but also* adds a second, stronger point: *Not only did she pass, but she also won a scholarship*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- and: *Not only* pairs with *but (also)*, not *and*.
- so: *So* shows a result; the sentence adds a second achievement.
- yet: *Yet* shows contrast, but the pair here is *not only… but also*.

### 45. `eng-conj-05`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

He ran all the way to the junction ____ he would not miss the last bus.

- **A.** so that  ✅
- **B.** such that
- **C.** in order to
- **D.** so as

*So that* introduces a clause of purpose: *He ran so that he would not miss the bus*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- such that: *Such that* describes a result or a degree (*the noise was such that…*), not a purpose.
- in order to: *In order to* is followed by a base verb (*in order not to miss*), not a full clause with *he would*.
- so as: *So as* is followed by *to* + verb (*so as not to miss*), not a clause.

## Subject-Verb Agreement

_Syllabus: Structure: concord (subject-verb agreement)_

### 46. `eng-concord-01`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Mathematics ____ my favourite subject.

- **A.** are
- **B.** is  ✅
- **C.** were
- **D.** have been

Names of subjects ending in -s, such as *Mathematics*, *Economics* and *Physics*, take a singular verb: *Mathematics is*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- are: Subjects that end in -s (*Mathematics, Physics, Economics*) are singular nouns.
- were: This is plural and past; the subject is singular and the sentence is about now.
- have been: *Have* is plural; a singular subject takes *has been*, and the simple *is* fits best.

---

### 47. `eng-concord-02`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Each of the candidates ____ given a seat number before the examination.

- **A.** were
- **B.** are
- **C.** was  ✅
- **D.** have been

*Each*, *every*, *either* and *neither* are singular: *Each of the candidates was given…*

**Why the other options are wrong**

- were: The subject is *each*, which is singular, even though *candidates* comes just before the verb.
- are: *Each* is singular and the sentence is in the past.
- have been: *Each* is singular, so it would take *has been*, not *have been*.

---

### 48. `eng-concord-03`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

The principal, together with his staff, ____ the visitors at the gate every year.

- **A.** welcome
- **B.** are welcoming
- **C.** have welcomed
- **D.** welcomes  ✅

Phrases such as *together with*, *as well as* and *along with* do not change the number of the subject: *The principal, together with his staff, welcomes…*

**Why the other options are wrong**

- welcome: The subject is *the principal* (singular). *Together with his staff* does not make it plural.
- are welcoming: *Are* is plural, and *every year* calls for the present simple.
- have welcomed: *Have* is plural, and the subject *the principal* is singular.

### 49. `eng-concord-04`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

Neither the boys nor their sister ____ coming to the party.

- **A.** is  ✅
- **B.** are
- **C.** were
- **D.** have

With *neither… nor* and *either… or*, the verb agrees with the subject closest to it: *…nor their sister is*.

**Why the other options are wrong**

- are: With *neither… nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, *their sister*, which is singular.
- were: The nearer subject is singular, and the sentence is in the present.
- have: *Have* is plural and does not form *coming* correctly here.

### 50. `eng-concord-05`

Choose the option that best completes the sentence.

The number of students who passed English ____ increased this year.

- **A.** have
- **B.** has  ✅
- **C.** were
- **D.** are

*The number of…* is singular (*the number has increased*); *a number of…* means *several* and is plural (*a number of students have passed*).

**Why the other options are wrong**

- have: The subject is *the number*, which is singular. (*A number of students have…* is plural.)
- were: *Were* is plural and past, and it does not form a tense with *increased*.
- are: *Are increased* is not the right tense, and *the number* is singular.
