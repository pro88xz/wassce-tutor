**Statistical graphs** present geographical data in a visual form so that patterns and comparisons are easy to see. The main types used in WASSCE Geography are **bar graphs** (simple, multiple and compound), **line graphs**, **pie charts**, **divided bars**, **climate graphs**, **proportional circles** and **pictograms**. Each suits a particular kind of data: bar graphs compare amounts, line graphs show change over time, and pie charts and divided bars show how a total is made up of parts. This lesson explains how to draw each graph correctly, with worked examples.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the importance of statistical graphs in geography
- draw simple, multiple and compound bar graphs
- draw line graphs and climate graphs
- calculate angles for a pie chart and draw it
- draw divided bars and proportional circles
- state the advantages and disadvantages of each graph

## Why use graphs?

Graphs **summarise** large amounts of data, make **patterns and trends** easy to see, allow **comparison** between places or years, and make reports and answers **clear and attractive**.

Every graph needs: a **title**, **labelled axes with units** (where there are axes), a suitable **scale**, a **key** where colours or shading are used, and the **source** of the data.

## The main types

![Four graphs of illustrative data: a simple bar graph of rice output over five years, a line graph of monthly temperature, a pie chart of land use, and a divided bar of the same land use](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/statistical-graphs.svg)

*Figure 1. A simple bar graph, a line graph, a pie chart and a divided bar (illustrative data). WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Graph | Best for | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple bar graph** | comparing amounts in separate categories or years | easy to draw and read | shows only one set of data |
| **Multiple (group) bar graph** | comparing two or more sets side by side (e.g. exports and imports each year) | direct comparison | crowded with many sets |
| **Compound (divided) bar graph** | showing totals and their parts | shows total and components together | hard to compare middle sections |
| **Line graph** | change over **time** (continuous data) | shows trends clearly | not for unrelated categories |
| **Climate graph** | temperature (line) and rainfall (bars) for one place | combines two variables | needs two vertical scales |
| **Pie chart** | parts of a **whole** as a circle | shows proportions clearly | hard to compare similar slices; needs calculation |
| **Divided bar (percentage bar)** | parts of a whole as one bar | easy to draw; easy to compare several bars | small parts hard to see |
| **Proportional circles** | totals at different places, often on a map | striking; works on maps | areas are hard to judge accurately |
| **Pictogram** | simple counts with symbols | attractive; easy to understand | not precise |

## Drawing a pie chart

The angle for each part is:

$$\text{Angle} = \frac{\text{value of the part}}{\text{total}} \times 360°$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The land use of a chiefdom (in km²) is: farming 180, forest 100, mining 48, settlement 40, other 32. Draw a pie chart.**

Total $= 180 + 100 + 48 + 40 + 32 = 400$ km².

For example, farming: $\frac{180}{400} \times 360° = 162°$.

| Land use | Area (km²) | Angle |
|---|---|---|
| Farming | 180 | **162°** |
| Forest | 100 | **90°** |
| Mining | 48 | **43.2°** |
| Settlement | 40 | **36°** |
| Other | 32 | **28.8°** |
| **Total** | **400** | **360°** |

Draw a circle and a radius from the centre to the top (12 o'clock). Measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, starting with the largest, and shade or colour each sector. Add a key and a title.
:::

## Drawing a divided (percentage) bar

1. Convert each value to a **percentage** of the total.
2. Draw a bar of convenient length, such as **10 cm**, so that **1 cm represents 10%**.
3. Mark off each part in turn, from the largest, and shade it.
4. Add a key and title.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Using the land-use data above, how long is the farming section of a 10 cm divided bar?**

Farming $= \frac{180}{400} \times 100\% = 45\%$, so its length is $45\% \times 10 = 4.5$ cm.
:::

## Drawing a line graph or bar graph

1. Put **time** or **categories** on the **horizontal (x) axis** and **values** on the **vertical (y) axis**.
2. Choose a **scale** that uses most of the graph paper and starts at zero for bar graphs.
3. For a bar graph, draw bars of **equal width** with **equal gaps**; for a line graph, plot points and join them with straight lines or a smooth curve.
4. Label the axes with units, and add a title and key.

## Proportional circles

The **area** of each circle is proportional to the value, so the **radius** is proportional to the **square root** of the value.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Town A has 40,000 people and town B has 10,000. If A's circle has a radius of 2 cm, what radius should B's have?**

$\dfrac{r_B}{r_A} = \sqrt{\dfrac{10\,000}{40\,000}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{4}} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so $r_B = 1$ cm.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not make proportional circles with radii proportional to the values. A town with four times the population needs a circle with **twice** the radius, not four times, because area grows with the **square** of the radius.
:::

## Key points

- Every graph needs a **title, labelled axes and units, a scale, a key and the source**.
- **Bar graphs** compare amounts; **line graphs** show change over time; **pie charts** and **divided bars** show parts of a whole.
- Pie chart angle $= \frac{\text{value}}{\text{total}} \times 360°$.
- **Climate graphs** combine a temperature line with rainfall bars.
- Proportional circles: **radius ∝ √value**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **draw a named graph from a table of data** (often a pie chart, compound bar or line graph), to **calculate angles or percentages**, and to **state the advantages and disadvantages** of the method. Neatness, accurate scales, a title and a key all earn marks.
:::

## Quick check

1. Which graph is best for showing change in population over twenty years?
2. A district's crops cover 90 km² out of a total of 360 km². What angle does this give in a pie chart?
3. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of pie charts.
4. What does a climate graph show?
5. Town X has 9 times the population of town Y. How many times larger should the radius of X's proportional circle be?

**Answers:**

1. A line graph.
2. $\frac{90}{360} \times 360° = 90°$.
3. Advantage: shows the parts of a whole clearly. Disadvantage: hard to compare slices of similar size, or it needs calculation.
4. The monthly temperature (as a line) and rainfall (as bars) for a place.
5. $\sqrt{9} = 3$ times.
