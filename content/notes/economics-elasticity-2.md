## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- calculate and interpret income elasticity and cross elasticity of demand
- calculate price elasticity of supply and explain its determinants
- explain how elasticity is used by firms and governments

## Income elasticity of demand

:::callout{type=definition}
**Income elasticity of demand (YED)** measures how responsive the quantity demanded of a good is to a change in **consumers' income**.
:::

$$\text{YED} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in income}}$$

Here the **sign matters**:

| YED | Type of good | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | **Normal good**: demand rises as income rises | Meat, clothes, mobile data |
| Greater than 1 | **Luxury**: demand rises faster than income | Cars, eating out |
| Between 0 and 1 | **Necessity**: demand rises slowly with income | Rice, salt |
| Negative | **Inferior good**: demand falls as income rises | Cheapest staple foods, second-hand clothes |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
A family's monthly income rises from NLe 2,000 to NLe 2,500 (a rise of 25%).

- Their purchases of chicken rise from 10 kg to 13 kg: a rise of 30%. $\text{YED} = \dfrac{30}{25} = 1.2$: a **normal good**, and a luxury for this family.
- Their purchases of gari fall from 20 cups to 18 cups: a fall of 10%. $\text{YED} = \dfrac{-10}{25} = -0.4$: an **inferior good** for this family.
:::

## Cross elasticity of demand

:::callout{type=definition}
**Cross elasticity of demand (XED)** measures how responsive the quantity demanded of **one good (A)** is to a change in the price of **another good (B)**.
:::

$$\text{XED} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded of A}}{\%\ \text{change in price of B}}$$

| XED | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | **Substitutes** | Rice becomes dearer, so more gari is bought |
| **Negative** | **Complements** | Fuel becomes dearer, so fewer cars are bought |
| **Zero** | **Unrelated goods** | The price of pencils and the demand for fish |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
- The price of rice rises by 25% and the demand for gari rises by 25%. $\text{XED} = \dfrac{25}{25} = +1$: **substitutes**.
- The price of fuel rises by 20% and the demand for cars falls by 10%. $\text{XED} = \dfrac{-10}{20} = -0.5$: **complements**.
:::

## Price elasticity of supply

:::callout{type=definition}
**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures how responsive the quantity supplied of a good is to a change in its own price.
:::

$$\text{PES} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change in quantity supplied}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

The answer is positive, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
The price of a basket of tomatoes rises from NLe 10 to NLe 12 (20%). Farmers increase the quantity supplied from 100 to 110 baskets (10%).

$\text{PES} = \dfrac{10}{20} = 0.5$: supply is **inelastic**. Farmers cannot grow more tomatoes overnight.
:::

### Determinants of elasticity of supply

1. **Time.** Supply is more elastic in the **long run**. A farmer cannot increase output until the next harvest.
2. **Perishability and storage.** Goods that can be stored (rice, cement) can be released when prices rise, so supply is more elastic. Fresh fish and tomatoes cannot.
3. **Spare capacity.** A factory with idle machines can raise output quickly.
4. **Mobility of factors.** If labour and capital move easily into the industry, supply is more elastic.
5. **Length of the production period.** Tree crops such as cocoa and oil palm take years to mature, so their supply is very inelastic.

## Uses of elasticity

| Who | How elasticity helps |
|---|---|
| **Firms** | Decide whether raising or lowering price will increase revenue |
| **Government (taxation)** | Taxes on goods with inelastic demand (fuel, alcohol, cigarettes) raise more revenue, because sales fall only a little |
| **Government (incidence of tax)** | When demand is inelastic, most of an indirect tax is passed on to **consumers**; when elastic, **producers** bear more of it |
| **Farmers** | Explains why a bumper harvest can lower farm incomes: demand for food is inelastic, so prices fall sharply |
| **Trade policy** | Devaluation increases export earnings only if foreign demand for the country's exports is elastic |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Suppose a tax of NLe 2 is placed on each bottle of a soft drink. If demand is inelastic, sellers may raise the price by about NLe 1.50 and lose few customers: consumers bear most of the tax. If demand were elastic, sellers could raise the price only a little, so they would bear most of the tax themselves.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In income and cross elasticity, **do not drop the sign**. The sign tells you the answer: normal or inferior, substitute or complement.
:::

## Key points

- **YED** positive: normal good; negative: inferior good; above 1: luxury.
- **XED** positive: substitutes; negative: complements; zero: unrelated.
- **PES** depends on **time**, **storage**, **spare capacity**, **factor mobility** and the **production period**.
- Elasticity guides **pricing**, **taxation**, **tax incidence** and **trade policy**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Questions often give figures and ask you to **calculate income or cross elasticity and state what the result shows about the goods**. Always state the sign and interpret it. Essay questions ask for the **importance of elasticity to the government or to producers**: give each use a heading and explain it.
:::

## Quick check

1. Income rises by 10% and demand for a good falls by 5%. What is the YED, and what type of good is it?
2. The XED between two goods is $-0.8$. How are they related?
3. Why is the supply of cocoa inelastic in the short run?

**Answers:** 1. $-0.5$: an inferior good. 2. They are complements. 3. Cocoa trees take several years to mature, so farmers cannot quickly increase output when the price rises.
