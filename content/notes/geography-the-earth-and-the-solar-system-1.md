# The Earth and the Solar System: planetary order, shape and dimensions

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to describe the Solar System, identify the Earth's position, explain the Earth's shape and state its approximate dimensions.

## The Solar System
The Solar System consists of the Sun, the eight recognised planets, their natural satellites, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, meteoroids and interplanetary material. The Sun is the central star and contains most of the system's mass. Its gravitational attraction keeps planets and other bodies in orbit.

The planets in order outward from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The first four are terrestrial planets: relatively small, dense and rocky. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, while Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only world currently known to support abundant surface liquid water and life.

A planet follows an orbit around the Sun. Most planetary orbits lie close to a common plane and move in the same general direction. Moons orbit planets; Earth's natural satellite is the Moon.

## The Earth's shape
The Earth is not a perfect sphere. Rotation produces a slight equatorial bulge and polar flattening. Its more accurate general description is an **oblate spheroid**. For mapping and precise surveying, the irregular shape of mean sea level extended beneath the continents is represented by the geoid.

Approximate mean dimensions are:
- Equatorial diameter: about 12,756 km.
- Polar diameter: about 12,714 km.
- Mean radius: about 6,371 km.
- Equatorial circumference: about 40,075 km.

The equatorial diameter is roughly 42 km greater than the polar diameter. This difference is small compared with the Earth's total diameter, which is why the planet appears nearly spherical in photographs from space.

## Evidence for the Earth's shape
Photographs from satellites and spacecraft show the Earth's curved outline. During a lunar eclipse, the Earth's shadow on the Moon is circular. Ships moving away over the sea disappear hull-first, while distant high objects remain visible longer. Circumnavigation also demonstrates that a traveller can continue in one general direction and return to the starting region.

Each observation supports a curved Earth; modern satellite geodesy provides precise measurements of its shape and dimensions.

## Worked example: comparing diameters
The equatorial diameter is approximately 12,756 km and the polar diameter approximately 12,714 km.

Difference = 12,756 − 12,714 = **42 km**.

This is the approximate amount by which the equatorial diameter exceeds the polar diameter. Do not mistake this difference for the Earth's radius.

## Why this matters in Geography
The Earth's shape affects map projections, distance calculations, latitude and longitude, satellite positioning and the interpretation of global patterns. A flat map must distort at least one property of the curved surface, such as area, shape, distance or direction.

## WASSCE examination focus
Know the planets in order, distinguish planets from moons and stars, and describe the Earth as an oblate spheroid rather than a perfect sphere. Use approximate dimensions with units and explain at least two independent observations supporting a curved Earth.

## Quick check
1. Name the eight planets in order from the Sun.
2. What is meant by an oblate spheroid?
3. State two observations that support the Earth's curved shape.
4. Calculate the difference between the approximate equatorial and polar diameters.

**Answers:** 1. Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. 2. A sphere slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator. 3. Satellite photographs, circular lunar-eclipse shadow, ships disappearing hull-first or circumnavigation. 4. About 42 km.
