Why does a loaded lorry overturn on a sharp bend while a racing car does not? Why is a bus stable even though it is tall? The answer is the centre of gravity, and how its position compares with the base.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define centre of gravity and find it for regular and irregular laminas
- describe stable, unstable and neutral equilibrium
- explain the factors that affect stability
- use the principle of moments to find an unknown mass or the weight of a beam

## Centre of gravity

:::callout{type=definition}
The **centre of gravity** of a body is the point through which the **whole weight** of the body appears to act.
:::

For a uniform regular body the centre of gravity is at its geometrical centre:

| Body | Centre of gravity |
|---|---|
| Uniform rod or metre rule | its midpoint |
| Rectangle or square lamina | where the diagonals cross |
| Circular disc or ring | its centre |
| Triangular lamina | where the medians meet (one-third of the way up each median) |

The centre of gravity need not be inside the material: for a ring or a horseshoe it lies in the empty space.

### Finding the centre of gravity of an irregular lamina (practical)

1. Make three small holes near the edge of the lamina (a cardboard shape).
2. Hang it freely from a pin through one hole, with a **plumb line** hanging from the same pin.
3. When it comes to rest, mark the line of the plumb line on the lamina.
4. Repeat for the other two holes.
5. The point where the three lines cross is the centre of gravity.

It works because a freely hanging body always comes to rest with its centre of gravity **directly below** the point of suspension. The third line is a check.

## States of equilibrium

![Two blocks tilted about their bottom edge: in the first the vertical line through the centre of gravity G still falls inside the pivot edge so the block falls back; in the second it falls outside so the block topples](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/stability-tilting.svg)

*Figure 1. Whether a tilted body falls back or topples depends on where its weight acts. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| State | When slightly displaced… | Centre of gravity | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stable** | it **returns** to its original position | is **raised** by the displacement | a cone on its base; a ball in a bowl |
| **Unstable** | it **moves further** away and falls | is **lowered** by the displacement | a cone on its tip; a pencil on its point |
| **Neutral** | it **stays** in its new position | stays at the **same height** | a cone on its side; a ball on a flat floor |

A tilted body **falls back** if the vertical line through its centre of gravity still passes **within its base**. It **topples** once that line falls **outside** the base: the weight then has a moment about the edge that turns the body over.

## Factors that increase stability

1. A **low centre of gravity**: heavy parts placed low.
2. A **wide base**.

:::callout{type=local-example}
- Racing cars are low and wide; double-decker buses carry their engines and fuel low down.
- Loading a lorry with heavy sacks of rice on top of light goods raises its centre of gravity, so it overturns more easily on a bend.
- A Bunsen burner and a table lamp have heavy, wide bases.
- A woman carrying a heavy load on her head walks upright to keep the combined centre of gravity over her feet; a person carrying a bucket in one hand leans the other way for the same reason.
:::

## Using moments to find weights

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A uniform metre rule balances at the 50 cm mark. When a 40 g mass is hung at the 10 cm mark, the rule balances at the 30 cm mark. Find the mass of the rule.**

The rule's weight acts at its midpoint (50 cm), which is 20 cm from the new pivot (30 cm). The 40 g mass is $30 - 10 = 20$ cm from the pivot on the other side.

$$40 \times 20 = M \times 20 \Rightarrow M = 40\ \text{g}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A uniform beam 4 m long and weighing 200 N is pivoted 1 m from one end. What force must be applied at that near end to keep the beam horizontal?**

The beam's weight acts at its centre, $2 - 1 = 1$ m from the pivot, on the long side.

$$F \times 1 = 200 \times 1 \Rightarrow F = 200\ \text{N}$$

applied downwards at the short end.
:::

## Key points

- The centre of gravity is where the whole weight appears to act; for a uniform rod, its midpoint.
- Irregular lamina: hang from three points with a plumb line; the lines meet at the centre of gravity.
- Stable equilibrium: displacement **raises** the centre of gravity; unstable: lowers it; neutral: no change.
- Stability increases with a **low centre of gravity** and a **wide base**.

## Quick check

1. Where is the centre of gravity of a uniform triangular lamina?
2. Why does a bus carry its heavy engine low down?
3. A cone resting on its curved side is in which state of equilibrium?

**Answers:** 1. At the point where the medians intersect. 2. To keep the centre of gravity low, making the bus more stable. 3. Neutral equilibrium.
