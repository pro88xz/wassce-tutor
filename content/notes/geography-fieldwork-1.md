:::callout{type=definition}
**Geographical fieldwork** is the study of geography **outside the classroom**, by observing, measuring, recording and analysing features of the real environment, such as a river, a market, a farm, a settlement or the weather.
:::

A good fieldwork study follows clear steps: **choosing a topic and aims**, **planning**, **collecting data** by observation, measurement, interviews and questionnaires, **processing and presenting** the data, and **writing a report** with conclusions. This lesson explains how to plan and carry out fieldwork and how to write it up.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define fieldwork and explain its importance
- describe the steps in planning and carrying out fieldwork
- describe the methods of collecting data in the field
- explain sampling methods
- list the equipment used in fieldwork
- write a fieldwork report with the correct sections

## Importance of fieldwork

1. Gives **first-hand experience** of geographical features and processes.
2. Links classroom **theory** to the real world.
3. Develops skills of **observation, measurement, recording and analysis**.
4. Teaches the use of **instruments** and **maps** in the field.
5. Builds **teamwork** and responsibility.
6. Helps students understand their **local environment** and its problems.

## Steps in fieldwork

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| **1. Choose a topic** | something local and manageable: a stream, a market, land use along a road, a farm, a quarry |
| **2. State the aims (objectives)** | what you want to find out; a **hypothesis** to test, such as "the river gets wider downstream" |
| **3. Reconnaissance (pilot) survey** | visit the area first to check access, safety and what can be studied |
| **4. Plan** | choose methods, prepare questionnaires and record sheets, collect equipment, get permission, arrange transport, divide tasks |
| **5. Collect data** | observe, measure, count, interview, sketch and photograph |
| **6. Process the data** | organise into tables; calculate averages and percentages |
| **7. Present the data** | maps, graphs, tables, sketches and photographs |
| **8. Analyse and conclude** | explain patterns, test the hypothesis, compare with theory |
| **9. Write the report** | in a clear, standard format |

## Methods of collecting data

| Method | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | looking carefully and recording what you see | land use, vegetation, types of buildings |
| **Measurement** | using instruments | river width and depth, temperature, rainfall, slope angle |
| **Counting** | tallying items | traffic counts, number of stalls in a market |
| **Interviews** | asking people questions face to face | farmers, traders, chiefs, officials |
| **Questionnaires** | written lists of questions | where shoppers come from; how often they visit |
| **Field sketches** | labelled drawings of a view or feature | a valley, a settlement, a coastline |
| **Photographs** | a record of the area | before-and-after views of erosion |
| **Mapping** | plotting features on a base map | land-use map of a street |
| **Secondary sources** | records, maps, census data, reports | district population figures |

### Sampling

When you cannot study everything, you take a **sample**:

- **Random sampling:** every item has an equal chance of being chosen, for example by drawing numbers.
- **Systematic sampling:** choosing at regular intervals, for example every tenth house or every 50 m along a river.
- **Stratified sampling:** dividing the population into groups and sampling each in proportion, for example traders by type of goods.

## Equipment

Notebook and pencils, record sheets, a clipboard, maps, a **compass**, a **measuring tape**, ranging poles, a camera or phone, a **thermometer**, a **rain gauge**, a stopwatch and float (for river speed), a **clinometer** (for slope angles), sample bags, and first-aid kit.

## Writing the fieldwork report

1. **Title:** what was studied, where and when.
2. **Introduction and aims:** why the study was done; objectives or hypothesis.
3. **Location:** a **location map** and description of the study area.
4. **Methods:** how the data were collected, with equipment and sampling.
5. **Results:** tables, maps, graphs, sketches.
6. **Analysis and discussion:** what the results show; explanations.
7. **Conclusion:** answers to the aims; whether the hypothesis is accepted.
8. **Problems:** difficulties met during the study.
9. **Recommendations:** suggestions for improving the study or the area.
10. **Bibliography and appendix:** sources used; copies of questionnaires.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**A market study in Sierra Leone:** a class studies a weekly market (a **lumo**), such as one in a district town. Aims: to find what goods are sold, where traders and buyers come from, and how far they travel. Methods: count and map the stalls by type of goods, interview traders, and give buyers a short questionnaire. Results: a pie chart of goods, a map of the market and a **flow map** of where people come from. Conclusion: the market's **sphere of influence** and its importance to the local economy.
:::

## Problems of fieldwork

1. **Lack of funds** for transport and equipment.
2. **Uncooperative respondents** or suspicion of strangers.
3. **Bad weather** and difficult terrain.
4. **Inaccurate answers** to questions.
5. **Limited time.**
6. **Language barriers.**
7. **Safety risks** near rivers, roads and quarries.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not jump straight to collecting data. Examiners reward the **planning stages**: clear aims, a reconnaissance visit, permission, prepared record sheets and a sampling method.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Hypothesis**: a statement to test in fieldwork, such as "the river gets wider downstream".
- **Reconnaissance survey** (pilot survey): a first visit to an area to check access, safety and what can be studied.
- **Questionnaire**: a list of questions prepared to collect information from people.
- **Observation**: looking carefully at features and recording what is seen.
:::

## Key points

- Fieldwork is studying geography **in the real environment** by observing, measuring and recording.
- Steps: **topic → aims → reconnaissance → planning → data collection → processing → presentation → analysis → report**.
- Methods: **observation, measurement, counting, interviews, questionnaires, sketches, photographs, mapping, secondary sources**.
- Sampling: **random, systematic, stratified**.
- Report: **title, introduction and aims, location, methods, results, analysis, conclusion, problems, recommendations, appendix**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **describe how you would carry out a fieldwork study** of a named topic, to **list the steps and methods**, to **name equipment**, and to **state the problems of fieldwork**. Questions often name the topic (a river, market or farm) and ask for aims, methods and how you would present the results.
:::

## Quick check

1. Define geographical fieldwork.
2. What is a reconnaissance survey?
3. Distinguish random from systematic sampling.
4. Name four pieces of fieldwork equipment.
5. List four sections of a fieldwork report.

**Answers:**

1. The study of geography outside the classroom by observing, measuring, recording and analysing features of the real environment.
2. A first visit to the study area to check access, safety and what can be studied.
3. In random sampling every item has an equal chance of being chosen; in systematic sampling items are chosen at regular intervals.
4. Any four: notebook; record sheets; map; compass; measuring tape; ranging poles; camera; thermometer; rain gauge; clinometer; stopwatch.
5. Any four: title; introduction and aims; location; methods; results; analysis; conclusion; problems; recommendations; bibliography and appendix.
