A compass needle swings to point north, a fridge magnet holds a calendar, and a scrap-metal crane lifts a car. Magnetism is one of the oldest known forces — and the key to motors, generators and every loudspeaker.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish magnetic and non-magnetic materials, and hard and soft magnetic materials
- state the law of magnetic poles
- describe methods of magnetisation and demagnetisation
- explain magnetism using the domain theory

## Magnetic materials

- **Magnetic (ferromagnetic)** materials are strongly attracted by magnets and can be magnetised: **iron, steel, nickel, cobalt** and their alloys.
- **Non-magnetic** materials are not attracted: copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass, paper.

| Hard magnetic materials (e.g. steel, alnico) | Soft magnetic materials (e.g. soft iron, mumetal) |
|---|---|
| hard to magnetise but **keep** their magnetism | easy to magnetise but **lose** it easily |
| used for **permanent magnets** (loudspeakers, compasses, motors) | used for **temporary magnets**: electromagnet cores, transformer cores, relays |

## Properties of magnets

- A freely suspended bar magnet comes to rest pointing roughly **north–south**. The end pointing north is the **north-seeking pole (N)**.
- Magnetism is strongest at the **poles**, near the ends.
- Poles always come in **pairs**: cutting a magnet in half gives two smaller magnets, each with N and S poles.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Law of magnetic poles:** **like poles repel; unlike poles attract.**
:::

**Testing for a magnet:** a magnet attracts both poles of a compass needle on one end and repels one of them. Only **repulsion** proves that a bar is a magnet (an unmagnetised iron bar is attracted by both poles).

## Making magnets (magnetisation)

1. **Single touch (stroking):** stroke a steel bar repeatedly from one end to the other with one pole of a magnet, lifting it away at the end each time. The end where the stroke **finishes** gets the **opposite** pole to the stroking pole.
2. **Divided (double) touch:** stroke from the middle outwards with two magnets of opposite poles at the same time. Gives a stronger, more even magnet.
3. **Electrical method (best):** place the steel bar inside a **solenoid** (coil) and pass a large **direct current** for a moment. The polarity depends on the direction of the current.
4. **Induction:** an iron object near a magnet becomes a temporary magnet (this is why a chain of nails can hang from a magnet).

## Destroying magnetism (demagnetisation)

1. **Heating** strongly (above about 770 °C for iron, the Curie point).
2. **Hammering** the magnet, especially when it lies east–west.
3. **Electrical method (best):** place the magnet in a solenoid carrying **alternating current**, and slowly **withdraw** it while the current flows (in an east–west direction).
4. Rough handling, dropping, or storing magnets without keepers.

## The domain theory of magnetism

:::callout{type=definition}
A ferromagnetic material is made up of tiny regions called **domains**, each of which is a small magnet with all its atomic magnets lined up.
:::

- In an **unmagnetised** bar, the domains point in **random directions**, so their effects cancel.
- **Magnetising** the bar **aligns** the domains in one direction.
- When all the domains are aligned, the magnet is **saturated**: it cannot be made any stronger.

The theory explains: why breaking a magnet gives two magnets; why heating or hammering destroys magnetism (the domains are shaken back into disorder); and why there is a limit (saturation) to how strong a magnet can be.

## Storing magnets

Magnets slowly weaken as their poles repel the aligned domains at their ends (self-demagnetisation). They are stored in **pairs**, unlike poles side by side, with **soft-iron keepers** across the ends. The keepers become magnetised by induction and complete a closed loop, keeping the domains aligned.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Old radio and loudspeaker magnets salvaged at Freetown's electronics repair shops are kept stacked with steel pieces across them — keepers that stop them losing their strength.
:::

## Key points

- Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic; steel makes permanent magnets, soft iron temporary ones.
- Like poles repel, unlike poles attract; repulsion is the sure test for a magnet.
- Magnetise by stroking or with d.c. in a solenoid; demagnetise by heating, hammering or a.c. in a solenoid.
- Domain theory: magnetisation aligns domains; saturation when all are aligned.
- Store magnets with keepers.

## Quick check

1. Why is soft iron, not steel, used for the core of an electromagnet?
2. What is the best way to demagnetise a magnet?
3. According to the domain theory, what is a saturated magnet?

**Answers:** 1. Soft iron is easily magnetised and loses its magnetism as soon as the current is switched off. 2. Place it in a solenoid carrying alternating current and slowly withdraw it. 3. One in which all the domains are aligned in the same direction.
