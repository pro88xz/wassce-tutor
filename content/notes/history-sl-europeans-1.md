**European contact with Sierra Leone** began in **1462**, when the Portuguese explorer **Pedro de Sintra** sailed along the coast and named the mountains of the peninsula **"Serra Lyoa"** (Lion Mountains), from which the name **Sierra Leone** comes. Portuguese traders were followed by the **English, Dutch and French**, who exchanged **cloth, iron, beads, alcohol and guns** for **ivory, camwood, beeswax, gold, kola** and, increasingly, **slaves**. Europeans built trading posts such as **Bunce Island** and **York Island**, and **Afro-European trading families** such as the **Caulkers, Tuckers and Clevelands** became powerful middlemen. This lesson explains **the coming of the Europeans to Sierra Leone** and the effects of early trade.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the effects of early European contact on Sierra Leone

## The Portuguese and the name "Sierra Leone"

In **1462** the Portuguese navigator **Pedro de Sintra** reached the coast and named the mountains of the Freetown peninsula **Serra Lyoa**, meaning **"Lion Mountains"**. Writers have explained the name in two ways: the mountains looked like a **lion's back**, or the **thunder** that rolled round them sounded like a **lion's roar**. Later Spanish and Italian forms gave the name **Sierra Leone**.

**Portuguese activities:**

1. **Trade:** in ivory, gold (in small amounts), beeswax, camwood (a red dye wood), kola nuts, cloth and **slaves**.
2. **Settlement:** some Portuguese men, called **lançados**, lived among local people, married local women and acted as traders. Their children became **Luso-African (Afro-Portuguese)** traders.
3. **Art:** Portuguese buyers ordered the famous **Sapi ivories** from local carvers.
4. **Religion:** Jesuit missionaries, led by **Father Baltasar Barreira** from **1605**, preached in the Sierra Leone River area and baptised some local rulers.
5. **Language:** Portuguese words entered local trade languages, and a Portuguese creole was used for trade along the coast.

## Other Europeans

| Europeans | Activities in Sierra Leone |
|---|---|
| **English** | **John Hawkins** seized captives on the Sierra Leone coast in **1562–63** on the first English slaving voyage; **Francis Drake** called at Sierra Leone in **1580** on his voyage round the world; from **1672** the **Royal African Company** built posts on **Bunce Island** and **York Island** (Sherbro) |
| **Dutch** | traded on the coast in the seventeenth century and attacked English posts during wars between England and the Netherlands |
| **French** | traded from posts to the north, competed with the English and attacked Bunce Island in the eighteenth century |

## The goods of the trade

| Exports from Sierra Leone | Imports from Europe |
|---|---|
| ivory, camwood, beeswax, gold, kola nuts, rice and other provisions, hides, and **slaves** | cloth, iron bars, copper and brass, **beads**, **alcohol (rum and brandy)**, tobacco, **guns and gunpowder**, mirrors |

## Afro-European trading families

From the late seventeenth century, European traders who settled on the coast married into local ruling families. Their descendants became powerful **middlemen** and sometimes **rulers**:

1. **The Caulkers (Corkers):** descended from **Thomas Corker**, an English agent of the Royal African Company in the Sherbro, and a Sherbro royal woman; they became chiefs in the **Plantain Islands and Shenge** area and the **Bumpe** area.
2. **The Tuckers:** a family of traders and chiefs in the **Sherbro**.
3. **The Clevelands:** traders based on the **Banana Islands**.
4. **The Rogers:** traders and chiefs in the **Gallinas**.

These families controlled trade between European ships and the hinterland, dealt in slaves as well as produce, and later negotiated treaties with the British.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why local rulers welcomed European traders**

Coastal rulers did not see the early Europeans as conquerors. Traders paid **rent and customs** for land and harbours, brought **useful goods** such as iron and cloth, and could be **allies** in local wars. Rulers treated them as **strangers** under a landlord, and expected them to respect local authority. This "landlord and stranger" relationship explains why early contact was mainly peaceful and why later disputes arose when Europeans claimed to **own** land they had only been allowed to **use**.
:::

## Effects of early European contact

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| new goods such as cloth, iron tools and utensils | growth of the **slave trade**, which brought raids, wars and insecurity |
| new crops from the Americas, such as **cassava, maize and groundnuts** | **firearms** made wars more destructive |
| growth of coastal trade and wealth for middlemen | **alcohol** and imported goods harmed some local crafts |
| contact with the wider world; early Christianity and literacy | foundations of later **European claims** to the land |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say that the Portuguese "discovered" Sierra Leone. The land had been inhabited for thousands of years. Pedro de Sintra was the first **European** known to have reached and named the coast.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Pedro de Sintra**: the Portuguese navigator who in 1462 named the mountains of the peninsula Serra Lyoa (Lion Mountains), the origin of the name Sierra Leone.
- **Lançados**: Portuguese traders who settled on the coast and married into local families; their descendants became important middlemen.
- **Royal African Company**: the English company that traded on the Sierra Leone coast from 1672.
:::

## Key points

- **1462:** **Pedro de Sintra** named the mountains **Serra Lyoa** (Lion Mountains).
- Portuguese **trade**, **lançados**, **Sapi ivories** and Jesuit missions (**Barreira**, from 1605).
- English (**Hawkins 1562–63**, **Drake 1580**, **Royal African Company** from 1672), Dutch and French traders followed.
- Trade: **ivory, camwood, beeswax, kola, slaves** for **cloth, iron, beads, alcohol, guns**.
- **Afro-European families** (**Caulker, Tucker, Cleveland, Rogers**) became powerful middlemen.
