# Human reproduction: reproductive systems and the menstrual cycle

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the main structures of the male and female reproductive systems
- state the functions of the reproductive organs
- explain sperm production and the route taken by sperm
- explain the formation and release of ova
- describe the menstrual cycle and the roles of FSH, LH, oestrogen and progesterone
- distinguish ovulation, fertilisation, menstruation and implantation

## Human reproduction

Humans reproduce sexually. A male gamete (sperm) fuses with a female gamete (ovum) during fertilisation to form a zygote. Fertilisation normally occurs in the oviduct. The zygote divides repeatedly and, after reaching the uterus, the developing embryo implants in the uterine lining.

The male reproductive system produces sperm and transfers them into the female reproductive tract. The female reproductive system produces ova and provides the conditions for fertilisation, pregnancy and birth.

## The male reproductive system

The main structures and their functions are:

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Testes | Produce sperm and secrete testosterone |
| Scrotum | Holds the testes outside the main body cavity and helps maintain a temperature suitable for sperm production |
| Epididymis | Stores sperm while they mature |
| Sperm duct (vas deferens) | Transports sperm from the epididymis towards the urethra |
| Seminal vesicles | Add a fluid containing nutrients, including fructose, to form part of semen |
| Prostate gland | Adds fluid that contributes to semen and supports sperm function |
| Urethra | Carries semen out through the penis; it also carries urine at a different time |
| Penis | Deposits semen in the female reproductive tract during sexual intercourse |

The **testes** are located in the scrotum. Sperm are produced in coiled seminiferous tubules. Testosterone, produced by cells in the testes, supports sperm production and the development and maintenance of male secondary sexual characteristics.

The scrotum helps keep the testes slightly cooler than normal internal body temperature. Excessively high temperature can interfere with sperm production.

### The route taken by sperm

Sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules, mature and are stored in the epididymis, then pass through the sperm duct. They mix with secretions from accessory glands, enter the urethra and leave the body through the penis.

**Semen** is the mixture of sperm and fluids from accessory glands. It provides a medium that helps transport and support sperm. Semen and sperm are not the same thing: sperm are the male gametes, while semen is the fluid containing them.

## The female reproductive system

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Ovaries | Produce ova and secrete oestrogen and progesterone |
| Oviducts (fallopian tubes) | Receive the ovum; usual site of fertilisation; move the early embryo towards the uterus |
| Uterus | Muscular organ in which the embryo implants and the fetus develops |
| Endometrium | Inner lining of the uterus; thickens during the cycle and is shed during menstruation if pregnancy does not occur |
| Cervix | Narrow lower opening of the uterus into the vagina |
| Vagina | Receives the penis and semen during intercourse; forms the birth canal |

The ovaries contain follicles in which ova develop. Usually one mature ovum is released during each menstrual cycle. This release is called **ovulation**.

The oviduct has finger-like projections near its opening that help collect the ovum after ovulation. Cilia and muscular contractions help move the ovum or early embryo along the oviduct.

The uterus has a thick muscular wall. Its inner lining, the endometrium, becomes thicker and more richly supplied with blood during the menstrual cycle. This prepares it for possible implantation.

## The menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is a series of changes in the ovaries and uterus that prepares the female reproductive system for pregnancy. A commonly used teaching model is a 28-day cycle, but actual cycle length varies among individuals and from one cycle to another.

The cycle is controlled by hormones from the pituitary gland and ovaries.

### Main hormones

- **Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)**: released by the anterior pituitary; stimulates the development of ovarian follicles.
- **Luteinising hormone (LH)**: released by the anterior pituitary; a surge in LH triggers ovulation and helps form the corpus luteum.
- **Oestrogen**: produced mainly by developing ovarian follicles; rebuilds and thickens the endometrium and helps regulate FSH and LH release.
- **Progesterone**: produced mainly by the corpus luteum after ovulation; maintains the uterine lining and helps prevent another ovulation during that cycle.

### Stages of a typical cycle

**1. Menstruation (approximately days 1–5)**

If pregnancy has not occurred, the corpus luteum breaks down and progesterone and oestrogen levels fall. The endometrium is no longer maintained and its superficial layer is shed through the vagina as menstrual flow. Menstruation marks the beginning of the cycle.

**2. Follicular phase and rebuilding of the uterine lining**

FSH stimulates follicles in the ovary to develop. A developing follicle produces oestrogen. Oestrogen stimulates repair and thickening of the endometrium. As oestrogen rises, it helps regulate pituitary hormone release.

**3. Ovulation (often near day 14 in a 28-day model)**

A high level of oestrogen from the mature follicle contributes to a surge in LH. The LH surge causes the mature follicle to release an ovum. The ovum enters the oviduct, where fertilisation may occur if sperm are present.

**4. Luteal phase (approximately days 15–28)**

The remains of the ruptured follicle develop into the corpus luteum. It secretes progesterone and some oestrogen. Progesterone maintains the thick endometrium, making it suitable for implantation.

If fertilisation and implantation do not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates. Progesterone and oestrogen levels fall, the endometrium breaks down and menstruation begins again.

If pregnancy is established, the early embryo produces human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), which helps maintain the corpus luteum in early pregnancy. Progesterone therefore remains sufficiently high to support the uterine lining until the placenta takes over much of the hormone production.

## Summary of the menstrual hormones

| Hormone | Main source | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| FSH | Anterior pituitary | Stimulates ovarian follicle development |
| LH | Anterior pituitary | LH surge triggers ovulation; supports corpus luteum formation |
| Oestrogen | Developing ovarian follicles; later also corpus luteum | Rebuilds uterine lining and participates in feedback control |
| Progesterone | Corpus luteum; placenta during pregnancy | Maintains uterine lining after ovulation and during pregnancy |

## Key reproductive terms

- **Ovulation**: release of a mature ovum from an ovary.
- **Fertilisation**: fusion of the nuclei of sperm and ovum, usually in the oviduct.
- **Zygote**: the diploid cell formed by fertilisation.
- **Implantation**: attachment and embedding of the early embryo in the endometrium.
- **Menstruation**: shedding of the uterine lining when pregnancy has not been established.
- **Menstrual cycle**: recurring ovarian and uterine changes controlled by hormones.

Do not use these terms interchangeably. Ovulation is not fertilisation; fertilisation is not implantation; menstruation is not the release of an ovum.

## WASSCE examination focus

- Testes produce sperm and testosterone; ovaries produce ova, oestrogen and progesterone.
- The usual site of fertilisation in humans is the oviduct, not the uterus.
- The endometrium is the inner lining of the uterus; it is not the muscular uterine wall.
- The LH surge triggers ovulation.
- Progesterone maintains the endometrium after ovulation.
- Menstruation follows a fall in ovarian progesterone and oestrogen when pregnancy has not been established.
- A 28-day cycle is a model, not a fixed rule for every woman.
