Three times between 1967 and 1997, soldiers seized power in Sierra Leone and ruled by decree. The **National Reformation Council (NRC)**, the **National Provisional Ruling Council (NPRC)** and the **Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC)** each promised to clean up politics. Their records were very different. This lesson explains why the military intervened, how military regimes govern, and what each regime did.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the causes of military intervention in Sierra Leone
- state the features of military rule
- describe the NRC, NPRC and AFRC regimes
- evaluate the merits and demerits of military rule

## Causes of military intervention

| Cause | Sierra Leone example |
|---|---|
| **Disputed elections** | The **1967** election, when the army stopped Siaka Stevens taking office |
| **Corruption and mismanagement** | The NPRC (1992) blamed the APC's corruption and economic collapse |
| **Economic hardship** | Unpaid salaries, shortages and inflation in the late 1980s and early 1990s |
| **Poor conditions of soldiers** | Soldiers fighting the RUF in 1992 lacked pay, food and equipment |
| **Politicisation of the army** | Recruitment and promotion based on ethnic or party loyalty |
| **Ethnic and regional rivalry** | Divisions within both the army and politics |
| **Personal ambition** of officers | Desire for power and wealth |
| **Insecurity and war** | The civil war gave soldiers political weight |
| **Contagion** | Successful coups in other West African states |

## Features of military rule

1. **Suspension of the constitution**, wholly or in part.
2. **Dissolution of Parliament** and elected councils.
3. **Rule by decree**, issued by a supreme military council.
4. **Ban on political parties** and political activity.
5. **Fusion of executive and legislative powers** in the ruling council.
6. **Restriction of fundamental rights**: detention without trial, curfews, press censorship.
7. **Special military tribunals.**
8. **Promises of a return to civilian rule** after a transition period.

## The three military regimes

| Regime | Period | Leader | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **National Reformation Council (NRC)** | March 1967 – April 1968 | Lt-Col **Andrew Juxon-Smith** | Formed after Brigadier Lansana's intervention following the disputed 1967 election; promised to clean up public life; overthrown by the **Sergeants' revolt** (Anti-Corruption Revolutionary Movement), which restored civilian rule under Siaka Stevens |
| **National Provisional Ruling Council (NPRC)** | April 1992 – March 1996 | Captain **Valentine Strasser**; from January 1996 Brigadier **Julius Maada Bio** | Young officers from the war front overthrew President Momoh on **29 April 1992**; struggled to end the war; held consultative **Bintumani conferences** in 1995–1996; Bio handed power to the elected President **Kabbah** in **1996** |
| **Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC)** | May 1997 – February 1998 | Major **Johnny Paul Koroma** | Overthrew the elected Kabbah government on **25 May 1997**; invited the **RUF** rebels into government; widespread abuses; isolated by ECOWAS and the UN; removed by **ECOMOG** in **February 1998** |

## Merits and demerits of military rule

| Claimed merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| Can end a political crisis quickly | **Unconstitutional** and illegitimate |
| May reduce some corruption at first | **Abuse of human rights** and dictatorship |
| Quick decisions without political debate | **No accountability** to the people |
| Some regimes hand over power, as in 1968 and 1996 | Soldiers lack experience in governing |
| | **Corruption** often returns or grows |
| | Damages the **discipline** and professionalism of the army |
| | **Coups breed counter-coups** |
| | **Economic sanctions** and international isolation, as under the AFRC |

:::callout{type=local-example}
When **Valentine Strasser** seized power in **1992**, he was only about **25 years old**, among the youngest heads of state in the world. The NPRC was popular at first with young people tired of APC rule. But the war worsened, and in **1996** his deputy, **Julius Maada Bio**, removed him in a palace coup and allowed elections to go ahead. Bio handed over to President **Ahmad Tejan Kabbah** in **March 1996**. Twenty-two years later, Bio was himself elected President.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The **AFRC** and the **NPRC** are different regimes. The **NPRC (1992–1996)** overthrew the APC government of Momoh and later handed over to an elected government. The **AFRC (1997–1998)** overthrew the elected Kabbah government and ruled together with the **RUF** rebels.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Coup d'état**: the sudden, illegal seizure of power, usually by the army.
- **National Provisional Ruling Council (NPRC)**: the military government of April 1992 to March 1996, formed by soldiers from the war front.
- **Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC)**: the junta that seized power in May 1997 and invited the RUF to share power.
- **Politicisation of the army**: recruitment and promotion in the army based on ethnic or party loyalty.
- **Contagion**: the spread of coups, as successful coups in other West African states encouraged soldiers to try their own.
:::

## Key points

- Causes: **disputed elections, corruption, economic hardship, poor army conditions, politicisation, ambition, war**.
- Features: **suspended constitution, dissolved Parliament, rule by decree, banned parties, restricted rights**.
- **NRC (1967–68)**: Juxon-Smith; ended by the **Sergeants' revolt**.
- **NPRC (1992–96)**: Strasser, then Bio; handed over to **Kabbah**.
- **AFRC (1997–98)**: Johnny Paul Koroma with the **RUF**; removed by **ECOMOG**.

## Quick check

1. Who led the National Reformation Council?
2. On what date did the NPRC overthrow President Momoh?
3. Which military regime invited the RUF into government?
4. State three features of military rule.

**Answers:**

1. Lt-Col Andrew Juxon-Smith.
2. 29 April 1992.
3. The Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC), under Johnny Paul Koroma.
4. Any three: suspension of the constitution; dissolution of Parliament; rule by decree; ban on political parties; fusion of executive and legislative powers; restriction of rights; military tribunals; promises of return to civilian rule.
