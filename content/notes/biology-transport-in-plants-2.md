# Transport in plants: transpiration, water movement and translocation

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- define transpiration and explain its importance
- describe the pathway of water through a leaf
- explain how environmental factors affect transpiration
- interpret a simple potometer experiment
- distinguish transpiration from translocation in phloem

## Transpiration

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata in leaves. Water evaporates from moist cell surfaces inside the leaf, moves into the intercellular air spaces and diffuses out through open stomata.

Most leaves have many stomata, each surrounded by two guard cells. When guard cells become turgid, the stomatal pore usually opens; when they lose turgor, the pore usually closes. The opening and closing of stomata regulate gas exchange and water loss. Carbon dioxide enters through stomata for photosynthesis, while oxygen and water vapour can leave.

## The pathway of water through a plant

Water is absorbed from the soil by root hairs, moves across the root tissues and enters the xylem. It travels upwards through xylem in the root and stem to the leaf veins. From the xylem, water moves into mesophyll cells, evaporates from their moist surfaces and diffuses through the leaf air spaces before leaving through stomata.

Evaporation from leaf surfaces lowers the water potential of the leaf cells and creates tension in the water column within the xylem. Because water molecules cohere to one another through hydrogen bonding, the column can be pulled upwards. Adhesion between water molecules and xylem walls also helps maintain the column. This explanation is often called the **cohesion-tension mechanism**.

The pull generated by transpiration is a major force driving water movement in tall plants. Root pressure may contribute under some conditions, but it is not sufficient to explain the upward movement of water in tall trees by itself.

## Factors affecting the rate of transpiration

| Factor | Effect on transpiration | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Usually increases the rate | Stomata commonly open in light for carbon dioxide uptake |
| Temperature | Usually increases the rate | Evaporation and diffusion occur faster at higher temperatures |
| Humidity | High humidity decreases the rate | The water-vapour gradient between leaf and air becomes smaller |
| Wind speed | Moderate wind usually increases the rate | Moving air removes humid air around the leaf |
| Soil water availability | Severe shortage reduces the rate | Stomata close as the plant conserves water |

These are general trends. A plant may close its stomata in intense heat or water shortage, reducing transpiration even when evaporation could otherwise be rapid.

## Investigating water uptake with a potometer

A potometer estimates the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot. A typical apparatus contains a cut shoot connected to a water-filled capillary tube. An air bubble is introduced into the capillary, and the distance it moves in a measured time is recorded. If the capillary has a uniform cross-sectional area, the volume of water taken up can be estimated from the distance moved multiplied by that area.

A fair test changes one environmental factor at a time while keeping other conditions as constant as possible. For example, to investigate wind speed, keep light intensity, temperature, humidity, leaf area and the time interval constant.

A potometer does **not** measure transpiration directly. Some absorbed water is used in photosynthesis, growth and maintaining cell turgidity. The apparatus measures water uptake, which is used as an estimate of transpiration rate.

For reliability, repeat readings and calculate a mean. Check the apparatus for leaks and ensure the shoot is cut and fitted under water to reduce air entering the xylem.

## Translocation in the phloem

Translocation is the movement of dissolved organic substances through the phloem. The main transported carbohydrate is sucrose, although amino acids and other substances can also be moved. Unlike xylem transport, phloem transport can occur from a source to a sink in either direction in different sieve tubes.

A **source** is a region where sugars are loaded into the phloem, such as a mature photosynthesising leaf. A **sink** is a region where sugars are used or stored, such as a growing root, developing fruit, seed or storage organ.

Phloem contains sieve tube elements joined end to end, with sieve plates between them. Mature sieve tube elements have reduced cell contents and depend on companion cells for metabolic support. Companion cells contain many mitochondria and help load and unload sucrose.

In the pressure-flow explanation, sucrose is loaded into phloem at a source. This lowers the water potential, so water enters from nearby xylem by osmosis. The resulting pressure helps move phloem sap towards a sink. At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used or stored; water may move back towards the xylem.

## Xylem and phloem compared

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Main materials | Water and mineral ions | Sucrose and other organic solutes |
| Main direction | Mainly upwards from roots | Source to sink; may be in either direction in the plant |
| Conducting cells | Mostly dead vessel elements and tracheids | Living sieve tube elements supported by companion cells |
| Other role | Mechanical support | Distribution of manufactured food |

## WASSCE examination focus

Do not confuse transpiration with translocation. Transpiration is water-vapour loss, mainly through stomata. Translocation is the movement of dissolved organic substances in phloem.

When explaining transpiration, give the complete pathway from soil water to root hair, xylem, leaf cells, air spaces and stomata. In a potometer question, state that it measures water uptake, not water loss directly.
