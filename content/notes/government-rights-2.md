A child born in Makeni to Sierra Leonean parents is a citizen from birth. A foreign trader who has lived in Freetown for many years may apply to become one. A Sierra Leonean living in London may hold a British passport as well. **Citizenship** decides who fully belongs to a state, with the rights and the duties that come with it. This lesson explains how citizenship is acquired and lost, and what it means for the citizen and the state.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define citizenship and distinguish a citizen from an alien
- explain the methods of acquiring citizenship
- explain how citizenship can be lost
- state the rights, duties and obligations of citizens
- explain the obligations of the state to its citizens

## Who is a citizen?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **citizen** is a full, legal member of a state who enjoys all the rights the state grants and owes it loyalty and duties. **Citizenship** is the status of being a citizen.
:::

- An **alien** is a person living in a state who is **not** a citizen of it. Aliens enjoy many rights, but not full political rights such as voting.
- A **stateless person** is not recognised as a citizen by any state.
- **Dual citizenship** means being a citizen of two states at once.

## Acquiring citizenship

![Five routes to citizenship (birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and honorary citizenship) leading to the citizen, and two ways it is lost: renunciation and deprivation](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/citizenship-modes.svg)

*Figure 1. How citizenship is acquired and lost. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Method | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **By birth** | Born in the state, usually with at least one parent or grandparent who is a citizen |
| **By descent** | Born abroad to a parent who is a citizen |
| **By registration** | Granted on application to people with a close link, for example a foreign woman or man married to a citizen |
| **By naturalisation** | Granted to an alien after a period of lawful residence, good character, knowledge of the country and an oath of allegiance |
| **Honorary citizenship** | Granted as an honour to a distinguished foreigner |

Two principles underlie citizenship by birth:

- ***Jus soli*** ("right of the soil"): citizenship from being **born in the territory** (used widely in the USA).
- ***Jus sanguinis*** ("right of blood"): citizenship from the **parents' citizenship**, wherever the child is born. Sierra Leone's law is based mainly on this principle.

## Losing citizenship

1. **Renunciation:** a citizen voluntarily gives up citizenship, usually to take another.
2. **Deprivation:** the state takes away citizenship, usually from **naturalised or registered** citizens, for example for **fraud** in obtaining it, **disloyalty**, or certain serious convictions.

## Rights of citizens

- **Political rights:** to vote, to stand for election and to hold public office
- **Civil rights:** life, liberty, fair hearing, freedom of speech, religion, association and movement
- The right to a **passport** and to enter and live in the country freely
- **Protection** by the state at home and abroad
- **Social and economic opportunities**: education, health care and employment in public service

## Duties and obligations of citizens

| Duties (legally required) | Obligations (moral or civic) |
|---|---|
| Obey the laws | Vote in elections |
| Pay taxes | Take part in community development |
| Defend the state when called upon | Respect the rights of others |
| Serve on a jury when summoned | Report crimes to the police |
| Respect national symbols: the flag and the anthem | Protect public property |
| Be loyal to the state | Be tolerant and keep the peace |

## Obligations of the state to citizens

- Protect lives and property
- Guarantee fundamental human rights
- Provide social services: education, health, water and roads
- Hold free and fair elections
- Ensure justice through independent courts
- Promote economic welfare and employment

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's citizenship law is the **Sierra Leone Citizenship Act 1973**, as amended. A **2006** amendment allowed **dual citizenship**, so Sierra Leoneans abroad can keep their citizenship when they take another. The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2017** gave **mothers** the same right as fathers to pass citizenship to children born **outside** Sierra Leone. In recent years the government has also granted citizenship to members of the African diaspora who traced their ancestry to Sierra Leone.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Distinguishing registration from naturalisation.** Both require an application, but:

- **Registration** is for people who already have a **special link** to the state, such as marriage to a citizen; the conditions are lighter.
- **Naturalisation** is for **aliens with no special link**; they must show **long lawful residence**, good character and commitment, and take an oath of allegiance.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **citizen** and a **resident** are different. Many foreign residents live and work in Sierra Leone for years, but unless they acquire citizenship they remain **aliens**: they cannot vote or stand for election. Also, a duty is **legally enforceable** (such as paying taxes), whereas an obligation like voting is a **civic responsibility**.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Alien**: a person living in a state who is not a citizen of it.
- **Naturalisation**: citizenship granted to an alien after a period of lawful residence, good character, knowledge of the country and an oath of allegiance.
- **Jus soli**: the principle that citizenship is gained by being born in the state.
- **Jus sanguinis**: the principle that citizenship is gained by descent, from a parent who is a citizen.
- **Dual citizenship**: being a citizen of two states at once; allowed in Sierra Leone since 2006.
- **Renunciation**: a citizen voluntarily giving up citizenship, usually to take another.
:::

## Key points

- A **citizen** is a full legal member of a state; an **alien** is not.
- Citizenship is acquired by **birth, descent, registration, naturalisation** or as an **honour**; based on ***jus soli*** or ***jus sanguinis***.
- It is lost by **renunciation** or **deprivation**.
- Citizens have **rights** (vote, hold office, protection) and **duties** (obey laws, pay taxes, defend the state).
- Sierra Leone: **Citizenship Act 1973**; **dual citizenship from 2006**; **equal rights for mothers (2017)**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define citizenship**, to **explain the methods of acquiring it**, to **state how it can be lost**, and to **list the rights and duties of citizens**. Questions often ask you to distinguish naturalisation and registration, or a citizen and an alien.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is naturalisation?
2. State two ways citizenship can be lost.
3. What is *jus sanguinis*?
4. Give two duties of a citizen.

**Answers:**

1. The process by which an alien becomes a citizen after a period of lawful residence, good conduct and taking an oath of allegiance.
2. Renunciation (giving it up voluntarily) and deprivation (the state taking it away, for example for fraud or disloyalty).
3. The principle that a person's citizenship is determined by the citizenship of their parents, wherever they are born.
4. Any two: obeying the laws; paying taxes; defending the state; serving on a jury; respecting national symbols; being loyal to the state.
