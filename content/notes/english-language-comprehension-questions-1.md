Every comprehension question belongs to a type, and each type has its own way of earning the mark. Once you can recognise the type from the wording, you know what the examiner wants before you even look at the passage. This lesson covers the three types that carry most of the 20 marks: **literal**, **inferential** and **word-replacement** questions, with worked examples, the errors that lose marks, and plenty of practice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- recognise a literal question and give exactly the information it asks for
- recognise an inferential question and support your answer with evidence
- choose a replacement word that fits both the meaning and the grammar of the sentence
- test every answer before moving on

## Literal questions: the answer is on the page

:::callout{type=definition}
A **literal question** asks for information the passage **states directly**. The answer is in the words of the text. Your job is to **find it**, **take only what is asked**, and **write it clearly**.
:::

Literal questions usually begin with *What, Where, When, Who, How many, Name, State, List*.

### The three steps

1. **Locate**: find the sentence that holds the answer (you marked it in the margin on your second reading).
2. **Select**: take only the part that answers the question.
3. **Write**: give it as a short complete sentence, adjusting pronouns and tense so it reads naturally.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"Fatmata sold her groundnuts at Lumley before the first rains came, because the price always fell once the roads to the farms opened again and every farmer in the district brought sacks to the market."*

| Question | Weak answer | Why it is weak | Strong answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| When did Fatmata sell her groundnuts? | Because the price always fell. | answers *why*, not *when* | She sold them **before the first rains**. |
| Where did she sell them? | At the market where the farmers brought sacks. | vague, and the wrong market | She sold them **at Lumley**. |
| Why did the price fall when the roads opened? | The roads opened. | repeats the condition, not the reason | Because **every farmer** could then bring groundnuts to market, so there was **too much supply**. |
:::

### Pronouns and point of view

If the passage is written in the first person (*I, we*), change it in your answer:

- Passage: *"I left the village when I was twelve."*
- Question: *When did the writer leave the village?*
- Answer: **The writer left the village at the age of twelve.** (not *"I left…"*)

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Writing the answer in the passage's exact words when they don't fit the question.** *"Why did the boy run?" "Because of the dog that had been barking all morning chased him."* Joining the passage's words to *because* has broken the grammar. Write a sentence that makes sense: **Because a dog chased him.**
:::

## Inferential questions: reading between the lines

:::callout{type=definition}
An **inferential question** asks you to work out what the passage **suggests but does not say**: a feeling, a reason, a likely result, a person's character, the writer's attitude.
:::

The wording gives it away: *What can you infer…? What does this suggest about…? Why do you think…? What impression do you get of…? How did … feel?*

### The two-part answer: claim and evidence

A strong inferential answer has two parts:

1. **The inference** (what the passage implies), in your own words.
2. **The evidence** (the word, action or detail that shows it).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"When the results were read out, Abu folded the paper into a small square, pushed it deep into his pocket, and walked home the long way, past the football field where nobody would see him."*

**Question:** How did Abu feel about his results? Give a reason for your answer.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Inference** | He was disappointed and ashamed of his results. |
| **Evidence** | He hid the paper deep in his pocket and chose a route where nobody would see him. |

**Answer:** Abu was disappointed and ashamed of his results, because he hid the paper and took a road where nobody would see him.

The passage never uses the words *"disappointed"* or *"ashamed"*. You worked them out from his actions, and you showed the examiner how.
:::

### What makes an inference safe

| Safe inference | Unsafe inference |
|---|---|
| rests on details in the passage | rests on what *usually* happens |
| is the most likely meaning | is a possible but far-fetched meaning |
| would be agreed by most careful readers | depends on your own feelings |

*"Abu was afraid his father would punish him"* is unsafe: the passage never mentions his father.

:::callout{type=tip}
If you can point to the **exact words** that made you think it, your inference is probably safe. If you can't, think again.
:::

## Word-replacement questions

The question gives you several words from the passage:

> *For each of the following words, find another word or phrase which means the same and which can replace it as it is used in the passage.*

Each correct replacement usually earns one mark. Two tests decide whether yours is right.

### Test 1: meaning in context

Many words have several meanings. The right replacement is the meaning **in this sentence**.

| Word | Sentence | Wrong (another meaning) | Right |
|---|---|---|---|
| *fair* | *"The judge was **fair** to both sides."* | light-skinned; pretty | **just**, **impartial** |
| *settled* | *"They **settled** in Makeni after the war."* | paid (a bill) | **made their home**, **lived** |
| *cast* | *"She **cast** a worried look at the sky."* | threw (a net) | **gave**, **directed** |

### Test 2: the same grammar

The replacement must fit **exactly where the word was**: the same part of speech, the same tense, the same number.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"The rains had **abated** by evening, and the traders **hastily** packed their goods into the **sturdy** baskets."*

| Word | Wrong | Why it fails | Right |
|---|---|---|---|
| abated | decrease | wrong form: *"had decrease"* is ungrammatical | **lessened**, **subsided**, **eased** |
| hastily | quick | an adjective where an adverb is needed | **quickly**, **hurriedly** |
| sturdy | strength | a noun where an adjective is needed | **strong**, **solid**, **tough** |

**The substitution test:** read the sentence with your word in it. *"The rains had **subsided** by evening, and the traders **hurriedly** packed their goods into the **strong** baskets."* It reads correctly, so every answer is right.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Giving two or three alternatives:** *"lessened / reduced / stopped"*. If any of them is wrong (*stopped* is too strong), you may lose the mark. Give **one** word you have tested.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Take *"The Paramount Chief **dispatched** a messenger to the next chiefdom."* A good replacement is **sent**. *"Dispatch"* may make you think of a delivery company in Freetown, but here it is a **past-tense verb**, so the replacement must be a past-tense verb too: **sent**, not *"send"* or *"delivery"*.
:::

## Practice

Read this short passage, then try each question before you open the answer.

::::model{label="Practice passage" title="Market day"}
Every Saturday, Mariama's grandmother rose before the cockerels and walked the six miles to Daru market with a basin of palm oil balanced on her head. She never hurried and never stopped. Younger women, laden with bananas and cassava, overtook her on the road, laughing; by the time the sun was hot, she had passed most of them again, sitting exhausted in the shade. She would sell her oil before noon, buy salt, matches and a little dried fish, and be home before the evening meal, *"so that no one has to wait for me,"* she said, *"and no one has to worry."*
::::

:::reveal{title="1. How far did the grandmother walk to market? (literal)"}
**She walked six miles.** Just the fact asked for.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. What does the passage suggest about the grandmother's character? Give a reason. (inferential)"}
She was **steady, patient and determined**: she *"never hurried and never stopped"*, and she overtook the younger women who rushed and then tired. She was also **considerate**: she came home early so that no one would have to wait or worry.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give a word that can replace 'laden' as used in the passage. (vocabulary)"}
**loaded** (or *weighed down*). Test it: *"Younger women, **loaded** with bananas and cassava…"* It fits.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Why is 'tired' a wrong replacement for 'exhausted' here, and what is right?"}
*"Tired"* is close, but too weak: the women were completely worn out. **Worn out** or **drained** keeps the strength of the original.
:::

## Key points

- **Literal**: locate, select, write; give only what the question asks, in a clear sentence.
- **Inferential**: give the **inference** and the **evidence** from the passage.
- **Word replacement**: same **meaning in context**, same **grammar**; test it in the sentence; give **one** answer.
- Change *I* and *we* to *the writer* when the passage is in the first person.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
These three types make up most of the comprehension marks. Examiners reward the **exact point** for literal questions, a **supported** inference, and a replacement that **fits the sentence perfectly**. They penalise answers that are vague, too long, unsupported, or grammatically unable to take the word's place.
:::

## Quick check

:::reveal{title="Is 'What does the writer's silence suggest about her mood?' literal or inferential?"}
**Inferential**: it asks what her silence *suggests*.
:::

:::reveal{title="Replace 'reluctantly' in: 'He reluctantly agreed to lend his bicycle.'"}
**unwillingly** (an adverb, like *reluctantly*).
:::

:::reveal{title="Why is 'angry' wrong for 'fumed' in 'The driver fumed at the traffic'?"}
*"Angry"* is an adjective; *"fumed"* is a past-tense verb. The replacement must be a verb in the past tense: **raged**, **seethed**.
:::
