Every comprehension question has a type, and each type has its own way of earning the mark. Once you can recognise the type from the wording, you know what kind of answer the examiner wants. This lesson covers the three types that make up most of the marks: literal questions, inferential questions and word-replacement questions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- tell literal questions from inferential questions
- answer a literal question with the exact point, in a complete sentence
- support an inference with evidence from the passage
- choose a replacement word that fits the context and the grammar

## Literal questions: the answer is on the page

:::callout{type=definition}
A **literal question** asks for information the passage **states directly**: who, what, where, when, how many. The answer is in the text; your job is to find it and give it clearly.
:::

Literal questions usually begin *What…*, *Where…*, *When…*, *Who…*, *Name…*, *State…*.

How to answer:

1. Find the sentence that holds the answer.
2. Take only the part that answers the question.
3. Write it as a short, complete sentence, changing pronouns where needed (*"I"* in the passage may become *"the writer"* in your answer).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"Fatmata sold her groundnuts at Lumley before the first rains came, because the price always fell once the roads to the farms opened again."*

**Question:** When did Fatmata sell her groundnuts?

**Weak answer:** Because the price always fell. *(This answers "why", not "when".)*

**Good answer:** She sold them before the first rains.
:::

## Inferential questions: reading between the lines

:::callout{type=definition}
An **inferential question** asks you to work out something the passage **suggests but does not say directly**: a feeling, a reason, an attitude, a likely result.
:::

The wording often gives it away: *What can you infer…*, *What does this suggest about…*, *Why do you think…*, *What impression…*, *How did the writer feel…*.

How to answer:

1. Decide what the passage **implies**.
2. State it clearly.
3. Where you can, add the **evidence**: the word or action in the passage that shows it.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"When the results were read out, Abu folded the paper into a small square, pushed it deep into his pocket, and walked home the long way, past the football field where nobody would see him."*

**Question:** How did Abu feel about his results? Give a reason for your answer.

**Answer:** He was disappointed or ashamed, because he hid the paper and took a route where nobody would see him.

The passage never says *"disappointed"*. You inferred it from his actions, and you showed the evidence.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Inventing evidence.** An inference must be supported by the passage. *"Abu was disappointed because his father would beat him"* earns nothing if the passage never mentions his father.
:::

## Word-replacement questions

This question gives a list of words from the passage and asks you to find **another word or phrase that means the same and can replace it as used in the passage**. Usually each correct word earns a mark.

Two tests decide whether your word is right:

1. **Meaning in context.** Many words have several meanings. Use the one the passage intends.
2. **Same grammar.** The replacement must fit the sentence: same part of speech, same tense, same number.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"The rains had **abated** by evening, and the traders **hastily** packed their goods."*

| Word | Wrong | Why | Right |
|---|---|---|---|
| abated | decrease | wrong tense: *"had decrease"* does not fit | **lessened**, **eased**, **subsided** |
| hastily | quick | wrong part of speech: an adjective where an adverb is needed | **quickly**, **hurriedly** |

Test each answer by putting it into the sentence: *"The rains had **subsided** by evening, and the traders **hurriedly** packed their goods."* It reads correctly, so it is right.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Giving **two** replacements (*"lessened/subsided"*) is risky. If one is wrong, you may lose the mark. Give **one** word you are sure of.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Take *"The chief **dispatched** a messenger to the next village."* Good replacements are **sent** or **sent off**. *"Dispatch"* in a Freetown office might mean a delivery service, but here it is a verb in the past tense, so the replacement must be a past-tense verb too.
:::

## Key points

- **Literal** questions: the answer is stated; find it and give only what is asked.
- **Inferential** questions: say what the passage implies, and support it with evidence from the passage.
- **Word replacement**: the new word must have the **same meaning in context** and the **same grammar**; test it in the sentence.
- Give one clear answer, not several alternatives.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Most of the 20 comprehension marks come from these three types. Examiners reward **precise** answers: the exact point for a literal question, a **supported** inference, and a replacement that **fits the sentence**. They penalise answers that are vague, too long, or grammatically unable to replace the word.
:::

## Quick check

1. Is *"What does the writer's silence suggest about her mood?"* literal or inferential?
2. Replace *"reluctantly"* in: *"He reluctantly agreed to lend his bicycle."*
3. Why is *"angry"* a wrong replacement for *"fumed"* in *"The driver fumed at the traffic"*?
4. A passage says *"The hall was half empty when the speaker arrived."* Is *"Few people had come"* a literal answer or an inference?

**Answers:** 1. Inferential. 2. **unwillingly**. 3. *"Angry"* is an adjective; *"fumed"* is a past-tense verb, so the replacement must be a verb such as **raged**. 4. An inference, but a safe one: a half-empty hall suggests few people had come.
