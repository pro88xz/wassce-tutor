Two farmers plant the same rice variety on the same day, one in an inland valley swamp near Kenema and one on a dry, stony upland near Kabala. One harvests three times as much as the other. Rain, soil, pests, money, roads, markets and even government policy all decide how well a farm does. These are the **factors affecting agricultural production**, and understanding them is the first step to improving farming.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the factors affecting agricultural production
- explain the climatic, soil (edaphic), biotic and topographic factors
- explain the economic, social and political factors
- suggest ways of overcoming the main limiting factors in Sierra Leone

## What are the factors affecting agriculture?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Factors affecting agricultural production** are the natural and human conditions that determine **what** can be grown or reared in an area, **how much** is produced and **how profitably**.
:::

They are usually grouped into **environmental** (natural) factors and **socio-economic** (human) factors.

## Environmental factors

### 1. Climatic factors

:::callout{type=definition}
**Climatic factors** are the conditions of the weather over a long period, such as rainfall, temperature, sunlight, humidity and wind, that affect crops and animals.
:::

| Factor | How it affects agriculture |
|---|---|
| **Rainfall** | Amount and timing decide which crops grow and when to plant; too little causes drought, too much causes flooding and leaching |
| **Temperature** | Controls germination, growth and ripening; very high temperatures stress crops and animals |
| **Sunlight** | Needed for photosynthesis; day length affects flowering in some crops |
| **Relative humidity** | High humidity encourages fungal diseases; low humidity increases water loss |
| **Wind** | Helps pollination and drying, but strong winds lodge crops and spread pests and fire; the dry Harmattan stresses crops |

### 2. Edaphic (soil) factors

:::callout{type=definition}
**Edaphic factors** are the properties of the soil, such as its texture, structure, fertility, pH, depth and drainage, that affect crop growth.
:::

Sandy soils drain fast but hold few nutrients; clay soils hold water and nutrients but can waterlog; loams are best for most crops. **Soil pH** affects which nutrients are available, and many tropical soils are acidic and leached.

### 3. Biotic factors

:::callout{type=definition}
**Biotic factors** are the living things that affect agricultural production, such as pests, disease organisms, weeds, predators and useful organisms like earthworms and pollinators.
:::

Pests and diseases (rice blast, cassava mosaic disease, stem borers, rodents, birds) reduce yields; weeds compete for light, water and nutrients; earthworms, bees and nitrogen-fixing bacteria help.

### 4. Topographic factors

The **relief** of the land (slope, altitude and aspect) affects farming. Steep slopes erode easily and are hard to cultivate or mechanise; valley bottoms collect water and suit swamp rice; high altitudes are cooler.

## Socio-economic factors

1. **Capital**: money to buy seed, fertiliser, tools and machines; most smallholders lack it.
2. **Labour**: enough workers at planting and harvest; many young people have left farming for towns and mines.
3. **Land tenure**: secure rights encourage investment; insecure rights discourage it.
4. **Transport and roads**: poor feeder roads make it costly to bring in inputs and take produce to market, and crops rot.
5. **Markets and prices**: low or unstable prices discourage production; good markets encourage it.
6. **Storage and processing**: lack of stores and processing leads to heavy post-harvest losses.
7. **Education and extension**: farmers who are trained adopt improved varieties and methods.
8. **Credit**: access to loans lets farmers expand.
9. **Government policy**: subsidies, input supply, research, guaranteed prices and political stability all affect farming.
10. **Social and cultural factors**: religion, taboos and customs (for example, which animals may be kept or eaten) and the role of women in farming.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not list "farmers are lazy" as a factor. Low output is usually explained by **real constraints**, such as lack of capital, poor roads, pests, poor soil or insecure land, and good answers name them precisely.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
In Sierra Leone, farming is limited mainly by **lack of capital and credit**, **poor feeder roads**, **heavy post-harvest losses**, **pests and diseases**, **acidic, leached soils** on the uplands, and the **ageing farming population** as young people move to towns and mining areas. Inland valley swamps, which hold water through the dry season, offer great potential for rice when they are developed with water control.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Explaining a limiting factor and its solution.** "Poor feeder roads limit agricultural production."

- Effect: farmers cannot get fertiliser and improved seed to their farms cheaply, and cannot get produce such as tomatoes and pineapples to market before they spoil; prices at the farm gate are low.
- Solution: build and maintain feeder roads; set up collection centres and storage near farms; encourage farmers to process perishable produce.
:::

## Key points

- **Environmental factors**: **climatic** (rainfall, temperature, sunlight, humidity, wind), **edaphic** (soil), **biotic** (pests, diseases, weeds, useful organisms) and **topographic** (slope, altitude).
- **Socio-economic factors**: capital, labour, land tenure, transport, markets, storage, education and extension, credit, government policy, and social and cultural factors.
- In Sierra Leone the main limits are capital, roads, post-harvest losses, pests, poor upland soils and labour.

## Quick check

1. What are edaphic factors?
2. Give two biotic factors that reduce crop yields.
3. How do poor roads affect agriculture?
4. Why do valley swamps suit rice?

**Answers:** 1. Properties of the soil, such as texture, fertility, pH and drainage, that affect crop growth. 2. Any two of pests, diseases, weeds, predators. 3. Inputs are costly to bring in and produce cannot reach markets before it spoils, so farm-gate prices are low. 4. They collect and hold water, which rice needs, even into the dry season.
