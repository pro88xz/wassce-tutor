The **effects of early European contact on West Africa** were both positive and negative. Contact brought **new crops** from the Americas (such as **maize, cassava and groundnuts**), new trade goods and **firearms**, the first **Christian missions** and **Western education**, and turned the **coast** into a busy trading zone. But it also **drew trade away** from the Sahara, made coastal peoples **middlemen**, fuelled **wars**, and led to the **Atlantic slave trade**, which caused enormous suffering. This lesson weighs the effects of European contact before the colonial period.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the goods traded between Europeans and West Africans
- explain the positive effects of early European contact
- explain the negative effects of early European contact
- explain the shift of trade from the Sahara to the coast
- describe the role of African middlemen

## The trade

| West African exports | European imports |
|---|---|
| gold, ivory, pepper (malaguetta), gum, hides, timber, palm products, and later **slaves** | cloth, iron bars, copper and brass, beads, alcohol, mirrors, **guns and gunpowder**, tobacco |

Trade was carried on through **African rulers and middlemen**, who controlled access to the interior and charged **rents and customs duties** on European traders.

## Positive effects

1. **New crops** from the Americas: **maize, cassava, groundnuts, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, pineapples**, which became staple foods and helped to support growing populations.
2. **Growth of coastal trade and towns**, and new wealth for coastal rulers and merchants.
3. **New goods** such as cloth, metal tools and utensils.
4. **Christian missions and Western education**, which grew especially in the nineteenth century.
5. **Literacy** in European languages, useful in trade and diplomacy.
6. **Contact with the wider world** and knowledge of other cultures.

## Negative effects

1. **The Atlantic slave trade**, which took millions of people from West Africa (see the next topic).
2. **Wars and insecurity**, as states raided neighbours for captives to trade for guns.
3. **Firearms** made warfare more destructive and helped some states expand at the expense of others.
4. **Decline of local crafts**, such as cloth weaving and iron smelting, in some areas, as imported goods replaced them.
5. **Decline of the trans-Saharan trade** and of savanna cities, as trade shifted to the coast.
6. **Foundations of colonialism**: forts, treaties and trading interests later became claims to territory.

## The shift of trade to the coast

Before the fifteenth century, West Africa's main link to the outside world was the **Sahara**. After European ships arrived:

- **gold** and other goods began to flow **south to the coast**, where Europeans paid good prices;
- **coastal states** (Asante, Dahomey, the Niger Delta city-states) grew stronger;
- the **savanna empires** that depended on trans-Saharan trade lost revenue, which contributed to the decline of **Songhai** and the importance of Timbuktu.

:::callout{type=local-example}
On the Sierra Leone coast, Portuguese traders, and later English and other traders, exchanged cloth, iron, beads and alcohol for **ivory, beeswax, camwood (a red dye) and slaves**. Some Portuguese settled and married into local families; their descendants, sometimes called **lançados** or "Luso-Africans", became important middlemen in the trade along the Upper Guinea coast.
:::

## African middlemen

Coastal peoples became **middlemen** between European traders and the producers inland. They:

- **controlled access** to the interior, so Europeans could not trade directly with inland markets;
- **charged duties** and set prices;
- grew **rich and powerful**, sometimes founding trading towns and new states.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Early European traders did **not** rule West Africa. Until the late nineteenth century they mostly stayed on the **coast**, renting land for their forts from African rulers, who kept political control of the interior.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Middlemen**: African traders on the coast who controlled trade between the European forts and the peoples of the interior.
- **Trading post**: a fort or station on the coast where Europeans stored goods and traded with Africans.
:::

## Key points

- Trade: gold, ivory, pepper, later **slaves**, for **cloth, metals, beads, alcohol, guns**.
- Positive effects: **new crops**, coastal **trade and wealth**, new goods, **missions and education**, wider contact.
- Negative effects: **slave trade, wars, firearms, decline of crafts and the Sahara trade**, foundations of **colonialism**.
- Trade **shifted from the Sahara to the coast**; **African middlemen** controlled access to the interior.

## Quick check

1. Name three crops introduced to West Africa from the Americas.
2. Give two European goods imported into West Africa.
3. Why did trade shift from the Sahara to the coast?
4. What role did African middlemen play?
5. Give two negative effects of early European contact.

**Answers:**

1. Any three: maize; cassava; groundnuts; sweet potatoes; tomatoes; pineapples.
2. Any two: cloth; iron bars; copper and brass; beads; alcohol; guns and gunpowder; tobacco.
3. Because European ships traded directly on the coast and paid good prices, drawing goods south.
4. They controlled trade between European traders on the coast and producers inland, charging duties and setting prices.
5. Any two: the slave trade; wars and insecurity; destructive firearms; decline of local crafts; decline of the trans-Saharan trade; foundations of colonialism.
