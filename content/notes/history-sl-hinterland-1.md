**Relations between the Colony of Sierra Leone and its hinterland** in the nineteenth century were built on **trade**. Freetown depended on the interior for **food, timber, palm kernels, palm oil, kola nuts, groundnuts, hides and gold**, and sent back **cloth, salt, tobacco, guns, spirits and hardware**. **Krio traders and missionaries** moved inland, and the Colony government signed **treaties of friendship** with chiefs and paid them **stipends** to keep the trade routes open. But **wars between chiefdoms**, raids by **"war boys"**, and the arrival of **Samori's sofas** in the north-east often disrupted trade, leading the British to send **military expeditions** inland and, eventually, to take control. This lesson explains **the relations between the Colony and the hinterland before 1896**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why the Colony depended on the hinterland
- describe the trade between the Colony and the hinterland
- describe the role of Krio traders and missionaries in the interior
- explain the Colony government's policy towards the hinterland
- describe the conflicts that disrupted trade
- explain how these problems led towards British control of the interior

## Why the Colony needed the hinterland

1. The Colony was **small and mountainous**, with poor soils; it could not feed itself.
2. Its **revenue** came mainly from **customs duties** on trade with the interior.
3. Freetown's **merchants**, both European and Krio, lived by buying produce from the interior and selling imported goods.

## Krio traders and missionaries inland

1. **Krio traders** settled in market towns such as **Port Loko, Kambia, Bonthe** and along the rivers, buying produce and selling imported goods; many married local women.
2. **Missionaries** set up stations inland: the **CMS** in the north and among the Temne, the **American United Brethren** at **Shenge** and **Rotifunk**, and the **American Missionary Association's Mende Mission**, begun after the **Amistad** captives returned in 1842.
3. They spread **Christianity, Western education and the Krio language** in the interior.
4. Some chiefs welcomed them; others saw them as agents of the Colony.

## The Colony government's policy

For most of the century Britain did **not** want to rule the interior, because it would be **expensive**. Instead the Colony government:

1. signed **treaties of friendship** with chiefs, promising to keep roads open, protect traders and **end the slave trade**;
2. paid chiefs yearly **stipends** for keeping the treaties;
3. sent **messengers and envoys** to settle disputes, through an office for native affairs;
4. used **force** only when trade was badly threatened, through **expeditions** against chiefs who blocked the routes.

## Conflicts in the hinterland

| Conflict | Effect on trade |
|---|---|
| **Wars between chiefdoms** over land, trade and succession, often fought by hired **"war boys"** | closed roads, burned towns and markets |
| **Temne–Loko and Temne–Susu wars** in the north | disrupted the routes to Port Loko and the Scarcies |
| **Kpaa Mende raids** in the south | attacks on Sherbro trade |
| **The Yoni Temne** raids of the 1880s | led to the British **Yoni expedition of 1887** |
| **Samori Toure's sofas** in the north-east (1880s) | raided Koinadugu and the routes from the north; Samori also sought British friendship and bought guns in Freetown |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why trade wars drew Britain inland**

When a trade route was closed by war, **produce stopped reaching Freetown**, **customs revenue fell**, and **Krio and European merchants** pressed the Governor to act. Each time, the government sent envoys, then soldiers, and signed more treaties. Step by step, the Colony was drawn **deeper into the interior**, even though London did not want the cost of ruling it.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Falaba**, in the far north, was a great market town on the caravan route from Futa Jallon to Freetown. Its trade in **cattle, gold, hides and ivory** linked the Sierra Leone coast with the Western Sudan, and explains why both the British and Samori Toure were interested in the north-east in the 1880s.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Before **1896** most of present-day Sierra Leone was **not** British territory. Only the **Colony** (Freetown, the peninsula and some coastal areas) was ruled by Britain; the hinterland was made up of **independent chiefdoms**, many of them bound to the Colony only by **treaties of friendship**.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Hinterland**: the land lying inland, behind the coast and the Colony.
- **Stipend**: a yearly payment made by the Colony government to chiefs to keep trade routes open and the peace.
:::

## Key points

- The **Colony** depended on the **hinterland** for food, produce, trade and customs revenue.
- Trade: **timber, palm kernels, palm oil, kola, groundnuts, rice, hides and gold** for **cloth, salt, tobacco, guns, spirits and hardware**.
- **Krio traders** and **missionaries** (CMS, United Brethren, Mende Mission) spread inland.
- Policy: **treaties of friendship**, **stipends**, envoys and occasional **expeditions** (e.g. **Yoni 1887**).
- **Wars, raids** and **Samori's sofas** disrupted trade and drew Britain inland.
