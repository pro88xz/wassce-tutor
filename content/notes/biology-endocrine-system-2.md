## Hormones and their functions

Hormones regulate many processes, including growth, metabolism, response to danger, reproduction, salt-water balance and the concentration of nutrients in the blood. A useful examination method is to learn each hormone with its **source gland**, **target or process**, and **main effect**.

| Hormone | Source | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Growth hormone (GH) | Anterior pituitary gland | Stimulates growth of bones and body tissues |
| Thyroxine | Thyroid gland | Regulates metabolic rate and supports normal growth and development |
| Adrenaline | Adrenal medulla | Prepares the body for rapid action in stressful or dangerous situations |
| Insulin | Beta cells of pancreatic islets | Lowers blood glucose concentration |
| Glucagon | Alpha cells of pancreatic islets | Raises blood glucose concentration |
| ADH (antidiuretic hormone) | Made in hypothalamus; released by posterior pituitary | Increases water reabsorption by the kidneys |
| Oestrogen | Mainly ovaries | Supports female secondary sexual characteristics and regulates the menstrual cycle |
| Progesterone | Mainly corpus luteum in the ovary; placenta during pregnancy | Helps prepare and maintain the uterus lining for pregnancy |
| Testosterone | Testes | Supports male secondary sexual characteristics and sperm production |
| Cortisol | Adrenal cortex | Helps regulate metabolism and the body's longer-term response to stress |
| Aldosterone | Adrenal cortex | Increases sodium reabsorption by the kidneys and influences water and potassium balance |

The adrenal gland has two regions with different functions. The **adrenal medulla** releases adrenaline and noradrenaline, while the **adrenal cortex** releases steroid hormones such as cortisol and aldosterone.

## Blood glucose regulation: insulin and glucagon

Glucose is an important respiratory substrate. The concentration of glucose in the blood must be regulated so that cells receive a suitable supply without the concentration becoming excessively high or low.

The **pancreas** contains clusters of endocrine cells called the **islets of Langerhans**. Two important cell types are:

- **Beta cells**, which secrete insulin when blood glucose rises.
- **Alpha cells**, which secrete glucagon when blood glucose falls.

### When blood glucose is too high

After a carbohydrate-containing meal, digestion and absorption cause blood glucose concentration to rise. The sequence is:

1. Beta cells in the pancreatic islets detect the rise in blood glucose.
2. The beta cells release insulin into the blood.
3. Insulin acts on target tissues, including muscle and fat cells, to increase glucose uptake.
4. Insulin promotes the conversion of glucose to glycogen in the liver and skeletal muscles. This process is called **glycogenesis**.
5. The liver and other tissues use or store glucose, so blood glucose concentration falls towards its normal range.

Insulin therefore lowers blood glucose. It does not convert all glucose directly into glycogen in every cell; its effects vary among target tissues.

### When blood glucose is too low

During fasting or between meals, blood glucose concentration may fall. The sequence is:

1. Alpha cells in the pancreatic islets detect the fall in blood glucose.
2. The alpha cells release glucagon.
3. Glucagon acts mainly on the liver.
4. The liver breaks down stored glycogen into glucose. This is called **glycogenolysis**.
5. The liver releases glucose into the blood, raising blood glucose concentration towards its normal range.

The liver can also make new glucose from non-carbohydrate substances through **gluconeogenesis**, particularly during prolonged fasting. At this level, remember that glucagon raises blood glucose mainly by acting on the liver.

### Insulin compared with glucagon

| Feature | Insulin | Glucagon |
|---|---|---|
| Secreted by | Pancreatic beta cells | Pancreatic alpha cells |
| Released when | Blood glucose is high | Blood glucose is low |
| Main target for glucose regulation | Liver, muscle and fat tissues | Mainly liver |
| Main effect | Promotes glucose uptake and storage; reduces blood glucose | Promotes glucose release by the liver; raises blood glucose |
| Relationship | Opposes the action of glucagon in blood glucose regulation | Opposes the action of insulin in blood glucose regulation |

## Negative feedback and homeostasis

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment within limits that allow cells to function properly. It does not mean that every condition remains perfectly constant. Body temperature, blood glucose and water balance fluctuate around regulated levels.

**Negative feedback** is a control mechanism in which a change away from a regulated level triggers responses that oppose the original change. It helps prevent internal conditions from moving too far from their normal range.

Blood glucose regulation is an example:

- If blood glucose rises, insulin secretion increases and blood glucose falls.
- As blood glucose returns towards its normal range, the stimulus for insulin secretion is reduced.
- If blood glucose falls, glucagon secretion increases and blood glucose rises.
- As blood glucose returns towards its normal range, the stimulus for glucagon secretion is reduced.

The response reduces the original disturbance. Negative feedback is not the same as negative or harmful feedback; it is a stabilising process.

## ADH and water balance

Water balance is important because cells need an appropriate water content for normal chemical reactions. The hypothalamus contains osmoreceptors that detect changes in the concentration of the blood. When the blood becomes too concentrated because the body has lost water, more **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)** is released from the posterior pituitary.

ADH acts on the kidney tubules, especially the collecting ducts, making them more permeable to water. More water is reabsorbed from the filtrate into the blood. The urine becomes smaller in volume and more concentrated.

When the blood is too dilute, less ADH is released. The collecting ducts reabsorb less water, so a larger volume of dilute urine is produced.

| Condition | ADH secretion | Kidney response | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dehydration; blood more concentrated | Increases | More water reabsorbed | Smaller volume, more concentrated |
| Excess water; blood more dilute | Decreases | Less water reabsorbed | Larger volume, more dilute |

ADH is made by nerve cells in the hypothalamus and transported to the posterior pituitary for storage and release. The posterior pituitary releases it; it does not manufacture it.

## Thyroxine, adrenaline and reproductive hormones

### Thyroxine

Thyroxine is produced by the thyroid gland. It helps regulate the rate of metabolism and is important for normal growth and development. The thyroid needs iodine to make thyroid hormones. Too little iodine in the diet can reduce thyroid hormone production and may cause enlargement of the thyroid gland, known as a goitre.

The pituitary hormone TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland. When thyroid hormone levels are sufficient, they reduce the release of TSH through negative feedback.

### Adrenaline

Adrenaline is released from the adrenal medulla during excitement, fear, stress or danger. It prepares the body for rapid action, often called the **fight-or-flight response**. Its effects include increasing heart rate, increasing blood flow to skeletal muscles, widening air passages and making more glucose available for respiration.

Adrenaline does not cause the body to create energy from nothing. It helps make stored fuels and oxygen available to tissues that need to respond quickly.

### Reproductive hormones

Oestrogen contributes to the development of female secondary sexual characteristics and helps regulate the menstrual cycle. Progesterone helps prepare and maintain the lining of the uterus, particularly in relation to pregnancy. Testosterone contributes to male secondary sexual characteristics and supports sperm production.

These hormones interact with hormones from the pituitary gland. Reproductive control is therefore a coordinated process rather than the action of one gland alone.

## Diabetes mellitus and diabetes insipidus

**Diabetes mellitus** is a disorder in which blood glucose remains abnormally high because insulin is absent, insufficient or not working effectively. In type 1 diabetes, the pancreatic beta cells are destroyed by an autoimmune process, so little or no insulin is produced. In type 2 diabetes, body tissues become resistant to insulin and insulin production may also become inadequate.

Possible signs include frequent urination, excessive thirst, tiredness and unexplained weight loss. Long-term poorly controlled diabetes can damage blood vessels, nerves, kidneys and eyes. Diagnosis and treatment require qualified healthcare professionals.

**Diabetes insipidus** is different. It results from inadequate ADH production or release, or from the kidneys failing to respond properly to ADH. The kidneys cannot conserve water effectively, so the person produces large volumes of dilute urine and may become very thirsty.

| Feature | Diabetes mellitus | Diabetes insipidus |
|---|---|---|
| Main problem | Insulin production or action | ADH production/release or kidney response to ADH |
| Main regulation affected | Blood glucose | Water balance |
| Typical urine finding | May contain glucose when blood glucose is high enough to exceed the kidney threshold | Large volume of dilute urine |
| Key hormone | Insulin | ADH |

Do not confuse the two conditions because both can cause frequent urination and thirst, but their causes and treatments are different.

## WASSCE examination focus

- Pair each hormone with the correct gland: insulin and glucagon—pancreas; thyroxine—thyroid; adrenaline—adrenal medulla; ADH—made in hypothalamus and released by posterior pituitary.
- State the direction of change: insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it.
- Use the correct terms: glycogenesis is formation of glycogen; glycogenolysis is breakdown of glycogen.
- For negative feedback, describe the stimulus, hormone released, target-organ response and how the response reverses the change.
- In water-balance questions, increased ADH means more water reabsorption and a smaller volume of concentrated urine.
- Diabetes mellitus concerns insulin and blood glucose; diabetes insipidus concerns ADH and water balance.

1. Which pancreatic cells release insulin, and when is it released?
2. State two ways insulin helps lower blood glucose.
3. What is the effect of glucagon on the liver?
4. Explain negative feedback using blood glucose regulation.
5. What happens to ADH secretion and urine output during dehydration?
6. Name the hormone that regulates metabolic rate and the gland that produces it.
7. Distinguish diabetes mellitus from diabetes insipidus.
8. State two effects of adrenaline during a fight-or-flight response.

**Answers**

1. Beta cells; insulin is released when blood glucose concentration rises.
2. It increases glucose uptake by certain tissues and promotes conversion of glucose to glycogen in the liver and muscles.
3. It stimulates the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose into the blood; it also supports glucose production during fasting.
4. A rise in blood glucose stimulates insulin release; insulin promotes uptake and storage of glucose, lowering the concentration. As the level returns towards normal, insulin secretion is reduced.
5. ADH secretion increases; the kidneys reabsorb more water, producing a smaller volume of concentrated urine.
6. Thyroxine, produced by the thyroid gland.
7. Diabetes mellitus is a disorder of insulin action and blood glucose regulation; diabetes insipidus is a disorder of ADH action and water balance.
8. It increases heart rate and makes more glucose available; it also widens air passages and increases blood flow to skeletal muscles.
