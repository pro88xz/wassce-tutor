The SLPP and APC are not the only parties Sierra Leone has known. From the Colony-based National Council of 1950 to the National Grand Coalition of 2017, smaller parties have challenged the big two, split from them or briefly held the balance of power. This lesson looks at these other parties, the development of the party system, and the problems parties face today.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the main smaller parties in Sierra Leone's history
- trace the development of the party system from 1950 to the present
- explain how parties are regulated in Sierra Leone
- explain the problems facing political parties in Sierra Leone

## Other parties in Sierra Leone's history

| Party | Formed | Leader | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **National Council of Sierra Leone (NCSL)** | 1950 | H. C. Bankole-Bright | Colony (Krio) party; opposed the 1951 constitution; declined after 1957 |
| **United Progressive Party (UPP)** | 1954 | Cyril Rogers-Wright | Colony-based; joined the United Front in 1960 |
| **People's National Party (PNP)** | 1958 | Albert Margai, with Siaka Stevens | Split from the SLPP; joined the United Front and merged back into the SLPP |
| **Sierra Leone Independence Movement (SLIM)** | 1950s | Edward Blyden III | Radical nationalist party; won no seats in 1957 |
| **United National People's Party (UNPP)** | 1990s | Dr John Karefa-Smart | Runner-up to Kabbah in the 1996 presidential run-off |
| **Revolutionary United Front Party (RUFP)** | 2002 | Former RUF | The rebel movement turned party after the war; won no seats in 2002 |
| **People's Movement for Democratic Change (PMDC)** | 2006 | Charles Margai | Split from the SLPP; backed the APC's Koroma in the 2007 run-off |
| **National Grand Coalition (NGC)** | 2017 | Dr Kandeh Yumkella | Won seats in Parliament in 2018 |
| **Coalition for Change (C4C)** | 2017 | Samuel Sam-Sumana (former Vice-President) | Won seats in Parliament in 2018, mainly in Kono |

## Development of the party system

| Period | Party system |
|---|---|
| **1951–1961** | Multi-party; the SLPP dominant |
| **1961–1978** | Multi-party, increasingly a two-party contest between the SLPP and APC |
| **1978–1991** | **One-party state**: only the APC |
| **1992–1996** | Parties suspended under the NPRC |
| **1996 onward** | Multi-party, with the **SLPP and APC dominant** and smaller parties occasionally holding seats |

In the **2023** elections, held under **district block proportional representation**, only the SLPP and the APC won seats in Parliament.

## Regulation of political parties

- The **1991 Constitution** requires parties to be registered by an independent commission.
- The **Political Parties Regulation Commission (PPRC)**, now governed by the **Political Parties Act 2022**, registers parties, monitors their conduct and finances, and mediates disputes between and within parties.
- Parties must have a **national character**: they may not be based on ethnicity, religion or region, and must have offices and members across the country.
- About **17** parties were registered in the 2020s, though only a few were active nationally.

## Problems of political parties in Sierra Leone

1. **Regional and ethnic voting**, which deepens divisions.
2. **Political violence** during campaigns.
3. **Weak internal democracy**: disputes over leadership and candidate selection, sometimes ending in court.
4. **Lack of funds** and dependence on wealthy individuals.
5. **Little difference in ideology** between the main parties.
6. **Defections and splits** before elections.
7. **Under-representation of women** and young people in party leadership.
8. **Winner-takes-all politics**, with jobs and contracts going to supporters of the ruling party.

:::callout{type=local-example}
In the **2007** presidential run-off, the **PMDC**, a breakaway from the SLPP led by **Charles Margai**, backed the APC's **Ernest Bai Koroma**, helping him defeat the SLPP's Solomon Berewa. The episode shows how a smaller party can hold the **balance of power** in a close election.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The **RUF** was a rebel movement during the war, but the **RUFP** that contested the **2002** elections was a registered political party. Turning armed movements into parties was part of the peace process, though the RUFP won no seats.
:::

## Key points

- Other parties: **NCSL (1950), UPP (1954), PNP (1958), SLIM, UNPP, RUFP (2002), PMDC (2006), NGC and C4C (2017)**.
- Party system: **multi-party (1951–78) → one-party (1978–91) → multi-party with SLPP and APC dominant (1996 onward)**.
- The **PPRC** registers and regulates parties under the **Political Parties Act 2022**; parties must have a **national character**.
- Problems: **regional voting, violence, weak internal democracy, poor funding, defections, few women and youth leaders**.

## Quick check

1. Which party did H. C. Bankole-Bright lead?
2. Who led the PMDC, and when was it formed?
3. What is the PPRC?
4. State three problems facing political parties in Sierra Leone.

**Answers:**

1. The National Council of Sierra Leone (NCSL).
2. Charles Margai, in 2006.
3. The Political Parties Regulation Commission, which registers and regulates political parties.
4. Any three: regional and ethnic voting; violence; weak internal democracy; lack of funds; little ideological difference; defections and splits; under-representation of women and youth; winner-takes-all politics.
