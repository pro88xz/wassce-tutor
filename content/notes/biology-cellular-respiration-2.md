# Cellular respiration: anaerobic respiration and energy use

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- define anaerobic respiration
- compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration
- state the products of anaerobic respiration in animals and yeast
- explain oxygen debt in exercise
- describe the importance of respiration in organisms and industry

## Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without using oxygen. It releases less energy per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration because glucose is not completely broken down.

In human muscle cells during vigorous activity, when oxygen supply cannot meet demand, glucose is converted to lactic acid (lactate). The simplified word equation is:

**glucose → lactic acid + energy**

In yeast and some microorganisms, anaerobic breakdown of glucose produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. This process is called alcoholic fermentation:

**glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy**

The energy yield is much lower than in aerobic respiration. The exact ATP yield depends on the pathway and organism; for school-level comparison, anaerobic respiration is described as releasing much less energy than aerobic respiration.

## Comparing aerobic and anaerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration requires oxygen and completely breaks down glucose to carbon dioxide and water, releasing much more energy. Anaerobic respiration does not require oxygen and produces lactic acid in human muscles or ethanol and carbon dioxide in yeast, releasing less energy.

Both processes begin with glycolysis in the cytoplasm and transfer energy to ATP. Both are important in living cells, but aerobic respiration is generally the more efficient way of extracting energy from glucose.

## Exercise and oxygen debt

During intense exercise, muscle cells may obtain some energy through anaerobic respiration. Lactate accumulates in the blood and tissues and is transported to the liver and other tissues, where it can be metabolised. After exercise, breathing and heart rates may remain elevated so the body can restore normal conditions. This recovery requirement is traditionally described in school biology as oxygen debt.

Oxygen after exercise supports aerobic processes that help remove or metabolise lactate, restore oxygen stores and return body systems towards resting conditions. Do not describe oxygen debt simply as a store of oxygen that has run out; it refers to the extra oxygen required during recovery after strenuous activity.

## Fermentation and its uses

Yeast fermentation is useful in food production. In bread making, carbon dioxide produced by yeast forms bubbles that make dough rise. Some of the ethanol evaporates during baking. In brewing, yeast produces ethanol, which is retained in the alcoholic drink. Fermentation must be controlled because temperature, sugar availability and oxygen conditions affect yeast activity.

Microorganisms are also used in other industrial processes, including production of organic acids and some food products. Industrial fermentation requires control of conditions to obtain the desired product and reduce contamination.

## Factors affecting respiration

Respiration is enzyme-controlled, so temperature affects its rate. A rise in temperature can increase reaction rate up to an optimum. Above the optimum, enzymes may denature and the rate falls. Availability of respiratory substrate and oxygen can also affect aerobic respiration. In living organisms, the rate is regulated according to energy demand.
