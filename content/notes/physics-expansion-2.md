Liquids expand more than solids, but measuring that expansion is tricky because the container expands too. And water breaks all the rules between 0 °C and 4 °C — a quirk that keeps fish alive under frozen lakes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish real (absolute) and apparent expansivity of a liquid
- use $\gamma_r = \gamma_a + \gamma_g$
- describe and explain the anomalous expansion of water and its consequences

## Expansion of liquids

Liquids expand much more than solids for the same temperature rise. Because a liquid must be held in a container, which also expands, we distinguish two expansivities.

:::callout{type=definition}
- The **real (absolute) expansivity** $\gamma_r$ of a liquid is its true increase in volume per unit volume per kelvin.
- The **apparent expansivity** $\gamma_a$ is the increase in volume **observed in the container** per unit volume per kelvin. It is less than the real expansivity because the container expands too.

$$\gamma_r = \gamma_a + \gamma_g$$

where $\gamma_g = 3\alpha$ is the cubic expansivity of the container material.
:::

**A demonstration:** when a flask full of coloured water with a narrow tube is plunged into hot water, the level in the tube **first falls slightly** (the glass is heated first and expands) and **then rises** well above its starting point (the water warms and expands more than the glass).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The apparent expansivity of a liquid in a glass vessel is $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ K⁻¹. The linear expansivity of the glass is $9 \times 10^{-6}$ K⁻¹. Find the real expansivity of the liquid.**

$$\gamma_g = 3\alpha = 3 \times 9 \times 10^{-6} = 2.7 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{K}^{-1}$$

$$\gamma_r = 1.8 \times 10^{-4} + 2.7 \times 10^{-5} = 2.07 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$$
:::

### Measuring apparent expansivity with a specific gravity bottle (or weight thermometer)

Fill the bottle with liquid at θ₁ and weigh it; heat it to θ₂ so some liquid overflows; weigh again.

$$\gamma_a = \dfrac{\text{mass expelled}}{\text{mass remaining} \times \Delta\theta}$$

where the masses are of the liquid that overflowed and the liquid left in the bottle, and $\Delta\theta$ is the temperature rise.

## The anomalous expansion of water

![Graph of the volume of a fixed mass of water against temperature from 0 to 10 degrees Celsius: the volume falls to a minimum at 4 degrees and rises again above it](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/water-anomalous.svg)

*Figure 1. Water has its smallest volume, and greatest density, at 4 °C. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Most liquids contract steadily as they cool. **Water** contracts as it cools down to **4 °C**, but from **4 °C to 0 °C it expands**. On freezing it expands further, by about 9%: **ice is less dense than water** and floats.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Anomalous expansion of water:** water **contracts** when heated from 0 °C to 4 °C and **expands** when heated above 4 °C. Water therefore has its **maximum density (1000 kg m⁻³) at 4 °C**.
:::

### Consequences

1. **Ponds and lakes freeze from the top down.** As the surface water cools below 4 °C it becomes less dense, stays at the top and freezes. The ice floats and insulates the water below, which stays at about 4 °C at the bottom. Fish and plants survive the winter.
2. **Water pipes burst** in very cold weather: the water expands as it freezes.
3. **Rocks are broken (weathered)**: water in cracks freezes and expands, splitting the rock.
4. **Bottles of water left in a freezer crack.**

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Water is **densest at 4 °C, not at 0 °C**. And it is the **expansion on cooling below 4 °C and on freezing**, not ordinary heating, that bursts pipes.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone never freezes, but the effect is visible every day: put a full, sealed plastic bottle of water in a freezer and it bulges or splits as the ice forms. Ice cubes float in a glass of drinking water because ice is less dense than water.
:::

## Expansion of gases (preview)

Gases expand far more than liquids — roughly $\tfrac{1}{273}$ of their volume at 0 °C for every kelvin at constant pressure — and all gases expand by the same amount. This leads to Charles's law and the Kelvin scale, covered in the gas laws.

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Real expansivity** (absolute expansivity): the true increase in volume per unit volume per kelvin of a liquid, allowing for the expansion of its container.
- **Apparent expansivity**: the increase in volume per unit volume per kelvin of a liquid as it appears in its container, without allowing for the container's expansion.
:::

## Key points

- Real expansivity of a liquid = apparent expansivity + cubic expansivity of the container ($\gamma_r = \gamma_a + 3\alpha$).
- Water contracts from 0 °C to 4 °C and expands above 4 °C; maximum density at 4 °C.
- Ice is less dense than water, so lakes freeze from the top and aquatic life survives.
