**Plate tectonics** is the theory that the Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into large slabs called **plates** that move slowly over the softer mantle beneath, a few centimetres a year. Where plates meet, at **plate boundaries**, they move **apart** (constructive), **together** (destructive) or **past each other** (conservative), and these movements cause most earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains. This lesson explains the theory, the evidence for it and the three types of boundary.

## From continental drift to plate tectonics

In **1912** the German scientist **Alfred Wegener** proposed **continental drift**: that the continents were once joined in a single supercontinent, **Pangaea**, which split up about 200 million years ago and drifted apart.

**Evidence for continental drift:**

1. **Fit of the coastlines:** the bulge of West Africa fits into the curve of South America's east coast like a jigsaw.
2. **Fossils:** the same fossils of land animals and plants (such as the reptile *Mesosaurus*) are found in South America and Africa, oceans apart.
3. **Rocks and mountains:** matching rock types and mountain ranges continue across oceans.
4. **Climate evidence:** traces of ancient glaciers in tropical Africa and India, and coal (formed in warm swamps) under Antarctic ice.

Wegener could not explain **how** continents moved. In the 1960s, studies of the ocean floor showed **sea-floor spreading** at mid-ocean ridges, and the theory of **plate tectonics** was born.

## The plates and what moves them

The lithosphere is broken into about **seven major plates** (African, Eurasian, North American, South American, Pacific, Indo-Australian and Antarctic) and many smaller ones, such as the Nazca and Arabian plates.

Plates move because of **convection currents** in the mantle: hot material rises, spreads out beneath the plates and drags them along, then cools and sinks. The pull of sinking plate edges and the push from mid-ocean ridges also help.

## The three types of plate boundary

![Cross-sections of a constructive boundary where plates move apart and magma rises, a destructive boundary where an oceanic plate sinks under a continental plate, and a conservative boundary where plates slide past each other](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/plate-boundaries.svg)

*Figure 1. Plates move apart, together or past each other. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Boundary | Movement | What happens | Landforms and hazards | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Constructive (divergent)** | plates move **apart** | magma rises and forms new crust | mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, gentle volcanoes, mild earthquakes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge; East African Rift Valley; Iceland |
| **Destructive (convergent)** | plates move **together** | the denser oceanic plate sinks (**subduction**) and melts | ocean trenches, fold mountains, explosive volcanoes, strong earthquakes | Andes (Nazca under South American plate); Japan |
| **Collision** (a kind of destructive boundary) | two continental plates meet | neither sinks; crust crumples upwards | the highest fold mountains, earthquakes | Himalayas (Indo-Australian and Eurasian plates) |
| **Conservative (transform)** | plates slide **past** each other | crust is neither made nor destroyed | strong earthquakes, no volcanoes | San Andreas Fault, California |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Explain why the Andes Mountains have volcanoes and earthquakes.**

The oceanic **Nazca plate** moves towards the continental **South American plate**. Being denser, the Nazca plate is **subducted** beneath it. Friction as it sinks causes **earthquakes**; as it descends into the hot mantle it melts, and the magma rises through the crust to form **volcanoes**. The edge of the continent is crumpled into **fold mountains**: the Andes.
:::

## Plate boundaries and West Africa

:::callout{type=local-example}
West Africa, including Sierra Leone, lies well inside the **African plate**, far from any active boundary, so large earthquakes and volcanoes are rare. The great exception is **Mount Cameroon**, an active volcano on a line of weakness in the crust (the Cameroon Volcanic Line) that erupted as recently as 1999 and 2000. In East Africa, the African plate is slowly splitting along the **East African Rift Valley**, a constructive boundary in the making.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say volcanoes occur at conservative boundaries. At a conservative boundary crust is neither created nor destroyed, so there is no magma to form volcanoes, only earthquakes.
:::

## Key points

- **Plate tectonics:** the lithosphere is broken into **plates** moving on the mantle, driven by **convection currents**.
- **Wegener (1912):** continental drift from **Pangaea**; evidence from coastlines, fossils, rocks and climate.
- **Constructive:** apart, new crust, ridges and rifts (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift).
- **Destructive:** together, subduction, trenches, fold mountains, explosive volcanoes (Andes, Japan).
- **Collision:** continental plates crumple into high fold mountains (Himalayas).
- **Conservative:** past each other, earthquakes only (San Andreas Fault).

## Quick check

1. Who proposed the theory of continental drift, and what was the supercontinent called?
2. What drives the movement of plates?
3. What happens at a constructive plate boundary?
4. Why are there no volcanoes at conservative boundaries?
5. Name a fold mountain range formed by the collision of two continental plates.

**Answers:**

1. Alfred Wegener; Pangaea.
2. Convection currents in the mantle (helped by ridge push and slab pull).
3. Plates move apart and magma rises to form new crust, building ridges or rift valleys.
4. Crust is neither created nor destroyed there, so no magma is produced.
5. The Himalayas.
