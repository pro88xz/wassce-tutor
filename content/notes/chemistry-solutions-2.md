Sugar dissolves faster and in greater amounts in hot tea than in cold; fish gasp for air in warm, still water; a bottle of soft drink fizzes when opened. All three are about **solubility**: how much of a substance dissolves, and what changes it. This lesson covers solubility, solubility curves and the calculations WASSCE sets on them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define solubility in g per 100 g of water and in mol/dm³
- calculate solubility from experimental data
- read and use solubility curves
- calculate the mass of crystals formed when a saturated solution cools
- explain the effects of temperature and pressure on the solubility of solids and gases
- describe fractional crystallisation

## Solubility

:::callout{type=definition}
The **solubility** of a substance at a given temperature is the **maximum amount** of it that dissolves in a given amount of solvent to form a **saturated solution** at that temperature.
:::

It is commonly given as:

- **grams of solute per 100 g of water**, or
- **moles of solute per dm³ of solution** (mol/dm³).

Always state the **temperature**, because solubility changes with it.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**1. At 30 °C, 12 g of a salt saturates 40 g of water. Find its solubility in g per 100 g of water.**

$$\text{solubility} = \dfrac{12}{40} \times 100 = 30 \text{ g per 100 g water}$$

**2. 250 cm³ of saturated sodium chloride solution at 25 °C is evaporated to dryness, leaving 79.0 g of NaCl. Find the solubility in mol/dm³.** (NaCl = 58.5)

- Moles of NaCl $= 79.0 \div 58.5 = 1.35$ mol in 0.250 dm³.
- Solubility $= 1.35 \div 0.250 = 5.40$ mol/dm³.
:::

### Determining solubility in the laboratory

1. Make a saturated solution at a known temperature (stir with excess solute; some must stay undissolved).
2. Filter, or decant, some of the clear saturated solution into a weighed evaporating dish; weigh again.
3. Evaporate to dryness carefully, cool and weigh (repeat to constant mass).
4. Mass of solute = final mass − dish; mass of water = solution − solute. Calculate per 100 g of water.

## Solubility curves

![Solubility curves: potassium nitrate rises steeply with temperature, copper(II) sulphate rises moderately and sodium chloride hardly changes; cooling saturated potassium nitrate from 60 °C to 20 °C crystallises about 78 g](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/solubility-curves.svg)

*Figure 1. Solubility curves for three salts. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

A **solubility curve** plots solubility against temperature. From it you can read:

- the **solubility** at any temperature
- the temperature at which a solution becomes **saturated**
- how much solid **crystallises** when a solution cools

**Points on the curve** are saturated solutions; **points below** are unsaturated; **points above** are supersaturated.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Using the curves in Figure 1.**

1. **Solubility of potassium nitrate at 60 °C:** read off 110 g per 100 g water.
2. **Crystallisation:** a saturated solution of KNO₃ in 100 g of water is cooled from 60 °C (110 g) to 20 °C (about 32 g). Mass of crystals $= 110 - 32 = 78$ g.
3. **For 50 g of water**, halve it: $78 \div 2 = 39$ g of crystals.
4. **Which salt is best purified by cooling crystallisation?** KNO₃, because its solubility changes most with temperature. NaCl changes so little that cooling gives almost no crystals; it is recovered by **evaporation** instead.
:::

## Factors affecting solubility

### Solids in liquids

- **Temperature:** the solubility of most solids **increases** with temperature (KNO₃ strongly, NaCl only slightly). A few, such as calcium hydroxide, become **less** soluble when hot.
- **Nature of solute and solvent:** like dissolves like.
- **Pressure** has almost no effect on solids.

Stirring and crushing the solid make it dissolve **faster**, but they do **not** change how much eventually dissolves.

### Gases in liquids

- **Temperature:** gases are **less soluble at higher temperatures**. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so fish in warm, still ponds may suffocate. Boiled water tastes flat because its dissolved air has been driven out.
- **Pressure:** gases are **more soluble at higher pressure** (Henry's law). Soft drinks are bottled under high pressure of carbon(IV) oxide; when the bottle is opened, the pressure drops and the gas comes out as bubbles.
- Very soluble gases such as **ammonia and hydrogen chloride** react with water, which is why they dissolve so much.

## Fractional crystallisation

When two salts with **different solubility curves** are dissolved together, cooling the hot saturated solution crystallises mainly the salt whose solubility falls fastest, while the other stays in solution.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Separating potassium nitrate from a little sodium chloride.** Dissolve the mixture in the minimum of hot water and cool to 20 °C. Most of the KNO₃ crystallises, because its solubility falls steeply. The small amount of NaCl stays dissolved, because its solubility hardly changes. Filter off the KNO₃ crystals. Repeating the process (recrystallisation) gives purer crystals.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sea-salt makers along the Sierra Leone coast **evaporate** brine rather than cooling it. Sodium chloride's solubility hardly changes with temperature, so cooling would give very few crystals, but evaporating the water forces the salt out of solution.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Stirring and heating make sugar dissolve **faster**; only heating also makes **more** dissolve. Do not confuse the **rate** of dissolving with **solubility**.
:::

## Key points

- **Solubility** is the maximum mass (or moles) of solute that saturates a given amount of solvent **at a stated temperature**.
- Solubility (g/100 g water) = mass of solute ÷ mass of water × 100.
- **Solubility curves** show saturated solutions; cooling a saturated solution crystallises (solubility at high T − solubility at low T).
- Most solids are **more** soluble when hot; gases are **less** soluble when hot and **more** soluble at high pressure.
- **Fractional crystallisation** separates salts whose solubility changes differently with temperature.

## Quick check

1. At 25 °C, 9.0 g of a salt saturates 25 g of water. Find its solubility in g per 100 g of water.
2. A salt's solubility is 80 g/100 g water at 70 °C and 30 g/100 g water at 20 °C. What mass crystallises when a saturated solution in 50 g of water is cooled from 70 °C to 20 °C?
3. Why do fizzy drinks go flat faster when warm?
4. Why is sodium chloride recovered from solution by evaporation rather than cooling?

**Answers:**

1. $9.0 \div 25 \times 100 = 36$ g per 100 g water.
2. For 100 g: $80 - 30 = 50$ g; for 50 g of water: 25 g.
3. Gases are less soluble at higher temperatures, so carbon(IV) oxide escapes faster.
4. Its solubility changes very little with temperature, so cooling gives few crystals.
