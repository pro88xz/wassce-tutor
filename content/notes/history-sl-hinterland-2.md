The **Sierra Leone Protectorate** was declared on **31 August 1896**, bringing the hinterland of Sierra Leone under British rule. Britain acted to stop **French expansion** from Guinea, to protect Freetown's **trade routes**, to end **wars and the slave trade** in the interior, and to meet the **"effective occupation"** rule of the Berlin Conference. The way was prepared by **boundary agreements with France (1889 and 1895) and Liberia**, by **Travelling Commissioners** and by the **Frontier Police (1890)**. The Protectorate was divided into **five districts**, each under a **District Commissioner**, and ruled through **paramount chiefs**. This lesson explains **why the British declared a Protectorate over the Sierra Leone hinterland in 1896** and how it was governed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning of a protectorate and how it differed from the Colony
- explain the reasons for the declaration of the Protectorate in 1896
- describe the steps that prepared the way for the Protectorate
- describe how the Protectorate was governed under the Protectorate Ordinance
- explain how the Protectorate affected the chiefs and people

## Colony and Protectorate

:::callout{type=definition}
A **protectorate** is a territory that is not formally annexed as a colony, but whose external affairs and government are controlled by a stronger power that claims to **protect** it. Its people were **British-protected persons**, not British subjects, and were largely ruled under **customary law** through their chiefs.
:::

## Why Britain declared the Protectorate

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **French expansion** | the **French** were advancing from Guinea and the upper Niger and might cut Freetown off from its hinterland |
| **Protecting trade** | Freetown's trade and customs revenue depended on safe **trade routes** to the interior |
| **Ending wars** | wars between chiefdoms, raids by war boys and **Samori's sofas** disrupted trade and threatened the Colony |
| **The Berlin Conference** | the rule of **effective occupation (1885)** meant Britain had to show real control to keep its claim |
| **The slave trade** | Britain wanted to stop slave raiding and trading in the interior |
| **Pressure from merchants and missionaries** | Krio and European traders and missionaries wanted **protection** |
| **Revenue** | a protectorate could be made to **pay for itself** through taxes |

## Steps towards the Protectorate

1. **Treaties** of friendship and cession with chiefs throughout the nineteenth century.
2. **Boundary agreements:** with **France (1882, 1889, and the boundary settlement of 1895)**, fixing the border with Guinea; and with **Liberia (from 1885)**, fixing the southern border.
3. **Travelling Commissioners** (from **1890**), such as **T. J. Alldridge** and **G. H. Garrett**, toured the interior making treaties with chiefs and collecting information.
4. The **Frontier Police**, created in **1890**, set up posts along the borders and trade routes to keep order.
5. The **declaration of the Protectorate** on **31 August 1896**, followed by the **Protectorate Ordinance** (1896, revised 1897).

## Government of the Protectorate

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Five districts** | **Karene, Ronietta, Bandajuma, Panguma and Koinadugu** |
| **District Commissioners (DCs)** | British officials who ran each district, judged serious cases and supervised chiefs |
| **Paramount chiefs** | recognised as rulers of their chiefdoms; collected taxes and kept order under the DC |
| **Courts** | **chiefs' courts** for minor civil cases under customary law; **DCs' courts** for serious crimes and cases involving non-natives; mixed courts of DC and chiefs |
| **Frontier Police** | kept order and supported the DCs |
| **Taxation** | a **hut tax**, to begin in **January 1898**, to pay for the administration |
| **Land and laws** | the Ordinance gave the government power over **"waste" land**, and banned **slave dealing**, **some secret-society practices** and other customs |

## Effects on the chiefs and people

1. Chiefs **lost their independence**: they could not make war, and their judicial powers were reduced.
2. Chiefs who resisted or failed to collect tax could be **fined, imprisoned or deposed**.
3. The **Frontier Police**, many of them former slaves or men from other areas, often **abused** chiefs and people.
4. People feared that the government would **take their land**.
5. The **hut tax** was seen as payment for land that already belonged to them.

These grievances led directly to the **Hut Tax War of 1898**, studied in the next topic.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **chiefdoms** of today's Sierra Leone, and the office of **paramount chief**, took their modern form under the Protectorate. The country remained divided into **Colony** and **Protectorate** until **1951**, when the **Stevenson Constitution** began to bring the two together; this division shaped politics up to independence.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The Protectorate was **not** the same as the Colony. The **Colony** (Freetown and nearby areas) had been British since **1808**; the **Protectorate** (the hinterland) was declared only in **1896**, and its people had a different legal status and different courts.
:::

## Key points

- **Protectorate declared 31 August 1896** over the hinterland; people were **British-protected persons** under **customary law**.
- Reasons: **French expansion, trade, ending wars and slave trade, effective occupation**, merchants' pressure, revenue.
- Steps: **treaties**, **boundary agreements** with France and Liberia, **Travelling Commissioners** and the **Frontier Police (1890)**.
- **Five districts** (Karene, Ronietta, Bandajuma, Panguma, Koinadugu) under **District Commissioners**; rule through **paramount chiefs**; **hut tax** from 1898.
- Chiefs lost power; police abuses, land fears and the tax led to the **Hut Tax War**.
