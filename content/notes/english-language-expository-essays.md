An expository essay **explains**. It tells the reader how something works, why something happens, what the causes and effects of a problem are, or how to do something. *"Explain the causes and effects of rural-urban migration"*, *"How can students prepare for examinations?"*, *"Describe the steps involved in making gari"*: all are expository questions. The writer does not take sides, as in an argument, or tell a story. The goal is **clarity**: a reader who knew little should finish the essay understanding the subject well. This lesson shows the main patterns of exposition, the language of clear explanation, and a complete model essay.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- recognise an expository question and the pattern it needs
- organise explanation by cause and effect, by process, or by classification
- write clear topic sentences and use transitions that show logical relationships
- explain technical or unfamiliar ideas simply, with examples
- write an expository essay with a balanced, informative tone

## Four patterns of exposition

| Pattern | The question asks | Organise by |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause and effect** | *What are the causes and effects of…?* | causes first, then effects (or one cause and its effects at a time) |
| **Process** | *How is … made? How can you…? Describe the steps…* | steps in **time order** |
| **Classification** | *What are the types / kinds of…?* | one type per paragraph, with its features |
| **Problem and solution** | *What problems… and how can they be solved?* | problems, then solutions, matched |

:::callout{type=tip}
The question word tells you the pattern. *"Explain the causes and effects"* → cause and effect. *"How is gari made?"* → process. *"What are the types of pollution?"* → classification. Plan in that pattern before you write.
:::

## The language of explanation

### Transitions that show logic

| Relationship | Transitions |
|---|---|
| **Cause** | because, since, as, due to, owing to |
| **Effect** | as a result, therefore, consequently, so, this leads to |
| **Sequence** | first, next, then, after that, finally |
| **Addition** | moreover, furthermore, in addition, also |
| **Example** | for example, for instance, such as |
| **Contrast** | however, on the other hand, whereas |

### Explaining clearly

- **Define** any key term the first time you use it.
- Give an **example** for every general statement.
- Keep a **neutral, informative tone**: *"This leads to…"* rather than *"This is terrible because…"*.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Taking sides in an expository essay.** *"Explain the causes of rural-urban migration"* does not ask whether migration is good or bad. If you spend half the essay arguing that young people should stay in the villages, you have answered a different question.
:::

## A process essay in miniature

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Question:** *Describe the steps involved in making gari.*

*Gari, a staple food across West Africa, is made from cassava in five main steps.*

1. ***Peeling and washing.** Freshly harvested cassava roots are peeled with a knife to remove the brown skin and the layer beneath it, then washed thoroughly in clean water.*
2. ***Grating.** The roots are grated, by hand or with a motorised grater, into a wet pulp.*
3. ***Fermenting and pressing.** The pulp is packed into sacks and left for two or three days to ferment, while heavy weights or a press squeeze out the water. Fermentation gives gari its slightly sour taste and makes it safe to eat.*
4. ***Sieving.** The pressed cake is broken up and sieved to remove fibres and lumps.*
5. ***Frying (garifying).** The sieved pulp is roasted in a wide, shallow pan over a fire, stirred continuously until it is dry, crisp and light.*

*The gari is then cooled, packed and stored, and can keep for months.*

Each step has a **heading word**, follows **time order**, and **explains why** where it matters (fermentation).
:::

## A complete model essay (cause and effect)

**Question:** *Explain the causes and effects of the movement of young people from rural areas to the cities in Sierra Leone.*

::::model{title="Why young people leave the villages, and what follows" label="Model expository essay"}
**RURAL-URBAN MIGRATION IN SIERRA LEONE: CAUSES AND EFFECTS**

Every week, buses and lorries arrive in Freetown, Bo and Kenema carrying young people from the villages, many with a single bag and an address scribbled on a piece of paper. This movement, known as rural-urban migration, has changed the face of the country. To understand it, we must look both at what pushes young people out of the villages and at what pulls them towards the towns, and then at the effects it has on both.

The first and most important cause is the search for work and income. In most villages, farming is the main occupation, but it is hard, the tools are simple, and the returns are small and uncertain. A young person with some education can see little future in the family farm. Towns, on the other hand, appear to offer jobs in trade, transport, construction and offices, and even the hope of an income is enough to attract many.

Education is a second cause. Many villages have only a primary school, so children who wish to continue must move to a town with a secondary school. Once they have finished school, few return, because the jobs that match their certificates are in the towns.

Amenities are a third cause. Electricity, piped water, hospitals and entertainment are far more common in towns. For a young person, the bright lights of the city, with its music, phones and social life, can seem far more attractive than an evening in a village with no power.

The effects of this movement are felt in both places. In the villages, the loss of young people leaves farming in the hands of the elderly, so less food is produced and more must be imported. Communities lose the energy and skills they need to develop, and some villages are slowly emptying.

In the towns, the effects are just as serious. The sudden growth in population puts pressure on housing, so many newcomers end up in crowded informal settlements on hillsides or beside waterways, where they are at risk of floods and disease. Water, electricity, schools and hospitals are stretched beyond their limits. Because there are not enough jobs for everyone, unemployment rises, and some frustrated young people turn to petty crime or drugs.

Not every effect is negative, however. Money sent home by relatives in the towns supports many village families, and migrants bring new ideas and skills back with them when they visit.

In short, young people leave the villages mainly in search of work, education and a better life, and their movement weakens rural farming while overcrowding the towns. Understanding these causes is the first step towards balanced development that would give young people good reasons to stay.
::::

### Why it scores

- **Pattern**: causes (work, education, amenities), then effects (villages, towns, and a balancing positive effect), exactly matching *"causes and effects"*.
- **Clarity**: a definition (*rural-urban migration*) in the introduction, a topic sentence for every paragraph, examples throughout.
- **Transitions** that show logic: *the first and most important cause*, *on the other hand*, *because*, *so*, *however*, *in short*.
- **Neutral tone**: it explains rather than argues, ending with understanding rather than a demand.
- **Length**: about 460 words.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Which pattern does each question need? (a) 'What are the types of soil?' (b) 'How can a student prepare for the WASSCE?' (c) 'Explain the effects of deforestation.'"}
(a) **Classification**. (b) **Process** (or steps / advice in a logical order). (c) **Cause and effect** (effects).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Join with a transition that shows effect: 'The rains failed. Many farmers lost their crops.'"}
*The rains failed; **as a result**, many farmers lost their crops.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Rewrite neutrally for an expository essay: 'Deforestation is a stupid and evil practice.'"}
*Deforestation removes the trees that hold soil in place and provide habitats for wildlife, leading to erosion, flooding and the loss of species.*
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Expository essay**: an essay that explains a subject clearly, such as causes and effects, a process or types.
- **Cause and effect essay**: an expository essay that explains why something happens and what results.
- **Process essay**: an expository essay that explains the steps of how something is made or done.
- **Linking words**: words that show how ideas relate, such as "therefore" for effect, "however" for contrast and "first… next… finally" for sequence.
- **Classification essay**: an expository essay that explains the types or kinds of something.
- **Problem and solution essay**: an expository essay that explains problems and how they can be solved.
:::

## Key points

- Expository writing **explains**; it does not argue or tell a story.
- Choose the **pattern** the question needs: **cause and effect**, **process**, **classification**, or **problem and solution**.
- Use **topic sentences**, **definitions**, **examples** and **logical transitions**.
- Keep the tone **neutral and informative**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expository questions are rewarded for **clear, accurate, well-organised explanation**. Examiners look for content that covers **every part** of the question (both causes **and** effects), paragraphs in a logical order, and transitions that make the relationships between ideas clear. Essays that drift into argument or storytelling lose content and organisation marks.
:::
