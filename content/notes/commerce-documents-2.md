Importing a container of goods into Freetown involves a buyer, a seller in another country, two banks, a shipping line, an insurer and the customs authority, and each needs proof of what is happening. This lesson covers the documents of foreign trade, the import and export procedures, and the price terms (FOB, CIF and others) used in international contracts.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the procedure for importing and exporting goods
- explain the purpose of the main documents of foreign trade
- explain how a letter of credit works
- explain terms such as FOB, FAS, CIF, C&F, ex-works and franco
- calculate a CIF price

## Import procedure

1. **Enquiry** and quotation from the foreign supplier.
2. Obtain an **import licence** or permit where required, and arrange **foreign exchange** through a bank.
3. Send an **order (indent)** to the supplier or through an import agent (**indent house**).
4. Arrange **payment**, often by **letter of credit**.
5. The goods are shipped; the importer receives the **shipping documents** (bill of lading, invoice, insurance certificate, certificate of origin).
6. **Customs clearance**: lodge the **customs entry (bill of entry)**, pay import duties and taxes; the goods may wait in a **bonded warehouse** until duty is paid.
7. Collect the goods from the port using the **bill of lading**, usually through a **clearing and forwarding agent**.

### The indent

| Open indent | Closed indent |
|---|---|
| The importer's agent may buy from **any** supplier who offers the best terms | The agent must buy from a **named** supplier or a named brand |

## Export procedure

1. Receive an **enquiry or indent** from a foreign buyer; send a **quotation** or **pro forma invoice**.
2. Obtain an **export licence** or permit where required.
3. Prepare and pack the goods; book space with a shipping line using a **shipping note**.
4. Load the goods; the ship's officer issues a **mate's receipt**, exchanged for the **bill of lading**.
5. Obtain **insurance** (certificate of insurance), a **certificate of origin** and, where required, a **consular invoice**.
6. Clear the goods through **export customs**.
7. Send the documents to the buyer or through the bank, and **receive payment**.

## Documents of foreign trade

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Bill of lading** | Issued by the shipping company: a **receipt** for the goods, **evidence of the contract** of carriage and a **document of title** (whoever holds it can claim the goods) |
| **Air waybill** | The equivalent for air freight (not a document of title) |
| **Certificate of origin** | States the country where the goods were made; used to apply the correct duties (issued by chambers of commerce) |
| **Consular invoice** | An invoice certified by the importing country's consulate, to check value and prevent fraud |
| **Certificate of insurance** | Proof that the goods are insured |
| **Shipping note** | Gives the shipping company details of goods to be loaded |
| **Mate's receipt** | Issued when goods are loaded on the ship; exchanged for the bill of lading |
| **Customs entry (bill of entry)** | Declaration to customs of goods imported or exported, for duty assessment |
| **Import / export licence** | Government permission to import or export certain goods |
| **Bill of exchange** | An order to pay a sum on demand or at a fixed future date |
| **Letter of credit** | A bank's guarantee that the exporter will be paid when correct documents are presented |
| **Dock warrant / warehouse warrant** | Proof of goods stored at the docks or in a warehouse; can be transferred |

**Types of bill of lading:** a **clean** bill (goods received in good condition) and a **claused (dirty)** bill (the shipping company notes damage or shortage); a **through** bill covers more than one carrier to the final destination.

The letter of credit protects **both** sides: the exporter is sure of payment when the documents are correct, and the importer pays only when proof of shipment is presented.

## Terms of sale in foreign trade

| Term | The seller's price covers… |
|---|---|
| **Ex-works (ex-factory, loco)** | The goods at the seller's premises only; the buyer pays all transport |
| **FAS** (free alongside ship) | Goods delivered alongside the ship at the port of shipment |
| **FOB** (free on board) | Goods loaded **on board** the ship; the buyer pays freight and insurance from then on |
| **C&F / CFR** (cost and freight) | Cost of goods plus freight to the destination port; the buyer insures |
| **CIF** (cost, insurance and freight) | Cost of goods **plus insurance plus freight** to the destination port |
| **Ex-ship** | Goods made available on board the ship at the destination port |
| **Franco (free delivered, delivered duty paid)** | All costs to the buyer's premises, including duty |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Calculating a CIF price.** A trader in Freetown buys goods costing US\$10,000 FOB. Freight to Freetown is US\$800 and insurance is US\$200.

$$\text{CIF} = \text{cost} + \text{insurance} + \text{freight} = 10000 + 200 + 800 = \text{US\$}11{,}000$$

If the FOB price is quoted, the importer must arrange and pay freight and insurance separately; a CIF price includes them.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Importers in Sierra Leone usually hire **clearing and forwarding agents** to handle the customs entry and port charges at the Queen Elizabeth II Quay, and pay duties and taxes through the NRA's customs system before the goods are released.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The bill of lading is a **document of title**: it represents ownership of the goods. An air waybill is **not** a document of title. Mixing them up is a common error.
:::

## Key points

- **Import procedure**: enquiry, licence and foreign exchange, indent, payment, shipping documents, customs entry, collection.
- **Export procedure**: indent, licence, shipping note, mate's receipt, bill of lading, insurance and certificate of origin, customs, payment.
- The **bill of lading** is a receipt, evidence of contract and **document of title**.
- A **letter of credit** guarantees payment through banks.
- **FOB** covers goods on board; **CIF** adds insurance and freight to the destination.

## Quick check

1. Give the three uses of a bill of lading.
2. What is the difference between an open indent and a closed indent?
3. Goods cost US\$5,000 FOB; freight is US\$600 and insurance US\$100. Find the CIF price.
4. Who issues a certificate of origin, and why is it needed?

**Answers:** 1. A receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title. 2. An open indent lets the agent buy from any supplier; a closed indent names the supplier or brand. 3. $5000 + 100 + 600 = $ US\$5,700. 4. A chamber of commerce (or an authorised body); it shows where the goods were made, so the correct duties and trade preferences apply.
