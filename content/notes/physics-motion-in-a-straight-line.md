# Motion in a straight line: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and graphs

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to distinguish distance from displacement and speed from velocity; calculate average speed, average velocity and acceleration; interpret motion in one dimension using signs and a chosen direction; read and construct distance–time, displacement–time and velocity–time graphs; obtain velocity and acceleration from graph gradients; determine displacement from the signed area under a velocity–time graph; and solve practical WASSCE problems with correct units and significant figures.

## 1. What motion means

An object is in motion when its position changes with time relative to a chosen reference point. A passenger sitting in a moving poda-poda is at rest relative to the vehicle but moving relative to the road. Motion is therefore described relative to a reference frame.

For straight-line motion, choose one axis and define a positive direction. For example, take motion towards the east as positive. A displacement of +12 m means 12 m east of the starting position; −5 m means 5 m west. The sign records direction, while the magnitude records size.

A body can move and return to its starting point. Its total distance travelled is then positive, but its net displacement is zero.

## 2. Distance and displacement

**Distance** is the total length of the actual path travelled. It is a scalar: it has magnitude but no direction.

**Displacement** is the change in position from the initial point to the final point. It is a vector: it has magnitude and direction. In one dimension:

\[
\text{displacement}=x_f-x_i
\]

where x_i is initial position and x_f is final position.

For motion along a straight road, suppose a student walks 300 m east to a shop and then 100 m west. The total distance is 300 + 100 = 400 m. Taking east as positive, displacement is +300 − 100 = +200 m, or 200 m east.

Distance is never negative. Displacement can be positive, negative or zero. The magnitude of displacement cannot exceed the distance travelled.

**Worked example 1 — Out-and-back journey.** A runner completes one 400 m lap and finishes at the starting point. Calculate the distance and displacement.

Distance = 400 m. Initial and final positions are the same, so displacement = 0 m.

Do not state that the runner travelled zero metres: zero is the displacement, not the distance.

## 3. Speed and velocity

**Average speed** is total distance divided by total time:

\[
\text{average speed}=\frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}
\]

**Average velocity** is displacement divided by elapsed time:

\[
\text{average velocity}=\frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{elapsed time}}
\]

Speed is scalar and cannot be negative. Velocity is a vector and its sign indicates direction along the chosen axis.

The SI unit of both is metre per second (m s⁻¹). Other common units include km h⁻¹ and cm s⁻¹. Convert km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹ by multiplying by 5/18; convert m s⁻¹ to km h⁻¹ by multiplying by 18/5.

**Worked example 2 — Average speed and velocity.** A cyclist travels 600 m east in 120 s, then 200 m west in 40 s. Find average speed and average velocity for the whole journey.

Total distance = 600 + 200 = 800 m. Total time = 120 + 40 = 160 s.

Average speed = 800/160 = 5.0 m s⁻¹.

Taking east as positive, displacement = +600 − 200 = +400 m.

Average velocity = +400/160 = +2.5 m s⁻¹, or 2.5 m s⁻¹ east.

The average speed is not the same as the magnitude of average velocity because the cyclist changed direction.

## 4. Instantaneous speed and velocity

Average speed describes an interval. **Instantaneous speed** is the speed at a particular instant, such as the value shown by a vehicle's speedometer. Instantaneous velocity is the rate of change of position at that instant and includes direction.

For a short time interval, average velocity can approximate instantaneous velocity. In calculus language, instantaneous velocity is the limiting rate of change of displacement with time; at WASSCE level, it is commonly obtained from the gradient of a tangent to a displacement–time graph.

## 5. Acceleration and deceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity:

\[
a=\frac{v-u}{t}
\]

where u is initial velocity, v is final velocity and t is elapsed time. The SI unit is m s⁻².

Acceleration is a vector. A positive acceleration means acceleration in the chosen positive direction; it does not always mean that the object is speeding up. If an object moves in the negative direction and has positive acceleration, its speed may decrease.

**Deceleration** or retardation describes a reduction in speed. It is not automatically synonymous with negative acceleration: the sign depends on the chosen positive direction and the direction of motion.

**Worked example 3 — Uniform acceleration.** A bus increases its velocity from 5 m s⁻¹ to 17 m s⁻¹ in 6 s. Find its acceleration.

\[
a=\frac{v-u}{t}=\frac{17-5}{6}=2.0\text{ m s}^{-2}
\]

The acceleration is +2.0 m s⁻² in the direction chosen as positive.

**Worked example 4 — Slowing down.** A car moving east at 20 m s⁻¹ comes to rest uniformly in 5 s. Take east as positive.

\[
a=\frac{0-20}{5}=-4.0\text{ m s}^{-2}
\]

The negative sign indicates acceleration towards the west, opposite to the car's initial motion. The magnitude of the deceleration is 4.0 m s⁻².

## 6. Distance–time and displacement–time graphs

Time is plotted on the horizontal axis. Distance or displacement is plotted on the vertical axis.

For a distance–time graph, the gradient gives speed:

\[
\text{speed}=\frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}
\]

For a displacement–time graph, the gradient gives velocity:

\[
v=\frac{\text{change in displacement}}{\text{change in time}}
\]

A horizontal line means the object is stationary. A straight sloping line means constant speed or constant velocity, respectively. A steeper line represents a greater speed or a greater magnitude of velocity. A curved line means the gradient is changing, so speed or velocity is changing.

On a displacement–time graph, a negative gradient represents motion in the negative direction. A curve that becomes progressively steeper has increasing speed in the direction represented by the gradient.

To calculate a gradient, choose two well-separated points on the straight line, read their coordinates, calculate the vertical change and divide by the horizontal change. Include units.

**Worked example 5 — Gradient.** A displacement–time graph passes through (2 s, 4 m) and (8 s, 22 m).

\[
v=\frac{22-4}{8-2}=\frac{18}{6}=3.0\text{ m s}^{-1}
\]

The constant velocity is +3.0 m s⁻¹.

## 7. Velocity–time graphs

A velocity–time graph plots velocity vertically and time horizontally.

The gradient gives acceleration:

\[
a=\frac{\text{change in velocity}}{\text{change in time}}
\]

The **signed area** between the graph and the time axis gives displacement. Areas above the time axis are positive; areas below it are negative. Total distance requires adding the magnitudes of the separate areas, not their signed sum.

For a constant velocity, the graph is horizontal and acceleration is zero. A straight rising line indicates constant positive acceleration. A straight falling line indicates constant negative acceleration. A line crossing the time axis indicates a change in direction if velocity changes sign.

For a triangular region, area = ½ × base × perpendicular height. For a rectangular region, area = base × height. Split a complex graph into rectangles, triangles and trapezia, and preserve the signs.

**Worked example 6 — Displacement from a velocity–time graph.** A body starts from rest and reaches 12 m s⁻¹ uniformly in 4 s. It then continues at 12 m s⁻¹ for 3 s. Find its total displacement.

First region (triangle):

\[
s_1=\frac12\times4\times12=24\text{ m}
\]

Second region (rectangle):

\[
s_2=3\times12=36\text{ m}
\]

Total displacement = 24 + 36 = 60 m in the positive direction.

## 8. Practical method for investigating motion

A ticker timer, light gate, stopwatch or motion sensor may be used, depending on the apparatus available. A trolley moving along a level track is a common school-laboratory model.

1. Set up a straight, stable track and identify the positive direction.
2. Measure and record the relevant distances or positions from a fixed reference point.
3. Record the time for each interval using the same timing method.
4. Repeat readings where practical and investigate unusual results rather than deleting them without justification.
5. Calculate speed or velocity with the correct distance or displacement and time.
6. Plot labelled axes with units and a sensible uniform scale.
7. Draw a best-fit line or smooth curve appropriate to the data.
8. Calculate gradients using well-separated points on the line, not two arbitrary noisy readings.

Reduce timing uncertainty by using electronic timing or light gates where available, repeating trials and timing over a longer interval when appropriate. Keep the track and release method consistent. Do not push a trolley differently between trials if the aim is to compare the same motion.

## 9. Common unit conversions and checks

- 1 km = 1,000 m.
- 1 h = 3,600 s.
- 1 km h⁻¹ = 5/18 m s⁻¹.
- 1 m s⁻¹ = 18/5 km h⁻¹.
- Acceleration is measured in m s⁻², not m s⁻¹.
- A gradient's unit is the vertical-axis unit divided by the horizontal-axis unit.
- An area under a velocity–time graph has unit (m s⁻¹) × s = m.

Check whether a numerical answer is physically reasonable. A walking speed of 5 m s⁻¹ is unusually high for an ordinary walk; a vehicle speed of 5 km h⁻¹ is very low. Unit checks often reveal conversion errors.

## WASSCE examination focus

Be prepared to:
- define distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration precisely;
- distinguish scalar quantities from vector quantities and state the direction of a velocity or displacement;
- calculate average speed and average velocity for journeys involving a change of direction;
- calculate acceleration from initial velocity, final velocity and time;
- interpret horizontal, sloping and curved sections of displacement–time and velocity–time graphs;
- calculate velocity or speed from a displacement–time gradient;
- calculate acceleration from a velocity–time gradient;
- calculate displacement from signed areas under a velocity–time graph and total distance from the sum of area magnitudes; and
- describe a practical procedure for collecting motion data and plotting a graph.

For graph questions, label both axes with quantities and units, use a uniform scale, plot points accurately and draw a suitable best-fit line. State whether an area is signed displacement or total distance. In numerical questions, write the formula, substitute values with units, retain the direction sign and round the final answer sensibly.

## 11. Formula summary

| Quantity | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Displacement | x_f − x_i |
| Average speed | Total distance ÷ total time |
| Average velocity | Displacement ÷ elapsed time |
| Acceleration | (v − u) ÷ t |
| Displacement from a velocity–time graph | Signed area under the graph |
| Velocity from a displacement–time graph | Gradient |
| Acceleration from a velocity–time graph | Gradient |
| km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹ | Multiply by 5/18 |

## 12. WASSCE practice questions

Show your working, include units and state the direction where relevant.

1. Define distance and displacement. State two differences between them.
2. A student walks 250 m north and then 90 m south. Calculate the total distance and the magnitude and direction of displacement.
3. A vehicle covers 1.8 km in 3 minutes. Calculate its average speed in m s⁻¹.
4. Convert 72 km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹.
5. A cyclist travels 500 m east in 100 s and then 200 m west in 50 s. Calculate average speed and average velocity for the whole journey.
6. Define acceleration and state its SI unit.
7. A car's velocity changes from 8 m s⁻¹ to 26 m s⁻¹ in 6 s. Calculate its acceleration.
8. A train moving west at 15 m s⁻¹ slows uniformly to 3 m s⁻¹ in 4 s. Taking east as positive, calculate its acceleration.
9. A displacement–time graph passes through (0 s, 0 m) and (5 s, 20 m). Find the velocity.
10. Explain what a horizontal line on a displacement–time graph represents.
11. State the physical quantity represented by the gradient of a velocity–time graph.
12. A velocity–time graph is a triangle with base 8 s and height 10 m s⁻¹ above the time axis. Calculate displacement.
13. A body moves at 6 m s⁻¹ for 5 s, then at 2 m s⁻¹ in the opposite direction for 3 s. Find total distance and net displacement.
14. Explain why the area below the time axis on a velocity–time graph is negative.
15. Describe four precautions when investigating the motion of a trolley along a straight track.

## 13. Answers and worked solutions

1. Distance is the total path length and is scalar. Displacement is the change in position with direction and is vector. Distance depends on the path; displacement depends only on initial and final positions.
2. Distance = 250 + 90 = 340 m. Displacement = 250 − 90 = 160 m north.
3. 1.8 km = 1,800 m; 3 min = 180 s. Average speed = 1,800/180 = 10 m s⁻¹.
4. 72 × 5/18 = 20 m s⁻¹.
5. Distance = 700 m; time = 150 s; average speed = 700/150 = 4.67 m s⁻¹. Displacement = 500 − 200 = 300 m east; average velocity = 300/150 = 2.0 m s⁻¹ east.
6. Acceleration is rate of change of velocity with time. SI unit: m s⁻².
7. a = (26 − 8)/6 = 3.0 m s⁻².
8. East is positive, so westward velocities are negative: u = −15 m s⁻¹ and v = −3 m s⁻¹. a = [−3 − (−15)]/4 = +3.0 m s⁻², eastward.
9. Gradient = (20 − 0)/(5 − 0) = 4.0 m s⁻¹.
10. The displacement is constant; the object is stationary.
11. Acceleration.
12. Displacement = ½ × 8 × 10 = 40 m.
13. Distance = (6 × 5) + (2 × 3) = 36 m. Taking the first direction as positive, displacement = 30 − 6 = 24 m in the first direction.
14. The velocity is negative, so the signed area represents displacement in the negative direction.
15. Examples: use a straight stable track; keep the release method consistent; use a fixed reference point; measure time with the same timing method; repeat trials; label graph axes and units; avoid parallax when reading a scale.
