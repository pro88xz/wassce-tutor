How do you measure the specific heat capacity of a metal? Drop it, hot, into cold water and see how warm the water gets. This "method of mixtures" is a WASSCE practical classic, and the heat-balance calculation behind it is examined almost every year.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the principle of the method of mixtures (heat lost = heat gained)
- find the specific heat capacity of a solid or liquid by the method of mixtures
- find specific heat capacity by the electrical method
- state precautions that reduce heat losses and describe Newton's law of cooling

## The principle of calorimetry

:::callout{type=definition}
**Method of mixtures:** when hot and cold bodies are mixed (with no heat lost to the surroundings), the **heat lost by the hot bodies equals the heat gained by the cold bodies**, until they reach the same final temperature.
:::

A **calorimeter** is a container, usually of polished copper, in which such measurements are made. It sits inside a larger, lagged container, has a lid, and comes with a stirrer.

## Specific heat capacity of a solid by the method of mixtures

1. Weigh the empty calorimeter with stirrer ($m_c$), then with cold water ($m_w$ of water). Record the water's temperature θ₁.
2. Heat the solid (mass $m_s$) in boiling water for several minutes so it reaches θ₂ (about 100 °C).
3. Transfer it **quickly** into the calorimeter. Stir and record the **highest** temperature reached, θ₃.

Heat lost by the solid = heat gained by the water + heat gained by the calorimeter:

$$m_s c_s(\theta_2 - \theta_3) = m_w c_w(\theta_3 - \theta_1) + m_c c_c(\theta_3 - \theta_1)$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 0.20 kg metal block at 100 °C is dropped into 0.30 kg of water at 25 °C in a copper calorimeter of mass 0.10 kg. The final temperature is 30 °C. Find the specific heat capacity of the metal. ($c_w = 4200$, $c_{Cu} = 400$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)**

Heat gained by water: $0.30 \times 4200 \times 5 = 6300$ J.

Heat gained by calorimeter: $0.10 \times 400 \times 5 = 200$ J.

Heat lost by metal: $0.20 \times c \times (100 - 30) = 14c$.

$$14c = 6300 + 200 = 6500 \Rightarrow c = 464\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$$

(close to iron, 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Mixing water.** 2 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 3 kg of water at 20 °C. Ignoring heat losses, find the final temperature.

$$2 \times 4200 \times (80 - \theta) = 3 \times 4200 \times (\theta - 20)$$

$$160 - 2\theta = 3\theta - 60 \Rightarrow 5\theta = 220 \Rightarrow \theta = 44\ °\text{C}$$

The specific heat capacity cancels when only water is mixed.
:::

## The electrical method

An immersion heater of known power heats a known mass of the substance (a liquid in a calorimeter, or a metal block with holes for the heater and a thermometer).

1. Measure the mass $m$ and the starting temperature θ₁.
2. Switch on for a measured time $t$, reading the voltmeter $V$ and ammeter $I$ (or knowing the power $P$).
3. Record the final temperature θ₂.

$$VIt = mc(\theta_2 - \theta_1) \Rightarrow c = \dfrac{VIt}{m(\theta_2 - \theta_1)}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 1.0 kg aluminium block is heated by a 12 V heater carrying 4.0 A for 5 minutes. Its temperature rises from 20 °C to 36 °C. Find its specific heat capacity.**

$$c = \dfrac{12 \times 4.0 \times 300}{1.0 \times 16} = \dfrac{14\,400}{16} = 900\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$$
:::

## Reducing heat losses (precautions)

- **Lag** the calorimeter (cotton wool, felt or a jacket) and place it on an insulating stand.
- Use a **lid** to reduce loss by convection and evaporation.
- Use a **polished** calorimeter to reduce loss by radiation.
- **Transfer the hot solid quickly** and avoid splashing.
- **Stir** gently so the temperature is uniform; read the **highest** temperature.
- Start with the cold water a few degrees **below** room temperature and finish a few degrees above, so gains and losses roughly cancel.

## Newton's law of cooling

:::callout{type=definition}
**Newton's law of cooling:** the **rate of heat loss** of a body is **directly proportional** to the **difference in temperature** between the body and its surroundings (for small temperature differences and under forced convection).
:::

A cup of tea cools fastest when it is hottest and more slowly as it approaches room temperature. The **cooling curve** (temperature against time) is steep at first and gradually flattens.

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Calorimetry**: the measurement of quantities of heat.
- **Lagging**: insulating a calorimeter with cotton wool, felt or a jacket to reduce heat loss.
- **Electrical method**: finding specific heat capacity by heating with an electric heater and using the energy $VIt$ supplied.
:::

## Key points

- Method of mixtures: heat lost by hot bodies = heat gained by cold bodies.
- Include the calorimeter: $m_sc_s(\theta_2 - \theta_3) = (m_wc_w + m_cc_c)(\theta_3 - \theta_1)$.
- Electrical method: $c = \dfrac{VIt}{m\Delta\theta}$.
- Reduce losses by lagging, a lid, a polished calorimeter and quick transfer.
- Newton's law of cooling: rate of cooling ∝ excess temperature.

## Quick check

1. State the principle of the method of mixtures.
2. 1 kg of water at 90 °C is mixed with 1 kg at 30 °C. What is the final temperature (no losses)?
3. Give two precautions for reducing heat loss from a calorimeter.

**Answers:** 1. Heat lost by the hot bodies equals heat gained by the cold bodies (no heat lost to the surroundings). 2. 60 °C. 3. For example: lag it; cover it with a lid; use a polished calorimeter; transfer the hot solid quickly.
