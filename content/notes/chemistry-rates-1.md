Some reactions are over in a flash, like a match igniting; others take years, like iron rusting. **Chemical kinetics** studies how fast reactions go and why. This lesson defines the rate of reaction, shows how to measure it in the laboratory, how to read rate graphs, and introduces the collision theory that explains everything about rates.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the rate of a reaction and state its units
- describe methods of measuring reaction rates
- calculate average rates from experimental data
- interpret graphs of product formed against time
- state the collision theory and explain activation energy

## What is the rate of a reaction?

:::callout{type=definition}
The **rate of a reaction** is the **change in the amount or concentration** of a reactant or product **per unit time**.

$$\text{rate} = \dfrac{\text{change in amount}}{\text{time taken}}$$

The amount may be a mass, a gas volume or a concentration.
:::

Typical units: **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**, **cm³ s⁻¹** (gas volume) or **g s⁻¹** (mass).

## Measuring rates

Choose a property that changes as the reaction proceeds.

| Method | Example reaction | What you measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume of gas** given off | Marble chips + hydrochloric acid; magnesium + acid | Volume of gas in a **gas syringe** (or collected over water) at regular times |
| **Loss of mass** | Marble chips + acid in a flask on a balance (cotton-wool plug lets CO₂ escape) | Mass of the flask at regular times |
| **Colour change** | Iodine reactions; KMnO₄ decolourising | Time for a colour to appear or disappear |
| **Precipitate formation** | Sodium thiosulphate + hydrochloric acid gives sulphur | Time for a cross under the flask to **disappear** |
| **Sampling and titration** | Hydrolysis of an ester | Concentration of acid at intervals |

For the **thiosulphate** reaction,

$$\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{SO}_2 + \text{S} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

the sulphur makes the solution cloudy. A shorter time for the cross to disappear means a **faster** reaction. The rate is taken as $\tfrac{1}{\text{time}}$.

## Rate graphs

![Volume of carbon(IV) oxide against time for marble chips with acid: the curve for more concentrated acid is steeper at the start but reaches the same final volume as the dilute acid curve](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/rate-curves.svg)

*Figure 1. The gradient of the curve is the rate. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- The **gradient (slope)** of the curve at any point is the **rate** at that time.
- The curve is **steepest at the start**, when the reactants are most concentrated, so the rate is **fastest at the start**.
- The curve becomes **less steep** as reactants are used up: the rate **decreases**.
- The curve becomes **flat** (horizontal) when the reaction **stops**, because one reactant (the **limiting reactant**) is used up.
- The **final volume** depends on the **amount of limiting reactant**, not on how fast it reacts.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Marble chips with hydrochloric acid.**

| Time (s) | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of CO₂ (cm³) | 0 | 28 | 46 | 56 | 60 | 60 |

1. Average rate in the first 30 s $= \dfrac{28}{30} = 0.93$ cm³/s.
2. Average rate from 30 s to 60 s $= \dfrac{46 - 28}{30} = 0.60$ cm³/s.
3. Average rate over the whole reaction (complete at 120 s) $= \dfrac{60}{120} = 0.50$ cm³/s.

The rate **falls** with time because the acid becomes less concentrated as it is used up.
:::

The **instantaneous rate** at a particular time is found by drawing a **tangent** to the curve at that point and calculating its gradient.

## Collision theory

:::callout{type=definition}
**Collision theory:** for a reaction to occur, reactant particles must **collide** with each other, with **enough energy** (at least the **activation energy**, Ea) and in the **correct orientation**.
:::

- Most collisions are **not successful**: the particles just bounce apart.
- Only collisions with energy **≥ Ea** break bonds and lead to products. These are **effective (successful) collisions**.
- The **rate** depends on the **number of effective collisions per second**.

Anything that increases the **frequency of collisions** or the **fraction of particles with energy ≥ Ea** increases the rate. The next lesson uses this idea to explain every factor that affects rate.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Market traders know reaction rates from experience. Fish **spoils quickly** in the heat because the decay reactions caused by bacteria and enzymes speed up at higher temperatures. Ice, refrigeration, smoking and drying all slow those reactions down. Cutting cassava or yam into small pieces makes it **cook faster**, because more surface is exposed to the hot water.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The rate is **not constant** during a reaction. It is fastest at the start and slows down as reactants are used up. A steeper curve means a faster rate, not a larger final amount of product.
:::

## Key points

- **Rate** = change in amount or concentration ÷ time (e.g. cm³/s, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹).
- Measure rates by **gas volume, mass loss, colour change, precipitate (disappearing cross)** or **titration**.
- On a graph of product against time, the **gradient is the rate**: fastest at the start, zero when the reaction stops.
- The **final amount** of product depends on the **limiting reactant**.
- **Collision theory:** particles must collide with energy **≥ activation energy** and the right orientation.

## Quick check

1. Define the rate of a reaction.
2. Why does the rate of reaction between marble and acid decrease with time?
3. 40 cm³ of gas is collected in the first 20 s. Find the average rate.
4. State two conditions for a collision to lead to a reaction.

**Answers:**

1. The change in the amount or concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.
2. The reactants are used up, so their concentration falls and there are fewer collisions per second.
3. $40 \div 20 = 2.0$ cm³/s.
4. The particles must collide with energy at least equal to the activation energy, and in the correct orientation.
