Pa Kamara owns family land near Kenema but has grown too old to farm all of it. A young farmer from the next village has strong hands but no land. They agree that the young man will clear and plant two acres of rice, and when the harvest is in, Pa Kamara will take one bag in every three. Neither pays the other any money, yet both gain. This arrangement is called **sharecropping**, and it is one of the oldest ways of getting land and labour together on a farm.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define sharecropping and name the two parties to it
- explain how a sharecropping agreement works and how the harvest is shared
- calculate each party's share of a harvest
- state the benefits of sharecropping to the landowner, the sharecropper and the nation
- state the problems of sharecropping and suggest ways of solving them

## What is sharecropping?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Sharecropping** is a system of land tenure in which a farmer cultivates land belonging to someone else and pays for its use with an agreed **share of the harvest**, not with money.
:::

There are always two parties:

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Landowner (landlord)**: the person, family or community that owns the land and allows another person to farm it.
- **Sharecropper**: the farmer who works the land and gives part of the crop to the landowner in return for using it.
:::

Sharecropping is a form of **tenancy**, but it differs from ordinary renting. A tenant who rents land pays a **fixed rent** in cash, whatever the size of the harvest. A sharecropper pays a **share** of whatever is actually harvested, so the landowner's income rises and falls with the harvest.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not confuse sharecropping with **renting**. In renting, the rent is fixed in money before the season starts. In sharecropping, the payment is a **portion of the crop**, so a poor harvest means a smaller payment and a good harvest a larger one.
:::

## How a sharecropping agreement works

Most agreements are made by word of mouth between families, often witnessed by elders or the town chief. A good agreement settles these questions before planting:

| Question | What is agreed |
|---|---|
| Which land? | The exact plot and its boundaries |
| Which crop? | Rice, groundnut, cassava, cocoa, coffee or vegetables |
| For how long? | One season, or several years for tree crops |
| Who provides the inputs? | Seed, fertiliser, tools and hired labour: one party or both |
| What share? | Often **one-third** or **one-half** of the harvest to the landowner |
| When is it shared? | At harvest, before the crop is sold or stored |

The share usually depends on who bears the costs. Where the landowner provides only the land, the sharecropper keeps the larger part. Where the landowner also provides seed, fertiliser or tools, the landowner's share is bigger.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Sharing a rice harvest.** A sharecropper harvests **90 bags** of rice. The agreement gives the landowner **one-third** of the harvest.

- Landowner's share: $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 90 = 30$ bags.
- Sharecropper's share: $90 - 30 = 60$ bags.

If instead the landowner had supplied the seed and fertiliser and the agreed share were **one-half**, each would receive $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 90 = 45$ bags.
:::

## Benefits of sharecropping

### To the landowner

1. Land that would lie idle is brought into use and produces an income.
2. The landowner gets a harvest without doing the hard work of farming.
3. Clearing and weeding keep the land from turning back into bush.
4. The landowner needs no cash to employ labourers.

### To the sharecropper

1. A farmer without land gets land to farm without paying rent in advance.
2. The risk is shared: if the harvest fails, the payment is small too.
3. Young farmers and settlers ("strangers") can start farming and save to buy or lease land later.
4. The sharecropper may learn from an experienced landowner who supplies inputs and advice.

### To the community and the nation

1. More land is cultivated, so **food production rises**.
2. It creates **employment** in rural areas and reduces drift to the towns.
3. It brings together **land** and **labour**, two factors of production that would otherwise be wasted.

:::callout{type=local-example}
In Sierra Leone most farmland in the provinces is family or community land held under customary tenure. Families with more land than workers, and young farmers or settlers with labour but no land, often come to sharecropping arrangements for upland and swamp rice, groundnut and vegetables, and for older cocoa and coffee plantations in the Eastern Province, where a caretaker farmer harvests the trees and shares the crop with the owner.
:::

## Problems of sharecropping

1. **Insecurity of tenure**: the sharecropper can be sent away at the end of the season, so has no reason to plant trees or improve the soil.
2. **Little incentive to invest**: any extra effort or fertiliser raises the landowner's share too.
3. **Disputes**: oral agreements lead to quarrels about boundaries, the size of the share or who paid for inputs.
4. **Exploitation**: a landowner may demand an unfair share, leaving the sharecropper poor.
5. **Soil exhaustion**: a farmer with short-term use of land may overuse it and not rest or fertilise it.
6. **Low output**: sharecroppers are often poor and cannot afford improved seed or fertiliser.

## How the problems can be reduced

- Write the agreement down and have it witnessed, stating the land, the crop, the share and the length of the agreement.
- Give longer agreements, especially for tree crops, so the sharecropper benefits from improving the land.
- Share the cost of inputs fairly, and set the share to match who bears the costs.
- Settle disputes through the chiefdom or local courts, or through farmers' associations.
- Extension officers can advise both parties on improved seed, fertiliser use and soil conservation.

## Key points

- **Sharecropping**: a sharecropper farms another person's land and pays with an agreed **share of the harvest**, often one-third or one-half.
- It differs from **renting**, where a fixed rent is paid in money.
- The share depends on who provides the inputs.
- Benefits: idle land is used, landless farmers get land, risk is shared, food output and employment rise.
- Problems: insecure tenure, little incentive to improve land, disputes, exploitation and soil exhaustion.
- Written, fair and longer agreements reduce the problems.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE asks you to **define sharecropping**, to **state its advantages and disadvantages**, and to **compare it with other tenure systems** such as leasehold, renting and freehold. Questions often say "State **four** advantages", so learn at least four benefits and four problems, and give one way of solving each problem.
:::

## Quick check

1. What does a sharecropper pay for the use of the land?
2. How is sharecropping different from renting land?
3. A harvest is 120 bags of groundnut and the landowner's share is one-quarter. How many bags does each party get?
4. Why does a sharecropper rarely plant trees on the land?

**Answers:** 1. An agreed share of the harvest. 2. Rent is a fixed amount of money paid whatever the harvest; sharecropping pays a share of the actual harvest. 3. Landowner: $\tfrac{1}{4} \times 120 = 30$ bags; sharecropper: $90$ bags. 4. Because the tenure is insecure: the sharecropper may be sent away before the trees bear fruit.
