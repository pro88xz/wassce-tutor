Sulphuric acid is so important to industry that the amount a country makes was once used as a measure of its industrial development. It goes into fertilisers, car batteries, detergents and paints. This lesson covers its manufacture by the Contact process, the very different properties of the concentrated and dilute acid, sulphates and hydrogen sulphide.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the Contact process and explain its conditions
- describe the dehydrating, oxidising and other properties of concentrated sulphuric acid
- describe the properties of dilute sulphuric acid
- state the uses of sulphuric acid
- describe the test for sulphate ions
- describe the preparation, test and properties of hydrogen sulphide

## The Contact process

![Contact process flow: sulphur burned to SO₂, purified and dried, oxidised to SO₃ over vanadium(V) oxide at 450 °C and 1–2 atm, absorbed in concentrated sulphuric acid to form oleum, then diluted](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/contact-process.svg)

*Figure 1. Manufacturing sulphuric acid by the Contact process. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Stage 1: making SO₂.** Burn **sulphur** in air (or roast sulphide ores such as iron pyrites):

$$\text{S(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$$

**Stage 2: purifying.** The gas is cleaned of **dust** and dried, because impurities (especially arsenic compounds) **poison the catalyst**.

**Stage 3: catalytic oxidation** in the converter:

$$2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3\text{(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -197 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

| Condition | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Catalyst | **Vanadium(V) oxide**, V₂O₅ | Speeds up the reaction; cheaper and less easily poisoned than platinum |
| Temperature | About **450 °C** | Compromise: lower temperature gives more SO₃ (exothermic) but is too slow |
| Pressure | **1–2 atm** | About 99% conversion is already achieved, so high pressure is not worth its cost |
| Excess air | Yes | Shifts the equilibrium to the right |

**Stage 4: absorption.** SO₃ is **absorbed in concentrated (98%) sulphuric acid**, forming **oleum** (fuming sulphuric acid):

$$\text{SO}_3\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7\text{(l)}$$

**Stage 5: dilution.** The oleum is diluted with the right amount of water:

$$\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7\text{(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)}$$

**Why not dissolve SO₃ directly in water?** The reaction is so exothermic that it forms a **fine mist** of acid droplets that is very hard to condense.

## Concentrated sulphuric acid

A dense, oily, colourless liquid (98%), **very corrosive**. Always **add the acid slowly to water**, stirring, never water to acid: so much heat is given out that water added to acid boils and spatters.

| Property | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Dehydrating agent:** removes water, or the elements of water, from compounds | **Sugar** turns into a black mass of carbon: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O. **Blue** hydrated CuSO₄ turns **white**. It chars paper, wood and skin |
| **Drying agent** | Dries most gases (Cl₂, SO₂, HCl, CO₂, O₂), but **not ammonia**, which it reacts with |
| **Oxidising agent** (hot, concentrated) | Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + 2H₂O + SO₂; C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O |
| **Involatile acid:** displaces more volatile acids from their salts | NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl; KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃ |

## Dilute sulphuric acid

A typical **strong, dibasic acid**:

- turns blue litmus red
- with **metals** above hydrogen: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
- with **bases**: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
- with **carbonates**: Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂
- forms **two series of salts**: normal sulphates (Na₂SO₄) and hydrogensulphates (NaHSO₄)

## Uses of sulphuric acid

| Use | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Fertilisers** | **Superphosphate** and **ammonium sulphate**: the largest use |
| **Car batteries** | Electrolyte in lead–acid accumulators |
| **Detergents** | Making soapless detergents |
| **Paints and pigments** | Making titanium(IV) oxide white pigment |
| **Fibres and plastics** | Rayon and some plastics |
| **Metal treatment** | Cleaning ("pickling") steel before galvanising or plating |
| **Laboratory** | Drying agent; making other acids |

## Sulphates and the test for sulphate ions

Most sulphates are soluble, except **barium sulphate** and **lead(II) sulphate** (calcium sulphate is slightly soluble).

**Test for sulphate ions:** add **dilute hydrochloric acid** (to remove carbonate and sulphite ions), then **barium chloride** solution. A **white precipitate** of barium sulphate shows a sulphate:

$$\text{Ba}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)}$$

## Hydrogen sulphide, H₂S

**Preparation:** dilute hydrochloric acid on **iron(II) sulphide**, in a fume cupboard:

$$\text{FeS(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{FeCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{S(g)}$$

**Test:** smell of **rotten eggs** (in tiny amounts), and it turns **lead(II) ethanoate (acetate) paper black** (lead sulphide).

**Properties:**

- **very poisonous**, colourless gas; denser than air
- weak acid in water
- burns with a blue flame
- **strong reducing agent**: it is oxidised to **yellow sulphur** by chlorine, SO₂, concentrated acids, KMnO₄ and iron(III) salts
- **precipitates metal sulphides**, many of them black (CuS, PbS), which is used in analysis

:::callout{type=local-example}
Every lead–acid **car battery** contains dilute sulphuric acid. In Sierra Leone, old batteries are sometimes broken open to recover the lead. Spilled acid burns skin and soil, and lead dust poisons workers and children nearby. Used batteries should go to proper recyclers, and acid on skin must be washed off at once with plenty of water.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Concentrated sulphuric acid **cannot dry ammonia**, because ammonia is a base and reacts with it. Use calcium oxide for ammonia.
:::

## Key points

- **Contact process:** S → SO₂ → (V₂O₅, 450 °C, 1–2 atm) SO₃ → oleum (absorbed in conc. H₂SO₄) → dilute to H₂SO₄.
- SO₃ is **not** dissolved directly in water: it forms an acid mist.
- **Concentrated H₂SO₄:** **dehydrating** (sugar → carbon), **drying** (not NH₃), **oxidising** (Cu → SO₂), **involatile** (makes HCl, HNO₃). **Add acid to water.**
- **Dilute H₂SO₄:** a strong dibasic acid.
- Uses: **fertilisers, batteries, detergents, pigments, pickling steel**.
- **Sulphate test:** dilute HCl, then BaCl₂: **white precipitate**. **H₂S:** rotten eggs; turns lead ethanoate paper black; a strong reducing agent.

## Quick check

1. Name the catalyst and temperature used in the Contact process.
2. What is seen when concentrated sulphuric acid is added to sugar? Name the property shown.
3. Why must acid be added to water and not the reverse?
4. Describe the test for sulphate ions.

**Answers:**

1. Vanadium(V) oxide; about 450 °C.
2. The sugar turns into a black, spongy mass of carbon, with steam: the acid is a dehydrating agent.
3. A great deal of heat is released; adding water to acid makes the water boil and spatter acid.
4. Add dilute HCl, then barium chloride solution: a white precipitate (BaSO₄) shows sulphate.
