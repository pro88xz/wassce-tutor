Alkanes were once called **paraffins**, from Latin words meaning "little affinity": they ignore acids, alkalis and most oxidising agents. But they burn, releasing a great deal of energy, and in sunlight they react with chlorine. This lesson covers the combustion and substitution reactions of alkanes and their uses.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why alkanes are relatively unreactive
- write equations for the complete and incomplete combustion of alkanes
- calculate volumes and masses in combustion reactions
- describe the substitution reaction of methane with chlorine and its mechanism
- state the uses of alkanes and of halogenoalkanes
- explain the environmental problems linked to alkanes

## Why alkanes are unreactive

- C–C and C–H bonds are **strong**.
- The bonds are **non-polar**, so reagents such as acids, alkalis and oxidising agents are not attracted to them.
- Alkanes are **saturated**: there is no double bond to add across.

Alkanes do **not** react with acids, alkalis, bromine water in the dark, or acidified potassium manganate(VII). This is a key way to tell them from alkenes.

## Combustion

**Complete combustion** (plenty of oxygen) gives **carbon(IV) oxide and water**, and much heat:

$$\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \qquad \Delta H = -890 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{(g)} + 5\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

**General equation:**

$$\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2} + \tfrac{3n+1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow n\text{CO}_2 + (n+1)\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Incomplete combustion** (limited oxygen) gives **carbon(II) oxide** (poisonous) and/or **soot** (carbon):

$$2\text{CH}_4 + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{C} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Small alkanes burn with a **clean blue flame**. Larger ones burn with a **more yellow, sooty flame**, because they have a higher proportion of carbon.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**10 cm³ of propane is burned completely in excess oxygen.** All volumes are measured at room temperature and pressure.

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

- Volume of oxygen used $= 5 \times 10 = 50$ cm³
- Volume of CO₂ formed $= 3 \times 10 = 30$ cm³
- Water is a liquid at room temperature, so it has almost no volume.

**What mass of CO₂ comes from burning 22 g of propane?** (C₃H₈ = 44, CO₂ = 44)

- Moles of propane $= 22 \div 44 = 0.5$ mol → CO₂ $= 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$ mol
- Mass $= 1.5 \times 44 = 66$ g
:::

## Substitution with halogens

In **sunlight (ultraviolet light)**, alkanes react with chlorine or bromine. **Hydrogen atoms are replaced, one at a time, by halogen atoms**: this is a **substitution reaction**.

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl} \qquad (\text{chloromethane})$$

$$\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 + \text{HCl} \qquad (\text{dichloromethane})$$

$$\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CHCl}_3 + \text{HCl} \qquad (\text{trichloromethane})$$

$$\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4 + \text{HCl} \qquad (\text{tetrachloromethane})$$

With bromine, the **orange-brown colour disappears** slowly in light. In the dark there is no reaction.

### How it happens: a free-radical chain

1. **Initiation:** UV light splits a chlorine molecule into two reactive **free radicals** (atoms with an unpaired electron): Cl₂ → 2Cl•
2. **Propagation:** each radical starts a chain that makes more radicals:
   - Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃•
   - CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
3. **Termination:** two radicals meet and the chain stops: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl; CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆

## Uses

| Alkane or product | Use |
|---|---|
| **Methane** (natural gas, biogas) | Fuel for cooking, power stations; making hydrogen for ammonia |
| **Propane and butane** (LPG) | **Cooking gas** cylinders; lighters; camping stoves |
| **Petrol** (C₅–C₁₀) | Fuel for cars and motorbikes |
| **Kerosene** (C₁₀–C₁₆) | Lamps, stoves, jet fuel |
| **Diesel** (C₁₅–C₂₀) | Lorries, generators, buses |
| **Lubricating oil, paraffin wax** | Lubricants; candles, polishes |
| **Trichloromethane** (chloroform) | Solvent (formerly an anaesthetic) |
| **Tetrachloromethane** | Solvent (now restricted, as it is toxic) |

**Environmental problems:**

- burning alkanes releases **CO₂** (global warming)
- incomplete burning gives **CO and soot**
- methane itself is a powerful **greenhouse gas**
- **CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons, once used in fridges and aerosols) damage the **ozone layer**

:::callout{type=local-example}
**LPG (cooking gas)** is increasingly used in Freetown homes instead of charcoal. Propane and butane burn cleanly with a blue flame when the burner gets enough air. A **yellow, smoky flame** means incomplete combustion, producing poisonous carbon(II) oxide, so the burner's air holes should be cleaned and the kitchen kept ventilated.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Alkanes undergo **substitution**, not addition. They have no double bond, so a halogen atom can only **replace** a hydrogen atom, and HCl or HBr is always a second product.
:::

## Key points

- Alkanes are **unreactive** (strong, non-polar bonds; saturated): no reaction with acids, alkalis, KMnO₄ or bromine in the dark.
- **Complete combustion** → CO₂ + H₂O; **incomplete** → CO and soot.
- In **UV light**, halogens **substitute** H atoms one by one (CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄) by a **free-radical chain** (initiation, propagation, termination).
- Uses: **fuels** (natural gas, LPG, petrol, kerosene, diesel), lubricants, wax, solvents.
- Problems: **CO₂, CO, soot and methane** emissions; CFCs harm the ozone layer.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **write balanced combustion equations** and do **gas-volume calculations**, to **describe the substitution of methane by chlorine** (conditions, products and mechanism), and to **state uses of alkanes**. Questions comparing alkanes and alkenes with bromine water are very common.
:::

## Quick check

1. Write the equation for the complete combustion of ethane.
2. What condition is needed for methane to react with chlorine?
3. Name the type of reaction between methane and chlorine and give the first organic product.
4. What volume of oxygen is needed to burn 20 cm³ of methane completely?

**Answers:**

1. 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O.
2. Sunlight (ultraviolet light).
3. Substitution; chloromethane, CH₃Cl.
4. 40 cm³ (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O).
