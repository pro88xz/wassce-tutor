Shine bright red light on a zinc plate for hours and nothing happens. Shine a weak ultraviolet lamp on it and electrons fly off at once. The wave theory of light could not explain this. Einstein could — by treating light as a stream of energy packets called photons.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the photoelectric effect and its experimental observations
- explain why the wave theory fails and how the photon theory succeeds
- use Einstein's equation $hf = W + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2_{max}$
- define work function, threshold frequency and stopping potential

## The photoelectric effect

:::callout{type=definition}
The **photoelectric effect** is the **emission of electrons from a metal surface** when electromagnetic radiation of **sufficiently high frequency** falls on it. The emitted electrons are called **photoelectrons**.
:::

**Demonstration:** a clean zinc plate on a negatively charged gold-leaf electroscope. Ultraviolet light makes the leaf **collapse** (electrons are emitted and the charge leaks away). Visible light — however bright — has **no effect**. A sheet of glass (which absorbs UV) placed in the beam stops the effect.

## The observations

1. Electrons are emitted **only if the frequency** of the light is **above a threshold frequency** $f_0$, which depends on the metal. Below $f_0$ there is **no emission**, however intense the light.
2. Emission is **instantaneous**: there is no delay, even with very dim light.
3. The **maximum kinetic energy** of the photoelectrons depends on the **frequency**, not the intensity; it increases linearly with frequency.
4. The **number** of electrons emitted per second (the photocurrent) is proportional to the **intensity** of the light.

The **wave theory** predicts that brighter light (bigger waves) should give electrons more energy, and that dim light should take time to build up enough energy — both **wrong**.

## The photon explanation

Einstein proposed that light consists of packets of energy called **photons** (quanta), each with energy

$$E = hf$$

where $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J s is **Planck's constant**.

- Each photon gives **all its energy to one electron**.
- The electron needs a minimum energy, the **work function** $W$ (or φ), to escape from the metal.
- Any energy left over becomes the electron's **kinetic energy**.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Einstein's photoelectric equation:**

$$hf = W + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2_{max}$$

- The **work function** $W$ is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of a metal.
- The **threshold frequency** $f_0$ is the minimum frequency that causes emission: $W = hf_0$.
:::

This explains every observation: a photon with $f < f_0$ cannot free an electron no matter how many photons arrive (intensity); one photon, one electron, so emission is instant; higher frequency means more energy left over; brighter light means more photons, so more electrons.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The work function of sodium is 2.3 eV. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on it. Find (a) the photon energy in eV, (b) the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, (c) the threshold frequency. (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)**

(a) $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{400 \times 10^{-9}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J $= 3.11$ eV.

(b) $E_{k,max} = 3.11 - 2.3 = 0.81$ eV.

(c) $f_0 = \dfrac{W}{h} = \dfrac{2.3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} = 5.55 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.
:::

## Stopping potential

If a reverse p.d. is applied to repel the photoelectrons, the current falls to zero at the **stopping potential** $V_s$, where

$$eV_s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2_{max}$$

So $V_s$ measures the maximum kinetic energy directly (in electronvolts it is numerically equal: a stopping potential of 0.81 V means a maximum kinetic energy of 0.81 eV).

## Key points

- Photoelectric effect: emission of electrons by radiation above a threshold frequency.
- Photon energy $E = hf$; one photon frees one electron.
- $hf = W + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2_{max}$; $W = hf_0$.
- Intensity affects the **number** of electrons; frequency affects their **energy**.
- $E_k$–$f$ graph: gradient $h$, intercept $f_0$.

## Quick check

1. What happens to the number of photoelectrons if the light intensity is doubled (frequency unchanged)?
2. A metal has a work function of 3.0 eV. Will photons of energy 2.5 eV cause emission?
3. What does the gradient of the $E_k$ against $f$ graph give?

**Answers:** 1. It doubles (their maximum kinetic energy is unchanged). 2. No: each photon has less energy than the work function. 3. Planck's constant, $h$.
