A **mountain** is a large, steep-sided area of land rising high above its surroundings, usually more than about 600 m, with a peak or ridge at the top. There are four main **types of mountains**: **fold mountains**, formed when rock layers are squeezed and crumpled (the Atlas, Alps and Himalayas); **block mountains**, raised between faults (the Ruwenzori); **volcanic mountains**, built up by eruptions (Mount Cameroon and Kilimanjaro); and **residual mountains**, the worn-down remains of older, higher land. This lesson explains how each type forms and why mountains matter.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a mountain and distinguish it from a hill
- explain the formation of fold mountains, with examples
- explain faulting and the formation of block mountains and rift valleys
- describe volcanic and residual mountains
- state the importance of mountains to people

## Fold mountains

![Fold mountains crumpled by compression, a block mountain (horst) raised between two faults, and a volcanic mountain built of lava and ash](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/mountain-types.svg)

*Figure 1. How fold, block and volcanic mountains form. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Formation:**

1. Layers of sediment build up in a sea or a shallow basin and harden into sedimentary rock.
2. Two plates **move towards each other** (compression).
3. The rock layers are squeezed and **buckle into folds**: upfolds are **anticlines** and downfolds are **synclines**.
4. Intense pressure can push folds over (**overfolds**) or break and thrust them over one another (**nappes**).
5. The folded rock is pushed up to great heights, forming long ranges of **fold mountains**.

**Examples:** the **Atlas Mountains** (north-west Africa), the **Cape Ranges** (South Africa), the **Alps**, the **Himalayas** (with Mount Everest, 8,849 m), the **Andes** and the **Rockies**. Young fold mountains are high and jagged; old ones, such as the Cape Ranges, are lower and rounded by erosion.

## Faulting: block mountains and rift valleys

A **fault** is a crack in the Earth's crust along which rocks have moved.

- **Tension** (pulling apart) or **compression** (pushing together) causes faults.
- A **block mountain (horst)** forms when a block of land between two roughly parallel faults is **raised**, or when the land on either side sinks. It has steep sides and a flat or gently sloping top.
- A **rift valley (graben)** forms when a block of land between two parallel faults **sinks**, leaving a long, flat-floored valley with steep walls (escarpments).

**Examples:** the **Ruwenzori** (between Uganda and DR Congo) and the **Vosges** and **Black Forest** (Europe) are block mountains. The **East African Rift Valley**, holding Lakes Tanganyika and Malawi, is the world's best-known rift valley.

## Volcanic mountains

**Volcanic mountains** are built up from layers of **lava and ash** from repeated eruptions around a vent. They are often cone-shaped with a crater at the top.

**Examples:** **Kilimanjaro** (5,895 m, Africa's highest mountain), **Mount Cameroon** (about 4,040 m, West Africa's highest), and **Mount Kenya**.

## Residual mountains

**Residual (relict) mountains** are the remains of older, higher land that has been worn down by weathering and erosion over very long periods. The harder rocks are left standing while softer rocks around them are removed.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's highest land is in the north-east, in the **Loma Mountains**, where **Mount Bintumani (Loma Mansa)**, at about **1,945 m**, is the highest point in Sierra Leone. The **Tingi Hills** rise nearby. These are ancient uplands of very old, hard rocks shaped by long erosion. Nearer the coast, the steep **Freetown Peninsula mountains**, rising to over 800 m, are formed of a large igneous intrusion exposed by erosion.
:::

## Importance of mountains

| Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|
| **sources of rivers** for water and hydroelectric power | steep slopes make farming, roads and building difficult |
| **cooler climates** at altitude, good for tea, coffee and settlement | soil erosion and landslides on slopes |
| **minerals** exposed by folding, faulting and erosion | barriers to communication and transport |
| **forests** for timber and wildlife (Loma, Gola, Western Area Peninsula forests) | thin, poor soils at high altitude |
| **tourism**: climbing, scenery, wildlife | hazards: volcanic eruptions, earthquakes |
| **relief rainfall** on windward slopes | rain shadow (dry) on leeward slopes |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **rift valley** is not cut by a river. It is formed by **faulting**: land sinks between two faults. Rivers and lakes may later occupy it.
:::

## Key points

- **Fold mountains:** compression folds rock layers (anticlines, synclines): Atlas, Alps, Himalayas, Andes.
- **Block mountains (horsts):** raised between faults: Ruwenzori, Vosges. **Rift valleys (grabens):** sunk between faults: East African Rift.
- **Volcanic mountains:** layers of lava and ash: Kilimanjaro, Mount Cameroon.
- **Residual mountains:** remains of older, eroded land.
- Sierra Leone's highest point: **Bintumani**, about **1,945 m**, in the **Loma Mountains**.

## Quick check

1. What causes fold mountains to form?
2. What is the difference between an anticline and a syncline?
3. How does a rift valley form?
4. Name Africa's highest mountain and state its type.
5. Give two benefits of mountains.

**Answers:**

1. Compression, when plates move towards each other and squeeze rock layers into folds.
2. An anticline is an upfold; a syncline is a downfold.
3. A block of land sinks between two parallel faults.
4. Kilimanjaro, a volcanic mountain.
5. Any two: sources of rivers; cool climates; minerals; forests; tourism; relief rainfall.
