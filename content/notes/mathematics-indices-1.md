When you write $2^3$, you don't mean *two times three*. You mean *two multiplied by itself three times*: $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$. The small $3$ above the $2$ is the **index** (plural: **indices**), and it tells you how many times to multiply the **base** by itself.

Indices are everywhere in mathematics — in algebra, in scientific notation, in compound interest, in geometric sequences. WAEC tests indices directly in every paper, both in objective and theory sections. Without solid foundations in this topic, indicial equations, logarithms, and surds will all feel impossible.

This lesson defines the basic terms, shows how to read and write powers, computes simple powers by hand, and introduces the special cases ($0^1$, $1^n$, etc.) that will return throughout the topic.

:::callout{type=definition}
In the expression $a^n$:

- $a$ is called the **base** — the number being multiplied.
- $n$ is called the **index** (or **exponent**, or **power**) — the number of times the base appears in the product.
- $a^n$ is read "$a$ to the power of $n$" or "$a$ raised to the $n$."

When $n$ is a positive whole number, $a^n$ means $a$ multiplied by itself $n$ times:
$$a^n = \underbrace{a \times a \times a \times \cdots \times a}_{n \text{ times}}$$
:::


## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- recognise powers in patterns and sequences

## Reading and writing powers

- $2^3$ = "two to the power of three" or "two cubed" = $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$
- $5^2$ = "five squared" = $5 \times 5 = 25$
- $10^4$ = "ten to the fourth" = $10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10{,}000$
- $3^1$ = "three to the power of one" = $3$
- $7^5$ = "seven to the fifth" = $7 \times 7 \times 7 \times 7 \times 7 = 16{,}807$

The second-power case ($a^2$) is so common it has its own name: **squared**. The third-power case ($a^3$) is called **cubed**. From $4^4$ onwards, just say *"to the power of n"*.

## Computing simple powers

For most WAEC questions, you compute powers by direct multiplication.

**Example.** Find $4^3$.

$4^3 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 16 \times 4 = 64$.

**Example.** Find $5^4$.

$5^4 = 5 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5 = 25 \times 25 = 625$.

**Example.** Find $6^3$.

$6^3 = 6 \times 6 \times 6 = 36 \times 6 = 216$.

### Step-by-step for larger powers

For $2^{10}$, multiply step by step:

$2^1 = 2$ 
$2^2 = 4$ 
$2^3 = 8$ 
$2^4 = 16$ 
$2^5 = 32$ 
$2^6 = 64$ 
$2^7 = 128$ 
$2^8 = 256$ 
$2^9 = 512$ 
$2^{10} = 1024$

For each step, just multiply the previous result by $2$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Compute each.**

**(a)** $3^4 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 9 \times 9 = 81$.

**(b)** $7^3 = 7 \times 7 \times 7 = 49 \times 7 = 343$.

**(c)** $2^7 = 128$ (memorised).

**(d)** $4^5 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 16 \times 16 \times 4 = 256 \times 4 = 1024$.

**(e)** $5^3 = 125$ (memorised).

**(f)** $11^2 = 11 \times 11 = 121$.

**(g)** $12^2 = 144$.

**(h)** $15^2 = 225$.

**(i)** $2^5 \times 3^2 = 32 \times 9 = 288$.

**(j)** $10^3 \times 5^2 = 1000 \times 25 = 25{,}000$.
:::

## Negative bases — careful with signs

When the base is negative, the result depends on whether the exponent is **even** or **odd**.

**Even exponent:** result is positive.
- $(-2)^2 = (-2) \times (-2) = 4$ ✓
- $(-3)^4 = (-3)^2 \times (-3)^2 = 9 \times 9 = 81$ ✓
- $(-5)^2 = 25$ ✓

**Odd exponent:** result is negative.
- $(-2)^3 = (-2) \times (-2) \times (-2) = 4 \times (-2) = -8$ ✓
- $(-3)^3 = -27$ ✓
- $(-5)^3 = -125$ ✓

The rule: in a product, count the number of negative factors. If even, the product is positive. If odd, the product is negative.

### Sign convention — the minus sign before the base

There is a critical difference between $-2^2$ and $(-2)^2$.

- $(-2)^2 = (-2) \times (-2) = 4$ — the parentheses tell you to square the **negative** number first.
- $-2^2 = -(2^2) = -(4) = -4$ — without parentheses, the minus is **outside** the exponent. You compute the power first, then apply the negative.

The convention: **the exponent applies only to the immediately preceding number or grouping**. A minus sign without parentheses is a separate operation applied *after* the power.

- $-3^2 = -(3 \times 3) = -9$
- $(-3)^2 = (-3) \times (-3) = 9$
- $-2^4 = -(16) = -16$
- $(-2)^4 = 16$

This is one of the most common WAEC traps. Read every minus carefully.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Compute, watching the signs.**

**(a)** $(-3)^2 = 9$.

**(b)** $-3^2 = -9$.

**(c)** $(-2)^4 = 16$.

**(d)** $-2^4 = -16$.

**(e)** $(-4)^3 = -64$.

**(f)** $-4^3 = -64$. (Both forms agree here because $4^3 = 64$ is positive, and applying minus gives $-64$.)

**(g)** $(-1)^{100} = 1$ (even).

**(h)** $(-1)^{101} = -1$ (odd).
:::

## Bases that are fractions

When the base is a fraction, the index applies to the *whole* fraction.

$$\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$$

**Examples:**

- $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \dfrac{1^3}{2^3} = \dfrac{1}{8}$
- $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{4}{9}$
- $\left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right)^3 = \dfrac{27}{64}$
- $\left(\dfrac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{25}{4}$

Notice how the value gets smaller when the fraction is less than $1$, and larger when greater than $1$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Compute each.**

**(a)** $\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{1}{9}$.

**(b)** $\left(\dfrac{2}{5}\right)^3 = \dfrac{8}{125}$.

**(c)** $\left(\dfrac{3}{2}\right)^4 = \dfrac{81}{16}$.

**(d)** $\left(\dfrac{1}{10}\right)^3 = \dfrac{1}{1000}$.

**(e)** $\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = -\dfrac{1}{8}$ (odd exponent → negative).
:::

## Bases of $0$ and $1$

Two special bases.

### Base of $0$

$0^n = 0$ for any positive $n$.

- $0^1 = 0$
- $0^2 = 0$
- $0^5 = 0$

*Note:* $0^0$ is a special case — sometimes defined as $1$, sometimes undefined. WAEC does not typically test this corner case.

### Base of $1$

$1^n = 1$ for any $n$.

- $1^1 = 1$
- $1^2 = 1$
- $1^{100} = 1$
- $1^{-5} = 1$

If you ever see $1$ to a power, the answer is $1$ — no matter the exponent.

## Why indices matter

Indices appear everywhere:

### Scientific notation

- $34{,}000 = 3.4 \times 10^4$
- $0.0056 = 5.6 \times 10^{-3}$

### Areas and volumes

- Area of a square with side $s$: $s^2$
- Volume of a cube with side $s$: $s^3$
- Volume of a sphere: $\dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$

### Growth and decay

- Population after $t$ years: $P_0 \times r^t$ (where $r$ is the growth rate)
- Compound interest: $P(1 + r)^n$

### Geometric sequences

- $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, \ldots$ — each term is the previous one times $2$. The $n$-th term is $2^{n-1}$.
- $1, 3, 9, 27, 81, \ldots$ — $n$-th term is $3^{n-1}$.

Later topics (sequences, financial arithmetic, variation) all rely on a solid understanding of indices.

## Comparing values raised to powers

Which is bigger, $3^4$ or $4^3$? Don't guess — compute.

$3^4 = 81$. $4^3 = 64$. So $3^4 > 4^3$.

Which is bigger, $2^{10}$ or $10^2$? $2^{10} = 1024$, $10^2 = 100$. So $2^{10} \gg 10^2$.

Which is bigger, $\dfrac{1}{2^3}$ or $\dfrac{1}{3^2}$? Compute: $\dfrac{1}{8}$ vs $\dfrac{1}{9}$. Since $\dfrac{1}{8} > \dfrac{1}{9}$, the first is bigger.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Compare each pair.**

**(a)** $5^3$ vs $3^5$. 
$5^3 = 125$. $3^5 = 243$. So $5^3 < 3^5$.

**(b)** $4^2$ vs $2^4$. 
$4^2 = 16$, $2^4 = 16$. Equal.

**(c)** $6^2$ vs $2^6$. 
$6^2 = 36$, $2^6 = 64$. So $6^2 < 2^6$.

**(d)** $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3$ vs $\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2$. 
First: $\dfrac{1}{8}$. Second: $\dfrac{1}{9}$. So $\dfrac{1}{8} > \dfrac{1}{9}$.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
A mosquito population in a stagnant pond in Bo doubles every day. On day $1$, there are $50$ mosquitoes. By day $10$, the population is $50 \times 2^9 = 50 \times 512 = 25{,}600$. By day $20$, it is $50 \times 2^{19}$, which is a much bigger number.

Indices capture this exponential growth precisely. Each day's population is the previous day's population times $2$ — the same operation repeated. Without indices, we'd have to multiply by $2$ ten times explicitly. With indices, $2^9$ does it in one symbol.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Common errors with simple powers:**

**1. Multiplying instead of using powers.**
- $2^3 \neq 6$. $2 \times 3 = 6$ is *multiplication*. $2^3 = 8$ is the *power*.

**2. Sign error with negative bases.**
- $(-3)^2 = 9$ (square cancels the negative).
- $-3^2 = -9$ (the minus is outside, applied after).

**3. Forgetting the parentheses.**
- $-2^4 \neq (-2)^4$. The first is $-16$. The second is $16$.

**4. Wrong order with fractions.**
- $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \dfrac{8}{27}$, NOT $\dfrac{2}{27}$.

**5. Confusing $a^n$ with $a \times n$.**
- $5^3 = 125$. $5 \times 3 = 15$. Very different.

**6. Adding indices instead of multiplying base.**
- $2^3 + 2^3 = 8 + 8 = 16$, NOT $2^6 = 64$. The rule for adding *same powers of the same base* is different from multiplying them (see the laws in the next lesson).
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Index** (power, exponent): the small raised number that tells how many times the base is multiplied by itself.
- **Base**: the number that is raised to a power, such as 3 in $3^4$.
:::

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Foundational power questions in WAEC:

- **Compute a power.** *Find the value of $3^4$.* (Answer: $81$)
- **Compute with negative base.** *Find the value of $(-2)^3$.* (Answer: $-8$)
- **Distinguish sign cases.** *Find the value of $-5^2$ vs $(-5)^2$.* ($-25$ vs $25$)
- **Compare powers.** *Which is greater: $5^3$ or $3^5$?*
- **Power of a fraction.** *Find $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^3$.*

The key foundational skill is the **sign convention** and the **memorisation of common powers**. These build the platform for everything that follows.
:::
