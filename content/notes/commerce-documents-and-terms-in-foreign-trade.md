# Documents and terms in foreign trade: shipping, origin and payment

## Learning objectives
Identify common foreign-trade documents, explain their purposes and distinguish FOB from CIF.

## Why documents matter
International trade involves parties in different countries, transport providers, banks, insurers and customs authorities. Documents provide evidence of the sale, goods, shipment, origin, insurance and payment instructions. Inconsistent documents can delay customs clearance or prevent payment under a documentary credit.

## Common documents

| Document | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Records seller, buyer, goods, quantities, prices and sale details |
| Packing list | Describes packages, contents, weights and dimensions |
| Bill of lading | Evidence of receipt and shipment by a sea carrier; many forms are documents of title |
| Air waybill | Evidence of an air-carriage contract and receipt; not normally a negotiable document of title |
| Certificate of origin | Identifies the country of origin under applicable rules |
| Insurance certificate | Evidence of insurance cover for specified risks |
| Customs declaration | Supplies information required for customs assessment and clearance |
| Letter of credit | A bank undertaking to pay when documents comply with the credit's terms |

A letter of credit is a documentary payment arrangement. Banks examine documents rather than physically inspecting the goods. The seller must present documents that comply with the credit conditions.

## FOB and CIF
**FOB (Free On Board)** is an Incoterms rule for sea and inland-waterway transport. The seller delivers the goods on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment. Risk transfers when the goods are on board; the buyer arranges the main carriage and insurance if desired.

**CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)** is also for sea and inland-waterway transport. The seller pays the cost and freight to the named destination port and obtains the required insurance, but risk transfers to the buyer when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Paying freight to destination does not mean the seller carries transit risk until arrival.

The chosen rule and named port must be stated in the contract. Incoterms allocate specified delivery responsibilities, costs and risks; they do not replace the full sales agreement.

## Practical example
A Sierra Leone importer buying machinery should check that the invoice, packing list, transport document, origin evidence and required insurance documents agree with the order. Under a letter of credit, even a discrepancy in names, quantities or dates may delay payment.

## WASSCE examination focus
An invoice records the sale and amount due; a bill of lading concerns carriage and shipment. Remember that FOB and CIF are sea and inland-waterway rules under Incoterms.
