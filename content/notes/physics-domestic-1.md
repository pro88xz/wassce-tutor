Every year, faulty wiring, overloaded sockets and illegal connections cause fires and electric shocks in homes and markets. Knowing how a house is wired — and what each safety device does — can save a life.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe how electricity reaches and is distributed in a house
- explain the functions of the live, neutral and earth wires
- wire a three-pin plug correctly
- explain the functions of fuses, circuit breakers, switches and earthing

## From the grid to the house

Electricity reaches a house through two **service cables** from a pole transformer: the **live** and the **neutral**. Inside, they pass through:

1. the **supplier's main fuse** (sealed)
2. the **electricity meter** (EDSA prepaid meter), which measures the energy used in kWh
3. the **consumer unit** (distribution board), with a **main switch** and separate **fuses or circuit breakers** for each circuit: lighting, sockets, cooker, water heater

All appliances are connected **in parallel**, so each gets the full 230 V and can be switched on and off independently.

## Live, neutral and earth

| Wire | Colour (modern) | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Live (L)** | **brown** (older: red) | carries the current to the appliance at about 230 V (alternating); **dangerous** |
| **Neutral (N)** | **blue** (older: black) | completes the circuit back to the supply; kept close to 0 V at the substation |
| **Earth (E)** | **green and yellow** (older: green) | a safety wire connected to the metal case of the appliance and to the ground |

**Switches and fuses are always placed in the live wire.** Then, when the switch is off or the fuse has blown, the appliance is disconnected from the high voltage. A switch in the neutral would leave the appliance "live" even when switched off.

## The three-pin plug

![Inside a three-pin plug: the brown live wire goes through the fuse to the right-hand pin, the blue neutral wire to the left-hand pin and the green and yellow earth wire to the top pin, with a cord grip holding the cable](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/three-pin-plug.svg)

*Figure 1. Wiring a three-pin plug. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Looking into the back of the plug with the cover off and the cable at the bottom:

- **Brown (live)** → **right-hand** pin, **through the fuse**
- **Blue (neutral)** → **left-hand** pin
- **Green/yellow (earth)** → **top** (longest) pin
- The **cord grip** clamps the outer sheath of the cable firmly, so the wires cannot be pulled out of their terminals.

The earth pin is longest so that it connects **first** and disconnects **last**.

## Fuses

:::callout{type=definition}
A **fuse** is a short, thin piece of wire with a low melting point, placed in the **live** wire. If the current exceeds its rating, the wire **melts** and breaks the circuit, protecting the cable from overheating and causing a fire.
:::

### Choosing a fuse

Calculate the normal working current, $I = \dfrac{P}{V}$, then choose the **next standard rating above** it (common ratings: 3 A, 5 A, 13 A).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Which fuse (3 A, 5 A or 13 A) should be fitted to the plug of (a) a 920 W iron, (b) a 60 W lamp, on a 230 V supply?**

(a) $I = \dfrac{920}{230} = 4.0$ A → **5 A** fuse.

(b) $I = \dfrac{60}{230} = 0.26$ A → **3 A** fuse.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A fuse with **too high** a rating (or a piece of ordinary wire or a nail used instead) will **not** melt when a fault occurs, so the cable can overheat and start a fire. Always use the correct rating, and never "bridge" a blown fuse.
:::

## Circuit breakers

A **circuit breaker** is an automatic switch that **trips** (opens) when the current is too large — usually by the magnetic pull of an electromagnet or the bending of a bimetallic strip. Advantages over fuses: it acts **faster**, and it can simply be **reset** after the fault is cleared.

A **residual current device (RCD)** compares the currents in the live and neutral wires and cuts off the supply in milliseconds if they differ (because current is leaking — perhaps through a person — to earth). It gives excellent protection against electric shock.

## Earthing

If a fault makes the **live wire touch the metal case** of an appliance (such as a kettle or a fridge), anyone touching it could get a severe shock. With an **earth wire** attached to the case, a **large current** flows straight to earth through the low-resistance earth wire. This **blows the fuse** (or trips the breaker) at once, disconnecting the supply and making the appliance safe.

**Double-insulated** appliances (with plastic cases, marked ⧈) need no earth wire.

## Key points

- Appliances are connected in parallel; the meter records energy in kWh.
- Live = brown, neutral = blue, earth = green/yellow; switches and fuses go in the live.
- Plug: brown → right pin via fuse, blue → left, green/yellow → top; cord grip on the sheath.
- Fuse rating: just above the working current $P/V$.
- Earthing + fuse: a live-to-case fault blows the fuse and protects the user.

## Quick check

1. What colour is the live wire in modern wiring?
2. Why are switches placed in the live wire?
3. A kettle is rated 2.3 kW, 230 V. Which fuse — 3 A, 5 A or 13 A — should it have?

**Answers:** 1. Brown. 2. So that when the switch is off, the appliance is disconnected from the high voltage and is safe. 3. $I = 2300/230 = 10$ A, so a 13 A fuse.
