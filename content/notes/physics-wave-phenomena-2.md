When two waves meet, they add together. Sometimes they reinforce each other, sometimes they cancel out. This "superposition" produces interference patterns, stationary waves and the sound of every musical instrument.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the principle of superposition
- explain constructive and destructive interference and the conditions for a steady pattern
- explain how stationary waves form and identify nodes and antinodes
- explain polarisation and why it shows that a wave is transverse

## The principle of superposition

:::callout{type=definition}
**Principle of superposition:** when two or more waves meet at a point, the **resultant displacement** is the **algebraic (vector) sum** of the individual displacements at that point.
:::

After they pass through each other, the waves continue unchanged.

## Interference

:::callout{type=definition}
**Interference** is the effect produced when two waves of the **same frequency** overlap, giving regions of **reinforcement** and regions of **cancellation**.
:::

- **Constructive interference**: crest meets crest (waves **in phase**). The amplitudes add, giving a large disturbance. This happens where the **path difference** is a whole number of wavelengths: $0, \lambda, 2\lambda, \ldots$
- **Destructive interference**: crest meets trough (waves in **antiphase**). The waves cancel. This happens where the path difference is an odd number of half wavelengths: $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda, \tfrac{3}{2}\lambda, \ldots$

### Conditions for a steady interference pattern

The two sources must be **coherent**: they must have the **same frequency** and a **constant phase difference**. For a clear pattern their amplitudes should also be similar.

**In a ripple tank**, two dippers on the same vibrating bar produce circular waves that overlap. Lines of calm water (destructive interference) radiate between lines of large waves (constructive).

**With sound**, two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator give alternate loud and quiet regions as you walk across in front of them.

**With light**, Young's double-slit experiment produces alternate bright and dark fringes — evidence that light is a wave.

## Stationary (standing) waves

![A string fixed at both ends vibrating in one loop (the fundamental) and in two loops (the second harmonic), with nodes N and antinodes A marked](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/stationary-waves-string.svg)

*Figure 1. Stationary waves on a string. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

When a wave is reflected back along its own path, the incident and reflected waves (same frequency, same amplitude, opposite directions) superpose to form a **stationary wave**.

- **Nodes (N)**: points of **zero displacement**, where the two waves always cancel.
- **Antinodes (A)**: points of **maximum displacement**, midway between nodes.
- Distance between adjacent nodes (or antinodes) $= \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$; from a node to the next antinode $= \dfrac{\lambda}{4}$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A stationary wave on a string has nodes 25 cm apart. The frequency is 120 Hz. Find the speed of the progressive waves that formed it.**

$\dfrac{\lambda}{2} = 25$ cm, so $\lambda = 50$ cm $= 0.50$ m.

$$v = f\lambda = 120 \times 0.50 = 60\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$
:::

## Polarisation

:::callout{type=definition}
A wave is **plane-polarised** when its vibrations are confined to **one plane** (one direction perpendicular to its travel).
:::

Only **transverse** waves can be polarised: in a longitudinal wave the vibrations are already along the direction of travel, so there is nothing to restrict.

**Demonstration with a rope:** pass a rope through a vertical slit in a fence. Vertical vibrations pass through; horizontal ones are stopped.

**With light:** ordinary light vibrates in all planes perpendicular to its travel. A **Polaroid** filter lets through only one plane. A second Polaroid rotated through 90° to the first blocks the light completely. This proves **light is a transverse wave**. Sound cannot be polarised, because it is longitudinal.

**Uses:** Polaroid sunglasses cut glare from water and roads (reflected light is partly polarised); polarised screens in phones and calculators (LCDs); stress analysis of plastics.

## Summary of wave behaviour

| Phenomenon | Shown by | Transverse? | Longitudinal? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reflection | all waves | yes | yes |
| Refraction | all waves | yes | yes |
| Diffraction | all waves | yes | yes |
| Interference | all waves | yes | yes |
| Polarisation | transverse waves only | yes | **no** |

## Key points

- Superposition: displacements add where waves meet.
- Constructive interference: path difference $n\lambda$; destructive: $(n + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambda$. Sources must be coherent.
- Stationary waves: nodes and antinodes; node-to-node $= \lambda/2$.
- Only transverse waves can be polarised; polarisation of light proves it is transverse.
