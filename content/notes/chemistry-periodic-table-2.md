Why is caesium more reactive than sodium, and fluorine more reactive than chlorine? Because atomic properties change in regular patterns across periods and down groups. Learn the two driving forces, nuclear charge and number of shells, and you can explain every trend WASSCE asks about.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the trends in atomic radius and ionic radius
- define ionisation energy and explain its trends, including the dips at boron and oxygen
- use successive ionisation energies to find an element's group
- define electron affinity and electronegativity and state their trends
- explain trends in metallic character and in the reactivity of groups 1 and 7

## Two driving forces

- **Across a period** (left to right), the **nuclear charge increases** (one more proton each step) while electrons are added to the **same shell**. Shielding hardly changes, so the outer electrons are **pulled in more strongly**.
- **Down a group**, a **new shell** is added each period. Outer electrons are **further from the nucleus** and **shielded** by more inner shells, so they are **held less strongly**.

## Atomic and ionic radius

| Trend | Direction | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomic radius** across a period | **Decreases** | Greater nuclear charge pulls the same shell inwards |
| **Atomic radius** down a group | **Increases** | More shells |
| **Positive ion** compared with its atom | **Smaller** (Na⁺ < Na) | Loses its outer shell; the remaining electrons are pulled in |
| **Negative ion** compared with its atom | **Larger** (Cl⁻ > Cl) | Extra electrons repel each other; same nuclear charge |

## Ionisation energy

:::callout{type=definition}
**First ionisation energy** is the energy needed to remove **one mole of electrons** from **one mole of gaseous atoms** to form one mole of gaseous positive ions:

$$\text{M(g)} \rightarrow \text{M}^+\text{(g)} + e^-$$
:::

![Graph of first ionisation energy against atomic number for elements 1 to 20, with peaks at helium, neon and argon and troughs at lithium, sodium and potassium](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/ionisation-energy.svg)

*Figure 1. First ionisation energies of hydrogen to calcium. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **Across a period** it generally **increases**, because of greater nuclear charge and smaller radius.
- **Down a group** it **decreases**, because the outer electron is further away and better shielded.
- The **noble gases** are at the **peaks** (very stable full shells); the **alkali metals** are at the **troughs**.

**Two small dips across a period:**

| Dip | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Be → B** (also Mg → Al) | Boron's outer electron is in a **2p** orbital, slightly higher in energy and better shielded than 2s, so it is easier to remove |
| **N → O** (also P → S) | Oxygen's fourth 2p electron is **paired**; repulsion between the paired electrons makes it easier to remove. Nitrogen's half-filled p sub-shell is extra stable |

### Successive ionisation energies

Removing electrons one after another needs more and more energy. A **very large jump** shows that an electron is being removed from an **inner shell**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Element Y has these successive ionisation energies (kJ/mol):**

| 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 738 | 1451 | 7733 | 10541 | 13630 |

The big jump comes between the **2nd and 3rd**, so Y has **two outer electrons**. It is in **group 2** (magnesium).
:::

## Electron affinity and electronegativity

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Electron affinity**: the energy **released** when one mole of gaseous atoms each **gain one electron** to form negative ions: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
- **Electronegativity**: the **ability of an atom in a covalent bond to attract the shared pair of electrons** towards itself.
:::

| Property | Across a period | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| Electron affinity | Generally **increases** (more negative) | Generally **decreases** |
| Electronegativity | **Increases** | **Decreases** |

**Fluorine is the most electronegative element** (4.0 on the Pauling scale), followed by oxygen and nitrogen. Electronegativity differences decide whether a bond is ionic, polar covalent or non-polar.

## Metallic character and reactivity

| Trend | Across a period | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| **Metallic character** (tendency to lose electrons) | Decreases: metal → metalloid → non-metal | Increases |
| **Reactivity of metals** (group 1, 2) | — | **Increases** (Li < Na < K): the outer electron is lost more easily |
| **Reactivity of non-metals** (group 7) | — | **Decreases** (F > Cl > Br > I): a new electron is attracted less strongly |
| **Nature of oxides** | Basic (Na₂O, MgO) → amphoteric (Al₂O₃) → acidic (SO₂, Cl₂O₇) | — |

### Diagonal relationships

Some elements resemble the element **diagonally below and to the right**: **lithium and magnesium**, **beryllium and aluminium**. They have similar charge-to-size ratios.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Group 1 metals become **more** reactive down the group, but halogens become **less** reactive. The same cause, more shells and more shielding, explains both: metals **lose** electrons more easily; halogens **gain** them less easily.
:::

## Key points

- Across a period: **nuclear charge rises**, so radius falls and ionisation energy and electronegativity rise.
- Down a group: **more shells**, so radius rises and ionisation energy and electronegativity fall.
- **Dips** in ionisation energy at **B** (2p electron) and **O** (paired electron).
- A **big jump** in successive ionisation energies gives the number of **outer electrons** (the group).
- **Fluorine** is the most electronegative element; metals get **more** reactive and halogens **less** reactive down their groups.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE often asks you to **explain the trend in atomic radius or ionisation energy** across a period and down a group, to **define electronegativity** and ionisation energy, and to **deduce the group** from successive ionisation energies. Always give the **reason** (nuclear charge, shells, shielding), not just the direction.
:::

## Quick check

1. Why does atomic radius decrease across period 3?
2. Why is the first ionisation energy of oxygen lower than that of nitrogen?
3. An element's successive ionisation energies jump sharply between the 1st and 2nd. Which group is it in?
4. Which is the most electronegative element?

**Answers:**

1. The nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same shell, so the outer electrons are pulled closer.
2. Oxygen's fourth 2p electron is paired, and repulsion makes it easier to remove; nitrogen's half-filled p sub-shell is more stable.
3. Group 1.
4. Fluorine.
