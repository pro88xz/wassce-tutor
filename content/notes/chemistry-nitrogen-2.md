From ammonia, industry makes **nitric acid**, and from nitric acid come fertilisers, explosives and dyes. In nature, nitrogen moves continuously between the air, the soil and living things in the **nitrogen cycle**. This lesson covers nitric acid and its unusual reactions, the effect of heat on nitrates, the test for nitrates, and the nitrogen cycle.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the manufacture of nitric acid by the Ostwald process
- describe the laboratory preparation of nitric acid
- describe the properties of nitric acid as an acid and as an oxidising agent
- describe the effect of heat on nitrates
- describe the brown-ring test for nitrates
- describe the nitrogen cycle and explain the importance of nitrogen fertilisers

## Manufacture: the Ostwald process

**Step 1.** Ammonia is **oxidised** by air over a **platinum (or platinum–rhodium) gauze** catalyst at about **900 °C**:

$$4\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + 5\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 4\text{NO(g)} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$$

**Step 2.** Nitrogen(II) oxide is cooled and reacts with more air:

$$2\text{NO(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$$

**Step 3.** Nitrogen(IV) oxide is absorbed in water with more air:

$$4\text{NO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 4\text{HNO}_3\text{(aq)}$$

## Laboratory preparation

Heat **potassium nitrate** (or sodium nitrate) with **concentrated sulphuric acid** in an **all-glass** retort, and condense the vapour:

$$\text{KNO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{KHSO}_4\text{(s)} + \text{HNO}_3\text{(g)}$$

All-glass apparatus is used because nitric acid vapour **attacks rubber and cork**. The acid is often yellow because it contains dissolved nitrogen(IV) oxide.

## Properties of nitric acid

**Physical:** colourless fuming liquid; pure acid boils at 83 °C; concentrated acid turns **yellow** on standing in light (2HNO₃ decomposes to NO₂, O₂ and water). It is stored in dark bottles.

### As an acid

Dilute nitric acid is a **strong acid**:

- turns blue litmus red
- reacts with bases: CuO + 2HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + H₂O
- reacts with carbonates: Na₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2NaNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

**But** nitric acid usually does **not** give hydrogen with metals: it is such a strong oxidising agent that any hydrogen formed is oxidised to water. (Very dilute nitric acid with magnesium is the exception.)

### As an oxidising agent

| Reaction | Equation and observation |
|---|---|
| **Copper + concentrated HNO₃** | Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O + 2NO₂. Copper dissolves, giving a **blue-green** solution and **brown** fumes of NO₂ |
| **Copper + dilute HNO₃** (50%) | 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 4H₂O + 2NO. Colourless NO, which turns **brown** in air (forming NO₂) |
| **Carbon + concentrated HNO₃** | C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂ + 4NO₂ + 2H₂O |
| **Sulphur + concentrated HNO₃** | Oxidised to sulphuric acid |
| **Iron(II) salts** | Oxidised to iron(III) (green → yellow-brown) |

**Passivity:** concentrated nitric acid makes iron and aluminium **passive**: a thin oxide layer stops further reaction.

## Effect of heat on nitrates

| Nitrates of | Products on heating | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Potassium, sodium** | **Nitrite (nitrate(III)) + oxygen** | 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂ |
| **Most other metals** (Ca to Cu) | **Metal oxide + nitrogen(IV) oxide + oxygen** | 2Cu(NO₃)₂ → 2CuO + 4NO₂ + O₂ (brown fumes) |
| **Silver, mercury** | **Metal + NO₂ + O₂** | 2AgNO₃ → 2Ag + 2NO₂ + O₂ |
| **Ammonium nitrate** | **Dinitrogen(I) oxide + water** | NH₄NO₃ → N₂O + 2H₂O |

The less reactive the metal, the more easily its nitrate decomposes. Brown fumes of NO₂ show that a nitrate (other than Na or K) is present. **Lead(II) nitrate** crackles on heating (**decrepitation**) and is used in the laboratory to prepare NO₂.

## Test for nitrates: the brown-ring test

1. To a solution of the suspected nitrate, add freshly prepared **iron(II) sulphate** solution.
2. Carefully pour **concentrated sulphuric acid** down the side of the tilted test tube, so that it forms a layer at the bottom.
3. A **brown ring** forms where the two layers meet: this confirms a **nitrate** (NO₃⁻).

Alternatively, warm the solution with **sodium hydroxide and aluminium (or Devarda's alloy)**: ammonia is given off, which turns damp red litmus blue.

## The nitrogen cycle

![The nitrogen cycle: nitrogen in the air is fixed by lightning, root-nodule bacteria and the Haber process; plants absorb nitrates; animals eat plants; decay produces ammonium, which nitrifying bacteria turn into nitrates; denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen to the air](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/nitrogen-cycle.svg)

*Figure 1. The nitrogen cycle. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Nitrogen fixation** | N₂ → nitrogen compounds: by **lightning** (N₂ + O₂ → NO → nitrates in rain), **nitrogen-fixing bacteria** in the root nodules of legumes and in soil, and the **Haber process** (fertilisers) |
| **Absorption** | Plants take up **nitrates** through their roots and make **proteins** |
| **Feeding** | Animals get nitrogen by eating plants (and other animals) |
| **Decay (ammonification)** | Bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms, urine and dung into **ammonium compounds** |
| **Nitrification** | **Nitrifying bacteria** oxidise ammonium to **nitrites** and then **nitrates** |
| **Denitrification** | **Denitrifying bacteria** in waterlogged soil turn nitrates back into **nitrogen gas** |

### Nitrogen fertilisers

Plants need nitrogen for **proteins** and healthy green leaves. Fertilisers include ammonium nitrate, ammonium sulphate, **urea** and NPK mixtures.

**Problems of overuse:**

- **eutrophication** of rivers and lakes (see the water topic)
- **nitrates in drinking water**, harmful to babies
- ammonium sulphate makes soil **more acidic** over time
- **wasted money** when fertiliser washes away in heavy rain

:::callout{type=local-example}
Rice farmers in Sierra Leone's inland valley swamps often apply **urea** fertiliser. Putting it on in small split doses, and working it into the soil rather than broadcasting it onto flood water, reduces losses to the air and to run-off. That gives the rice more nitrogen for the same money and causes less pollution of streams.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Metals do **not** give hydrogen with nitric acid, as they do with hydrochloric or sulphuric acid. Nitric acid is an oxidising agent, so it gives **oxides of nitrogen** (NO or NO₂) instead.
:::

## Key points

- **Ostwald process:** 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O (Pt, 900 °C) → NO₂ → HNO₃.
- Laboratory: **KNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄**, all-glass apparatus.
- Nitric acid is a **strong acid** but also a **strong oxidising agent**: with copper it gives **NO₂** (concentrated) or **NO** (dilute), not hydrogen.
- Heating nitrates: **Na, K → nitrite + O₂**; **most metals → oxide + NO₂ + O₂**; **Ag → metal + NO₂ + O₂**.
- **Brown-ring test** (FeSO₄ + concentrated H₂SO₄) identifies nitrates.
- The **nitrogen cycle**: fixation, absorption, feeding, decay, nitrification, denitrification.

## Quick check

1. Name the catalyst in the first stage of the Ostwald process.
2. Why does copper not give hydrogen with nitric acid?
3. Write an equation for the action of heat on sodium nitrate.
4. What do nitrifying bacteria do?

**Answers:**

1. Platinum (or platinum–rhodium) gauze.
2. Nitric acid is a strong oxidising agent; it oxidises any hydrogen to water and is itself reduced to NO₂ or NO.
3. 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂.
4. They convert ammonium compounds into nitrites and then nitrates.
