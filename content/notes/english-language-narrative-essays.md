A narrative essay tells a story. WASSCE narrative questions usually give you a situation (*"Write a story that ends with the words…"*, *"Narrate an experience that taught you a lesson"*, *"Write a story to illustrate the saying…"*) and ask you to bring it to life. Narratives are popular with students, and they can score very highly, but only when the story has a clear shape, a real moment of tension, and vivid detail. This lesson shows you how to build one, with a complete model story.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- plan a story with a clear beginning, rising action, climax and resolution
- choose the right point of view and keep your tenses consistent
- use dialogue, detail and the senses to bring scenes to life
- answer the three common kinds of narrative question
- end a story so it fits the question exactly

## The shape of a story

:::callout{type=definition}
Every good story follows the same arc:

| Stage | What happens | In an exam essay |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | set the scene: who, where, when | one short paragraph that hooks the reader |
| **Rising action** | a problem appears and grows | two or three paragraphs |
| **Climax** | the most tense or important moment | one strong paragraph |
| **Resolution** | how it ends, and what changed | one paragraph |
| **Reflection** (often) | the lesson learnt, or the meaning | a few sentences, especially for *"an experience that taught you…"* |
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**A list of events with no climax.** *"I woke up. I bathed. I went to school. We had Maths. Then we had lunch…"* is a diary, not a story. Start close to the action, and build towards one important moment.
:::

## The three common questions

| Question type | Example | What you must do |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience** | *Narrate an experience that taught you a lesson.* | tell **one** experience; make the **lesson** clear at the end |
| **Ending given** | *Write a story that ends with: "…and that was the last time I ever trusted a stranger."* | build the story so that this line is the **natural** last line; copy it **exactly** |
| **Proverb** | *Write a story to illustrate "All that glitters is not gold."* | the story must **show** the proverb's meaning, not just mention it |

:::callout{type=tip}
For an **ending-given** question, plan backwards: decide what must happen for that final line to make sense, then build the story towards it. Write the ending **word for word** as given; changing it can cost you content marks.
:::

## Point of view and tense

- **First person** (*I*) is the natural choice for *"an experience"*: it is personal and immediate.
- **Third person** (*he, she, they*) suits a story illustrating a proverb.
- Tell the story in the **past tense** and **stay in it**. Moving between past and present by accident is one of the commonest accuracy errors in narratives.

| Inconsistent | Consistent |
|---|---|
| *I ran to the door and I see a man standing there. He asks me…* | *I ran to the door and **saw** a man standing there. He **asked** me…* |

## Bringing scenes to life

### Show, don't tell

| Telling | Showing |
|---|---|
| *I was very afraid.* | *My hands were shaking so hard that I dropped the torch.* |
| *The market was busy.* | *Traders shouted prices over each other, a cart pushed through the crowd, and the smell of frying akara hung in the air.* |
| *My mother was angry.* | *My mother said nothing. She just folded her arms and waited.* |

### Use the senses

Sight is the obvious one. Add **sound**, **smell**, **touch** and **taste**, and the reader is there with you.

### Use a little dialogue

A few lines of speech make characters real and move the story faster. Punctuate them correctly:

> *"Where are you going?" my father asked.*
>
> *"Only to Musa's house," I said, not looking at him.*

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Too much dialogue.** A story that is mostly conversation loses the descriptive detail that earns expression marks. Use dialogue for key moments only.
:::

## A complete model narrative

**Question:** *Write a story that ends with the words: "…and that was the last time I ever trusted a stranger with my phone."*

::::model{title="The man at the motor park" label="Model narrative essay"}
**THE MAN AT THE MOTOR PARK**

The big motor park in the east of Freetown is never quiet, but on the Friday before the Christmas holidays it was a kind of madness. Drivers shouted the names of towns, apprentices dragged bags onto roof racks, and a woman selling cold water pushed through the crowd with a basin on her head, calling, *"Ice water! Ice water!"* I was sixteen, travelling alone to my grandmother's village near Makeni for the first time, and I felt very grown up.

My mother had given me exact instructions. *"Find the Makeni poda-poda, pay only the driver, keep your money in your shoe, and call me when you reach."* I had followed every one, except that the phone she had given me, an old smartphone with a cracked screen, had a flat battery. I had forgotten to charge it.

That was when the man appeared. He was well dressed, with a white shirt and polished shoes, and he spoke softly, like a teacher. *"You look worried, my sister,"* he said. When I explained, he smiled and pointed to a small kiosk with a sign that read **CHARGING 2 NLe**. *"My cousin runs that place. Give me the phone, I will put it on charge for you while you wait for your vehicle to fill. You can watch it from here."*

I hesitated. My mother's voice was in my head, but so was the picture of her face if I did not call. The kiosk was only a few metres away, and the man seemed so kind. I handed him the phone. He walked to the kiosk, bent over the counter, and I saw him plug something in. Then a lorry reversed between us, horn blaring, and for perhaps half a minute I could see nothing but its dusty side.

When the lorry had passed, the man was gone. The woman inside the kiosk looked at me blankly. She had no cousin in a white shirt. She had never seen my phone. I ran up and down the line of vehicles, my heart pounding, asking every driver and apprentice. One of them laughed. *"That one? He works this park every Friday. You are not the first today."*

I travelled to Makeni with my face pressed to the window, too ashamed to cry in front of the other passengers. At my grandmother's house, I borrowed a neighbour's phone to call my mother. She was silent for a long moment, and then, to my surprise, she did not shout. *"The phone can be replaced,"* she said quietly. *"I am glad it was only the phone."*

That night, lying on the mat beside my grandmother, I thought about how easily kindness can be worn like a clean shirt, and how quickly a minute's trust can be stolen. I have charged my phone every night since, and I have learnt to say "No, thank you" to smiling strangers, however well they are dressed. I still have the cracked phone my mother bought me next, and that was the last time I ever trusted a stranger with my phone.
::::

### Why it scores

| Feature | Where |
|---|---|
| **A hooking opening** that sets the scene with sound and movement | paragraph 1: the crowded motor park |
| **Rising action**: a problem (flat battery), then a temptation (the kind stranger) | paragraphs 2–3 |
| **Climax**: the moment the lorry blocks the view | paragraph 4 |
| **Resolution**: the discovery, and the mother's unexpected reaction | paragraphs 5–6 |
| **Reflection** that leads naturally into the given ending, copied **exactly** | paragraph 7 |
| **Show, don't tell**: *"my face pressed to the window"*, *"my heart pounding"* | throughout |
| **Brief, correctly punctuated dialogue** at key moments | paragraphs 2, 3, 5, 6 |
| **Consistent past tense** and first person | throughout |

It is about 520 words: a story needs a little more room than an argument, and every paragraph moves it forward.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Rewrite with 'showing' instead of 'telling': 'I was very nervous before the examination.'"}
*I read the same sentence in my notes four times without understanding a word of it, and my pen kept slipping in my damp hand.*
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Correct the tenses: 'When I reached the river, the water is very high and I decide to wait.'"}
*When I reached the river, the water **was** very high, and I **decided** to wait.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Plan a story to illustrate 'A stitch in time saves nine' (five stages in a few words each)."}
- **Opening**: a family's roof in Kenema with a small leak before the rains.
- **Rising action**: the father keeps postponing the repair; the leak grows.
- **Climax**: a storm at night; part of the roof collapses on the children's room.
- **Resolution**: no one is hurt, but the repair costs ten times as much.
- **Reflection**: had they fixed the small leak at once, they would have saved money and fear: a stitch in time saves nine.
:::

## Key points

- Shape every story: **opening, rising action, climax, resolution**, and **reflection** where the question needs it.
- Choose a point of view and **keep the past tense consistent**.
- **Show, don't tell**; use the **senses**; use **brief** dialogue, correctly punctuated.
- For an **ending given**, plan backwards and copy the ending **exactly**; for a **proverb**, make the story **show** its meaning.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Narratives are rewarded for a **clear plot** that answers the question, **vivid, controlled expression**, and **accurate grammar** (especially tense). Examiners mark down stories that drift from the question, list events with no tension, change tense carelessly, or end with a line that does not match the one given.
:::
