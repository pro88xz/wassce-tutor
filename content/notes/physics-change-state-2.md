Why does food cook faster in a pressure cooker? Why does water boil below 100 °C on top of a high mountain? Why does a refrigerator get cold inside? All depend on how pressure, vapour and latent heat interact.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain saturated vapour pressure and its link to boiling
- explain the effect of pressure and impurities on boiling and melting points
- explain the action of a refrigerator and a pressure cooker
- define humidity, relative humidity and dew point

## Saturated vapour pressure

In a closed container above a liquid, molecules evaporate and some return to the liquid. Eventually as many return as leave: the vapour is **saturated**.

:::callout{type=definition}
The **saturated vapour pressure (s.v.p.)** of a liquid is the pressure exerted by its vapour when it is in **dynamic equilibrium** with the liquid (as many molecules leaving as returning) at a given temperature. It **increases with temperature**.
:::

### Boiling and s.v.p.

:::callout{type=definition}
A liquid **boils** when its **saturated vapour pressure equals the external (atmospheric) pressure** above it.
:::

Bubbles of vapour can form inside the liquid only when the vapour pressure inside them can push back the pressure on the surface. At standard atmospheric pressure, water's s.v.p. reaches 760 mmHg at **100 °C**.

## Effect of pressure on boiling point

- **Higher pressure → higher boiling point.** In a **pressure cooker**, steam is trapped so the pressure rises to about twice atmospheric; water boils at about 120 °C, and food cooks faster.
- **Lower pressure → lower boiling point.** On a high mountain the air pressure is lower, so water boils below 100 °C (about 70 °C on Everest); food cooks more slowly.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Cooking beans or tough meat in a pressure cooker in a Freetown kitchen saves a lot of charcoal or gas: at 120 °C the food cooks in a fraction of the time it takes in an open pot at 100 °C.
:::

## Effect of impurities

- **Impurities raise the boiling point**: salted water boils slightly above 100 °C.
- **Impurities lower the melting (freezing) point**: salt melts ice on roads in cold countries; a mixture of ice and salt can reach −20 °C (used in old ice-cream makers).

## Effect of pressure on the melting point of ice

**Increasing pressure lowers the melting point of ice** (because ice contracts when it melts). **Regelation**: a weighted wire passes slowly through a block of ice — the ice melts under the high pressure beneath the wire and refreezes above it.

## The refrigerator

A refrigerator uses **latent heat of vaporisation** to move heat out of the cabinet.

1. A volatile liquid (the **refrigerant**) **evaporates** in the coils inside the freezer compartment, absorbing latent heat from the food and air there: the inside cools.
2. The **compressor** pumps the vapour out and compresses it, raising its temperature and pressure.
3. In the **condenser** coils at the back, the hot vapour **condenses**, releasing its latent heat to the room. (That is why the back of a fridge is warm.)
4. The liquid passes through an **expansion valve** back into the evaporator, and the cycle repeats.

## Humidity and dew point

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Humidity** is the amount of water vapour in the air.
- **Relative humidity** is the ratio of the mass of water vapour actually present in a given volume of air to the mass needed to saturate it at the same temperature, as a percentage.
- The **dew point** is the temperature to which air must be cooled for its water vapour to become **saturated** and start condensing (forming dew).
:::

- **Dew** forms on grass at night when the ground cools below the dew point.
- **Mist and fog** form when air near the ground cools below its dew point.
- On humid days sweat evaporates slowly, so we feel hot and sticky; in the dry harmattan, sweat evaporates quickly and skin and lips crack.
- **Relative humidity** is measured with a **wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer**: the wet bulb reads lower because evaporation cools it; the bigger the difference, the drier the air.

## Key points

- A liquid boils when its saturated vapour pressure equals the external pressure.
- Higher pressure raises the boiling point (pressure cooker); lower pressure lowers it (mountains).
- Impurities raise the boiling point and lower the freezing point.
- A refrigerator uses evaporation of a refrigerant to absorb latent heat inside and condensation to release it outside.
- Dew point: the temperature at which air becomes saturated.

## Quick check

1. Why does water boil at a lower temperature on a mountain?
2. In a refrigerator, where does the refrigerant absorb heat?
3. What is the dew point?

**Answers:** 1. The atmospheric pressure is lower there, so the saturated vapour pressure of water equals it at a lower temperature. 2. In the evaporator coils inside the freezer compartment, as it evaporates. 3. The temperature at which air becomes saturated with water vapour and dew begins to form.
