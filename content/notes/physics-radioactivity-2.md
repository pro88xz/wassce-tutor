The same nuclear forces that power the Sun can light a city or destroy one. This lesson covers detecting radiation, nuclear fission and fusion, mass–energy equivalence, and the uses and dangers of radioactivity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe how radiation is detected (Geiger–Müller tube, cloud chamber)
- explain background radiation
- explain nuclear fission, chain reactions and fusion, and use $E = mc^2$
- give uses of radioisotopes and describe safety precautions

## Detecting radiation

**Geiger–Müller (G-M) tube and counter:** a tube containing gas at low pressure with a central wire at high voltage. Radiation entering through a thin mica window **ionises** the gas; the ions and electrons cause an avalanche, producing a pulse of current that is counted (and often heard as a click). The count rate measures the **activity**.

**Cloud chamber:** a chamber of air saturated with alcohol vapour is cooled. Radiation ionises the air, and vapour condenses on the ions, leaving visible **tracks**: thick, straight tracks for alpha; thin, wiggly tracks for beta; few, faint tracks for gamma.

Other detectors: **photographic film** (film badges), the **spark counter**, and the **gold-leaf electroscope** (discharged by ionised air).

## Background radiation

Even with no source present, a G-M counter registers a small count: **background radiation**. Sources include:

- **natural**: radon gas from rocks and soil, cosmic rays from space, rocks and building materials, food and our own bodies (potassium-40, carbon-14)
- **artificial**: medical X-rays and treatments, fallout from nuclear tests, nuclear industry

In experiments, the background count is measured first and **subtracted** from every reading.

## Nuclear energy and mass–energy equivalence

The mass of a nucleus is slightly **less** than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. This **mass defect** is the mass equivalent of the **binding energy** that holds the nucleus together. Einstein's equation links mass and energy:

$$E = mc^2$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How much energy is released when 0.001 kg of mass is converted into energy?**

$$E = mc^2 = 0.001 \times (3.0 \times 10^{8})^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

That is roughly the energy released by burning 3000 tonnes of coal.
:::

### Nuclear fission

:::callout{type=definition}
**Nuclear fission** is the **splitting of a heavy nucleus** (such as uranium-235) into two lighter nuclei of roughly equal mass, with the release of **neutrons** and a large amount of **energy**.
:::

$$^{235}_{92}\text{U} +\ ^{1}_{0}n \rightarrow\ ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} +\ ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}n + \text{energy}$$

The 2 or 3 neutrons released can split more uranium nuclei, causing a **chain reaction**.

- In a **nuclear reactor**, the chain reaction is **controlled**: a **moderator** (graphite or water) slows the neutrons, **control rods** (boron or cadmium) absorb extra neutrons, and a **coolant** carries the heat to make steam for turbines.
- In an **atomic bomb**, the chain reaction is **uncontrolled**.

### Nuclear fusion

:::callout{type=definition}
**Nuclear fusion** is the **joining of light nuclei** (such as isotopes of hydrogen) to form a heavier nucleus, with the release of energy.
:::

$$^{2}_{1}\text{H} +\ ^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow\ ^{4}_{2}\text{He} +\ ^{1}_{0}n + \text{energy}$$

Fusion powers the **Sun and stars**. It needs extremely high temperatures (millions of degrees) so the positive nuclei move fast enough to overcome their repulsion.

| Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|
| splitting of heavy nuclei | joining of light nuclei |
| used in nuclear power stations | powers the Sun; not yet practical on Earth |
| produces long-lived radioactive waste | little radioactive waste |

## Uses of radioisotopes

| Field | Use |
|---|---|
| **Medicine** | radiotherapy (cobalt-60 gamma rays kill cancer cells); tracers (iodine-131 to study the thyroid); sterilising syringes and instruments |
| **Industry** | thickness gauges for paper and metal sheets (beta); detecting leaks in underground pipes (tracers); smoke detectors (americium-241, alpha) |
| **Agriculture** | tracers to study how plants take up fertiliser; killing pests; preserving food by irradiation |
| **Dating** | **carbon-14 dating** of wood, bones and cloth up to about 50 000 years; uranium–lead dating of rocks |

## Dangers and safety precautions

Ionising radiation can kill or damage cells, causing **radiation burns**, **radiation sickness**, **cancer** (e.g. leukaemia) and **genetic mutations**. Alpha sources are most dangerous **inside** the body (if swallowed or inhaled); gamma is most dangerous **outside** because it penetrates.

1. Handle sources with **long tongs**, never with bare hands.
2. Store sources in **lead-lined boxes**, labelled with the radiation hazard sign.
3. Keep the **exposure time short** and the **distance large**.
4. Wear **protective clothing** and **film badges** to monitor dose.
5. Never eat, drink or smoke where radioactive materials are used.
6. Dispose of radioactive waste safely (sealed, shielded containers, deep storage).

## Key points

- G-M tube and cloud chamber detect radiation by ionisation; subtract the background count.
- $E = mc^2$; the mass defect is the binding energy.
- Fission: heavy nucleus splits, chain reaction, controlled in reactors. Fusion: light nuclei join, powers the Sun.
- Uses: radiotherapy, tracers, thickness gauges, smoke detectors, carbon dating.
- Precautions: tongs, lead shielding, short exposure, distance, film badges.

## Quick check

1. What is the function of the control rods in a nuclear reactor?
2. Name the process that powers the Sun.
3. Give one medical use of radioisotopes.

**Answers:** 1. They absorb neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction. 2. Nuclear fusion. 3. For example: radiotherapy to destroy cancer cells, or tracers such as iodine-131.
