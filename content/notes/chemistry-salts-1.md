"Salt" to most people means the white crystals on the dinner table. To a chemist, sodium chloride is just one member of a huge family that includes copper(II) sulphate for spraying crops, ammonium nitrate fertiliser, baking soda and the alum used to clear muddy water. This lesson classifies salts, gives the rules for predicting whether they dissolve, and describes their uses.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a salt and name salts from their parent acid and base
- classify salts as normal, acid, basic, double or complex
- use the solubility rules to predict whether a salt is soluble
- state the uses of important salts

## What a salt is

:::callout{type=definition}
A **salt** is a compound formed when the **replaceable hydrogen ions of an acid are wholly or partly replaced** by a metal ion or an ammonium ion.
:::

A salt's name comes from its **metal (or ammonium)** and its **parent acid**:

| Acid | Salt name ends in | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid, HCl | **-chloride** | Sodium chloride, NaCl |
| Sulphuric acid, H₂SO₄ | **-sulphate** (or hydrogensulphate) | Copper(II) sulphate, CuSO₄ |
| Nitric acid, HNO₃ | **-nitrate** | Potassium nitrate, KNO₃ |
| Carbonic acid, H₂CO₃ | **-carbonate** (or hydrogencarbonate) | Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ |
| Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH | **-ethanoate** | Sodium ethanoate, CH₃COONa |
| Phosphoric(V) acid, H₃PO₄ | **-phosphate** | Calcium phosphate, Ca₃(PO₄)₂ |

## Types of salts

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal salt** | **All** the replaceable hydrogen of the acid has been replaced | NaCl, Na₂SO₄, CaCO₃, K₂CO₃ |
| **Acid salt** | Only **part** of the replaceable hydrogen has been replaced; it still contains replaceable H | NaHSO₄ (sodium hydrogensulphate), NaHCO₃ (sodium hydrogencarbonate), Ca(HCO₃)₂ |
| **Basic salt** | Contains **hydroxide (or oxide)** as well as the usual anion; formed when a base is only partly neutralised | Zn(OH)Cl, Mg(OH)Cl, basic lead(II) carbonate |
| **Double salt** | Two normal salts crystallised together in fixed proportions; gives the ions of both in solution | Potash alum, K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O; ammonium iron(II) sulphate |
| **Complex salt** | Contains a **complex ion**, a central metal ion bonded to molecules or ions | Na[Al(OH)₄] (sodium aluminate); [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ |

Only **dibasic or tribasic** acids can form acid salts. Sulphuric acid forms both NaHSO₄ (acid salt) and Na₂SO₄ (normal salt); monobasic HCl can form only NaCl.

## Solubility rules

| Salts | Solubility in water |
|---|---|
| All **sodium, potassium and ammonium** salts | **Soluble** |
| All **nitrates** | **Soluble** |
| **Chlorides** | Soluble, **except** silver chloride (AgCl) and lead(II) chloride (PbCl₂, which dissolves in hot water) |
| **Sulphates** | Soluble, **except** barium sulphate (BaSO₄) and lead(II) sulphate (PbSO₄); calcium sulphate is only slightly soluble |
| **Carbonates** | **Insoluble**, except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium |
| **Hydroxides** | Insoluble, except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium; calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Predicting solubility.** Is each salt soluble?

| Salt | Rule applied | Soluble? |
|---|---|---|
| Lead(II) nitrate | All nitrates are soluble | **Yes** |
| Barium sulphate | Exception among sulphates | **No** |
| Ammonium carbonate | All ammonium salts are soluble | **Yes** |
| Calcium carbonate | Carbonates are insoluble (Ca is not Na, K or NH₄) | **No** |
| Silver chloride | Exception among chlorides | **No** |

Whether a salt is soluble decides **how it is prepared** (next lesson).
:::

## Uses of salts

| Salt | Uses |
|---|---|
| **Sodium chloride** (common salt) | Seasoning and preserving food (salted fish); raw material for sodium hydroxide and chlorine; oral rehydration salts |
| **Sodium carbonate** (washing soda) | Softening hard water; making glass, soap and paper |
| **Baking soda**, NaHCO₃ | Baking powder (gives CO₂ to raise bread and cakes); antacid; fire extinguishers |
| **Calcium carbonate** (limestone) | Making cement, lime and glass; antacid tablets |
| **Calcium sulphate** (plaster of Paris) | Plaster casts for broken bones; building |
| **Copper(II) sulphate** | Fungicide (Bordeaux mixture) for crops; test for water; electroplating |
| **Ammonium nitrate, ammonium sulphate, potassium nitrate** | Fertilisers; KNO₃ and NH₄NO₃ also in explosives |
| **Ammonium chloride** | Electrolyte in dry cells; soldering flux |
| **Potash alum** | Clearing muddy water (coagulation); dyeing; stopping bleeding from small cuts |
| **Magnesium sulphate** (Epsom salt) | Laxative |
| **Silver bromide** | Photographic film |
| **Iron(II) sulphate** | Iron tablets for anaemia |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Along the coast of Sierra Leone, families produce **sea salt** by boiling or sun-drying seawater, a traditional source of income. **Potash alum** is sold in markets and used to clear muddy well and river water before use: it coagulates the fine clay particles so they settle out. The water still needs boiling or chlorinating to kill germs.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃, is an **acid salt**, even though its solution is slightly **alkaline**. "Acid salt" means it still has a replaceable hydrogen, not that its solution is acidic.
:::

## Key points

- A **salt** forms when the replaceable hydrogen of an acid is wholly or partly replaced by a metal or ammonium ion.
- Types: **normal** (all H replaced), **acid** (some H left: NaHSO₄, NaHCO₃), **basic** (contains OH⁻), **double** (alum) and **complex** (complex ion).
- Solubility rules: **all Na, K, NH₄ salts and all nitrates dissolve**; chlorides except **AgCl, PbCl₂**; sulphates except **BaSO₄, PbSO₄** (CaSO₄ slightly); carbonates are insoluble except Na, K, NH₄.
- Salts are used in **food, farming, medicine, water treatment and industry**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define a salt**, to **classify given salts** (normal, acid, basic, double), to **use solubility rules** to predict soluble and insoluble salts, and to **state uses** of named salts such as NaHCO₃, CuSO₄, alum and plaster of Paris.
:::

## Quick check

1. Define an acid salt and give an example.
2. Which of these is insoluble in water: KCl, PbSO₄, NH₄NO₃, Na₂CO₃?
3. Give one use of potash alum.
4. Why can hydrochloric acid not form an acid salt?

**Answers:**

1. A salt in which only part of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid has been replaced, e.g. NaHSO₄ or NaHCO₃.
2. PbSO₄.
3. Any one: clearing (coagulating) muddy water; dyeing; stopping bleeding from small cuts.
4. It is monobasic: it has only one replaceable hydrogen, so replacing it gives a normal salt.
