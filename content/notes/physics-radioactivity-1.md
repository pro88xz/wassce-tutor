Some atoms are unstable. Without any outside cause, their nuclei break up and fire out particles or rays. This radioactivity is used to treat cancer, date ancient bones and generate electricity — and it must be handled with respect.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define radioactivity and name the three types of radiation
- compare the nature, charge, penetration and ionising power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation
- write equations for alpha and beta decay
- define half-life and solve half-life problems

## Radioactivity

:::callout{type=definition}
**Radioactivity** (radioactive decay) is the **spontaneous disintegration** of the **unstable nuclei** of certain atoms, with the emission of **alpha particles**, **beta particles** and/or **gamma rays**.
:::

Decay is **spontaneous** (it happens by itself) and **random** (we cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay). It is **not affected** by temperature, pressure or chemical combination.

## The three radiations

![Alpha particles are stopped by paper, beta particles by a few millimetres of aluminium, while gamma rays pass through both and are only reduced by thick lead](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/radiation-penetration.svg)

*Figure 1. Penetrating power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Property | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | helium nucleus $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ (2 protons + 2 neutrons) | fast electron $^{0}_{-1}e$ | electromagnetic wave (photon) |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Relative mass | 4 | $\dfrac{1}{1840}$ | 0 |
| Speed | about 5% of the speed of light | up to about 99% of $c$ | speed of light |
| Penetration | **least**: stopped by paper or a few cm of air | stopped by a few mm of aluminium | **most**: reduced by thick lead or concrete |
| Ionising power | **strongest** | moderate | **weakest** |
| Deflection in fields | slightly, towards negative | strongly, towards positive | **not deflected** |

The greater the ionising power, the faster the radiation loses its energy, so the **less penetrating** it is.

## Nuclear equations

In any nuclear equation, the **nucleon numbers (top)** and the **proton numbers (bottom)** must balance.

**Alpha decay:** the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons. $A$ falls by **4**, $Z$ falls by **2**.

$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} +\ ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

**Beta decay:** a neutron changes into a proton and an electron, which is emitted. $A$ is **unchanged**, $Z$ rises by **1**.

$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow\ ^{14}_{7}\text{N} +\ ^{0}_{-1}e$$

**Gamma emission:** no change in $A$ or $Z$; the nucleus simply loses energy.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**$^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$ emits an alpha particle, and the product then emits a beta particle. Find the final nuclide's proton and nucleon numbers.**

After α: $A = 226 - 4 = 222$, $Z = 88 - 2 = 86$.

After β: $A = 222$, $Z = 86 + 1 = 87$.

Final: $^{222}_{87}\text{X}$.
:::

## Half-life

![Decay curve: the activity of a sample falls from 800 to 400 counts per minute in one half-life, to 200 after two half-lives and to 100 after three](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/decay-curve.svg)

*Figure 2. Radioactive decay curve. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
The **half-life** $T_{1/2}$ of a radioactive substance is the **time taken for half of the radioactive nuclei** in a sample to decay (equivalently, for its activity to fall to half).
:::

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction remaining is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n$:

$$N = N_0\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$$

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 × 10⁹ years; carbon-14: 5730 years; iodine-131: 8 days).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A radioactive sample has a half-life of 6 hours. What fraction remains after 24 hours? If it starts with 160 g, what mass remains?**

Number of half-lives $= \dfrac{24}{6} = 4$. Fraction remaining $= \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \dfrac{1}{16}$.

Mass remaining $= \dfrac{160}{16} = 10$ g. (160 → 80 → 40 → 20 → 10.)
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The activity of a sample falls from 800 to 100 counts per minute in 15 days. Find its half-life.**

$800 \rightarrow 400 \rightarrow 200 \rightarrow 100$: three half-lives in 15 days, so $T_{1/2} = 5$ days.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
After **two** half-lives, **a quarter** remains, not none. Half-life halves what is **left** each time; it never reaches exactly zero.
:::

## Key points

- Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random, unaffected by physical or chemical conditions.
- α: helium nucleus, +2, least penetrating, most ionising. β: electron, −1. γ: EM wave, most penetrating, least ionising.
- α decay: $A - 4$, $Z - 2$. β decay: $A$ same, $Z + 1$.
- Half-life: time for half the nuclei to decay; after $n$ half-lives, $(1/2)^n$ remains.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**Compare alpha, beta and gamma radiation** (nature, charge, penetration, ionisation, deflection), **complete nuclear equations**, and solve **half-life problems** (fractions remaining, masses, activities, or reading half-life from a decay curve). These appear in nearly every paper.
:::

## Quick check

1. Which radiation is the most penetrating?
2. $^{234}_{90}\text{Th}$ emits a beta particle. What is the product?
3. A source has a half-life of 2 days. What fraction remains after 6 days?

**Answers:** 1. Gamma. 2. $^{234}_{91}\text{Pa}$ (protactinium): $A$ unchanged, $Z$ up by 1. 3. $\tfrac{1}{8}$.
