A camera flash charges up with a whine and then releases its energy in a split second. Inside is a capacitor: a device that stores electric charge, and with it energy, ready to be released when needed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe a parallel-plate capacitor and define capacitance
- use $Q = CV$ and $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d}$
- state the factors affecting the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor
- describe charging and discharging of a capacitor

## The capacitor

:::callout{type=definition}
A **capacitor** is a device that **stores electric charge**. The simplest form is two parallel metal **plates** separated by an insulator called the **dielectric** (air, paper, mica, plastic or ceramic).
:::

When a capacitor is connected to a battery, electrons flow onto one plate (making it negative) and away from the other (leaving it positive). The plates gain **equal and opposite** charges, $+Q$ and $-Q$. No charge passes through the dielectric.

## Capacitance

:::callout{type=definition}
The **capacitance** $C$ of a capacitor is the ratio of the **charge** on either plate to the **potential difference** between the plates:

$$C = \dfrac{Q}{V}$$

Its unit is the **farad (F)**: 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹. Practical capacitors are measured in microfarads (μF = $10^{-6}$ F), nanofarads (nF) and picofarads (pF = $10^{-12}$ F).
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 50 μF capacitor is connected to a 12 V battery. How much charge does it store?**

$$Q = CV = 50 \times 10^{-6} \times 12 = 6.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{C}$$
:::

## Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor

$$C = \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

where $A$ = area of overlap of the plates, $d$ = separation, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}$ F m⁻¹ and $\varepsilon_r$ = relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of the material between the plates.

### Factors affecting capacitance

| Change | Effect on capacitance |
|---|---|
| **Larger** area of overlap $A$ | **increases** |
| **Smaller** separation $d$ | **increases** |
| Dielectric with **larger** $\varepsilon_r$ (e.g. mica instead of air) | **increases** |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Two plates, each of area 0.02 m², are 1 mm apart in air. Find the capacitance. (ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹)**

$$C = \dfrac{8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.02}{1 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.77 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 177\ \text{pF}$$

Filling the gap with mica ($\varepsilon_r = 6$) would raise it to about 1.06 nF.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Convert **mm to m** and **μF to F** before calculating. A separation of 1 mm is $1 \times 10^{-3}$ m; forgetting this makes the answer 1000 times too small.
:::

## Charging and discharging

**Charging** (capacitor and resistor in series with a battery):

- current is **largest at the start** and falls to zero
- p.d. across the capacitor rises from zero to the battery voltage
- charging is **quicker** with a smaller resistance or smaller capacitance

**Discharging** (charged capacitor connected across a resistor):

- current flows the **opposite** way, largest at first, falling to zero
- the p.d. and charge fall gradually

The time scale is set by the product $RC$ (the **time constant**).

## Uses of capacitors

1. **Camera flash units**: store charge and release it quickly through the flash tube.
2. **Smoothing** the output of rectifiers in power supplies (phone chargers).
3. **Tuning circuits** in radios (variable capacitor with the coil selects a frequency).
4. **Timing circuits** (the RC time constant), for example in indicator flashers.
5. Blocking d.c. while passing a.c. in amplifiers.
6. Reducing **sparking** at switch contacts and in car ignition systems.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Open an old phone charger or radio and you will see cylindrical **electrolytic capacitors** marked with values such as "470 μF 16 V". The voltage marked is the maximum it can safely take; exceeding it can make the capacitor burst.
:::

## Key points

- A capacitor stores charge on two plates separated by a dielectric.
- $C = Q/V$, in farads; practical values in μF, nF, pF.
- Parallel-plate: $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d}$; larger area, smaller gap and better dielectric increase $C$.
- Charging and discharging currents are largest at first and fall to zero.

## Quick check

1. What is the unit of capacitance?
2. A capacitor stores 2 × 10⁻⁴ C at 20 V. What is its capacitance?
3. How does halving the separation of the plates affect the capacitance?

**Answers:** 1. The farad (F). 2. $C = \dfrac{2 \times 10^{-4}}{20} = 1 \times 10^{-5}$ F = 10 μF. 3. It doubles.
