Put a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field and it is pushed sideways. That "motor effect" spins the fans, pumps and cassette players of the world, and it is the reason a moving-coil meter's needle swings.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field
- use Fleming's left-hand rule and $F = BIL$
- explain the action of a d.c. motor and a moving-coil galvanometer
- describe the force between parallel currents and on a moving charge

## The motor effect

![A wire carrying current out of the page between the poles of a magnet experiences an upward force; beside it, Fleming's left-hand rule shows Field, Current and Motion at right angles](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/motor-effect.svg)

*Figure 1. The motor effect and Fleming's left-hand rule. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

A conductor carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a **force** — unless the current is parallel to the field. The force is **at right angles** to both the field and the current.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Fleming's left-hand rule:** hold the **first finger**, **second finger** and **thumb** of the **left hand** mutually at right angles. If the **F**irst finger points along the **F**ield (N → S) and the se**C**ond finger along the **C**urrent, the thu**M**b gives the direction of the **M**otion (force).
:::

### Size of the force

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

where $B$ is the **magnetic flux density** (in **tesla, T**), $I$ the current, $L$ the length of conductor in the field and θ the angle between the conductor and the field. When the conductor is at right angles to the field, $F = BIL$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A wire 0.20 m long carries 3.0 A at right angles to a magnetic field of 0.5 T. Find the force on it.**

$$F = BIL = 0.5 \times 3.0 \times 0.20 = 0.30\ \text{N}$$
:::

The force increases with **larger current**, **stronger field** and **longer conductor in the field**; reversing either the current or the field **reverses** the force.

## The d.c. electric motor

A rectangular **coil** on an axle sits between the poles of a magnet; current enters and leaves through a **split-ring commutator** and **carbon brushes**.

1. Current flows in opposite directions in the two sides of the coil, so (by Fleming's rule) one side is pushed **up** and the other **down**: a **couple** turns the coil.
2. As the coil passes the vertical, the **split ring** reverses the current in the coil, so the forces keep turning it in the **same direction**.
3. The coil's **momentum** carries it past the vertical position where the forces have no turning effect.

**Increasing the speed/turning effect:** more turns on the coil, a larger current, a stronger magnet, winding the coil on a soft-iron armature, using several coils at different angles (for smoother running).

Uses: fans, blenders, water pumps, cassette and DVD players, electric vehicles, starter motors.

## The moving-coil galvanometer

A coil hangs in a **radial field** between curved magnet poles, with a soft-iron cylinder inside it. Current in the coil produces a couple that turns it against the **hairsprings** until the spring's restoring couple balances. The deflection is **proportional to the current**, so the scale is **linear** (evenly spaced). It is converted into an ammeter (shunt) or a voltmeter (multiplier).

## Force between two parallel currents

- Parallel currents in the **same direction** **attract**.
- Parallel currents in **opposite directions** **repel**.

The **ampere** is defined from this force.

## Force on a moving charge

A charge $q$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a field $B$ feels a force

$$F = Bqv$$

at right angles to its motion, so it moves in a **circle**. This is how the beam in an old cathode-ray TV was steered, and it is used to separate particles in a mass spectrometer.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Every ceiling fan and standing fan in Sierra Leone's hot season runs on the motor effect. Take apart a broken table fan at a repair stall and you will find the coils and the magnetic core that make it turn.
:::

## Key points

- A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a force ⟂ to field and current.
- Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger Field, seCond finger Current, thuMb Motion.
- $F = BIL$ (conductor ⟂ field); $B$ in tesla.
- The d.c. motor uses a split-ring commutator to keep the coil turning one way.
- Parallel currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel.

## Quick check

1. What is the function of the split-ring commutator in a d.c. motor?
2. Find the force on a 0.5 m wire carrying 2 A at right angles to a 0.4 T field.
3. Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel?

**Answers:** 1. It reverses the current in the coil every half turn so the coil keeps rotating in the same direction. 2. $F = 0.4 \times 2 \times 0.5 = 0.4$ N. 3. They attract.
