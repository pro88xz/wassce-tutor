# Excretion in plants and animals: nitrogenous wastes and homeostasis

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- explain why excess amino acids must be processed
- describe deamination and urea formation
- compare ammonia, urea and uric acid as nitrogenous wastes
- relate excretion to osmoregulation and homeostasis
- identify the roles of liver, kidneys, lungs and skin

## Deamination and urea formation

Proteins are needed for growth and repair, but the body cannot store excess amino acids in the same way it stores glycogen or fat. When amino acids are present in excess, their amino groups are removed in the liver by a process called deamination. The remaining carbon skeleton can be used in respiration or converted into other substances. The nitrogen-containing part is converted through reactions into urea.

Ammonia is highly toxic and must be removed or converted quickly. Urea is less toxic and can be transported in the blood to the kidneys. The kidneys filter the blood and regulate how much water and dissolved salts are retained. Urea, excess salts and variable amounts of water leave the body in urine.

## Comparing nitrogenous wastes

Different animals excrete nitrogenous waste in different forms.

- **Ammonia:** very toxic and highly soluble. It requires a large amount of water for safe removal and is common in many aquatic animals, where water is readily available.
- **Urea:** less toxic than ammonia and requires less water for removal. It is the main nitrogenous waste in mammals and some other animals.
- **Uric acid:** relatively insoluble and can be excreted as a semi-solid paste, conserving water. It is common in birds, reptiles and insects.

The form of nitrogenous waste is related to habitat, water availability and reproductive strategy. For example, a bird embryo develops inside an egg and cannot release large amounts of dilute waste into the environment, so uric acid helps conserve water and reduces accumulation of toxic ammonia.

## Homeostasis and osmoregulation

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite changes inside or outside the organism. Osmoregulation is the control of water and dissolved salt concentrations. The kidneys contribute to both excretion and osmoregulation by adjusting the composition and volume of urine.

When the body has insufficient water, the pituitary gland releases more antidiuretic hormone (ADH). ADH increases the permeability of parts of the kidney tubules and collecting ducts to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced. When the body has excess water, less ADH is released, less water is reabsorbed and a larger volume of more dilute urine is produced.

The kidneys also regulate important ions. Their function helps maintain blood volume, osmotic balance and suitable conditions for cells. Severe kidney failure allows metabolic wastes and excess water and salts to accumulate, which can be life-threatening.

## Other organs supporting excretion

The lungs remove carbon dioxide and water vapour. The liver forms urea and processes potentially harmful substances. The skin releases sweat containing water and salts, with small amounts of urea. These roles should be distinguished: the liver produces urea, while the kidneys remove it from blood.

## WASSCE examination focus

A frequent comparison asks why aquatic animals may excrete ammonia while birds excrete uric acid. Relate the answer to toxicity and water conservation. For questions on urine concentration, explain the role of ADH and water reabsorption. Do not claim that the kidneys produce urea; urea is formed in the liver.
