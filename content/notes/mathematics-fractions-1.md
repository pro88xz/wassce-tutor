A **fraction** is one of the most fundamental ideas in mathematics. It tells you how many *parts* you have out of a *whole*. When Aminata buys a loaf of bread and cuts it into $4$ equal pieces, each piece is $\frac{1}{4}$ of the loaf. If she eats $3$ of those pieces, she has eaten $\frac{3}{4}$ of the loaf. Simple — but the trouble comes when fractions appear in WAEC questions with awkward numbers, mixed forms, or comparisons.

This lesson covers everything about fractions: their structure, the different types, equivalent fractions, comparing fractions, and simplifying them. The next lesson handles the four operations, conversion between fractions and decimals, and approximation. By the end of the two lessons you will be able to handle any WAEC fraction question with confidence.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **fraction** represents a part of a whole. It is written as $\dfrac{a}{b}$, where:

- $a$ is the **numerator** (the top number) — how many parts you have.
- $b$ is the **denominator** (the bottom number) — how many equal parts the whole is divided into.
- $b$ must not be zero. Division by zero is undefined.

Example: $\dfrac{3}{4}$ means *three parts out of four equal parts*.
:::

## The structure of a fraction

Every fraction has two numbers: the top (numerator) and the bottom (denominator), separated by a line called the **fraction bar** (which actually means *divided by*).

$$\frac{a}{b} = a \div b$$

So $\dfrac{3}{4}$ is the same as $3 \div 4 = 0.75$. We use this fact when we convert between fractions and decimals in the next lesson.

### What the numbers mean

- **Numerator** ($a$): how many parts you are taking.
- **Denominator** ($b$): how many parts the whole is divided into.

If I divide a loaf of bread into $5$ equal pieces and you take $2$ of them, you have $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of the bread. The $5$ is the denominator (total slices), the $2$ is the numerator (slices you took).

### Visualising fractions

Imagine a rectangular bar:

- Divided into $4$ equal parts, $3$ shaded → $\dfrac{3}{4}$
- Divided into $8$ equal parts, $5$ shaded → $\dfrac{5}{8}$
- Divided into $2$ equal parts, $1$ shaded → $\dfrac{1}{2}$

The denominator tells you how *fine* the divisions are. The numerator tells you how many of those divisions you are taking.

## Types of fractions

### Proper fractions

The numerator is **less than** the denominator. The value is less than $1$.

- $\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{4}, \dfrac{5}{8}, \dfrac{7}{10}$ are all proper fractions.

### Improper fractions

The numerator is **greater than or equal to** the denominator. The value is $1$ or more.

- $\dfrac{5}{4}, \dfrac{7}{3}, \dfrac{8}{8}, \dfrac{15}{4}$ are all improper.

An improper fraction can be rewritten as a *mixed number* (next type).

### Mixed numbers

A whole number followed by a proper fraction.

- $1\tfrac{1}{4}$ means *one whole plus a quarter* — equal to $\dfrac{5}{4}$.
- $2\tfrac{2}{3}$ means *two wholes plus two thirds* — equal to $\dfrac{8}{3}$.
- $3\tfrac{3}{4}$ means *three and three quarters* — equal to $\dfrac{15}{4}$.

### Equivalent fractions

Different-looking fractions that represent the **same value**.

- $\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{4}{8} = \dfrac{50}{100}$
- $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{6}{8} = \dfrac{9}{12} = \dfrac{12}{16}$

You get equivalent fractions by **multiplying or dividing both numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number**.

## Converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions

### Mixed → improper

Multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, keep the denominator.

**Example.** Convert $2\tfrac{3}{5}$ to improper.

$$2\tfrac{3}{5} = \frac{(2 \times 5) + 3}{5} = \frac{10 + 3}{5} = \frac{13}{5}$$

**Example.** Convert $4\tfrac{2}{7}$ to improper.

$$4\tfrac{2}{7} = \frac{(4 \times 7) + 2}{7} = \frac{28 + 2}{7} = \frac{30}{7}$$

### Improper → mixed

Divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole-number part; the remainder over the denominator is the fraction part.

**Example.** Convert $\dfrac{17}{4}$ to mixed.

$17 \div 4 = 4$ remainder $1$. So $\dfrac{17}{4} = 4\tfrac{1}{4}$.

**Example.** Convert $\dfrac{23}{5}$ to mixed.

$23 \div 5 = 4$ remainder $3$. So $\dfrac{23}{5} = 4\tfrac{3}{5}$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Convert each between mixed and improper.**

**(a)** $3\tfrac{2}{7}$ to improper.
$$3\tfrac{2}{7} = \frac{(3 \times 7) + 2}{7} = \frac{21 + 2}{7} = \frac{23}{7}$$

**(b)** $\dfrac{19}{6}$ to mixed.
$19 \div 6 = 3$ remainder $1$. So $\dfrac{19}{6} = 3\tfrac{1}{6}$.

**(c)** $5\tfrac{3}{8}$ to improper.
$$5\tfrac{3}{8} = \frac{(5 \times 8) + 3}{8} = \frac{43}{8}$$

**(d)** $\dfrac{47}{12}$ to mixed.
$47 \div 12 = 3$ remainder $11$. So $\dfrac{47}{12} = 3\tfrac{11}{12}$.

**(e)** $\dfrac{100}{7}$ to mixed.
$100 \div 7 = 14$ remainder $2$. So $\dfrac{100}{7} = 14\tfrac{2}{7}$.
:::

## Equivalent fractions in detail

Two fractions are equivalent if they have the same value, even if they look different. You produce equivalent fractions by **scaling**:

$$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times k}{b \times k} \quad \text{for any } k \neq 0$$

$$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \div k}{b \div k} \quad \text{if } k \text{ divides both}$$

**Examples.**

- $\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{2 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \dfrac{4}{6}$ (multiplied top and bottom by $2$)
- $\dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{4 \div 2}{6 \div 2} = \dfrac{2}{3}$ (divided top and bottom by $2$)
- $\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{5}{20}$ (both multiplied by $5$)
- $\dfrac{8}{12} = \dfrac{2}{3}$ (both divided by $4$)

The second form — dividing — is how we **simplify** fractions (next section).

### Testing whether two fractions are equivalent

The cross-multiplication test: $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$ if and only if $a \times d = b \times c$.

**Example.** Is $\dfrac{3}{4}$ equivalent to $\dfrac{9}{12}$?

Cross-multiply: $3 \times 12 = 36$ and $4 \times 9 = 36$. Equal, so **yes** they are equivalent.

**Example.** Is $\dfrac{2}{3}$ equivalent to $\dfrac{6}{10}$?

Cross-multiply: $2 \times 10 = 20$ and $3 \times 6 = 18$. Not equal, so **no** they are not equivalent.

## Simplifying fractions

A fraction is in **simplest form** (or *lowest terms*) when the numerator and denominator have no common factors other than $1$.

To simplify: divide both numerator and denominator by their **greatest common factor** (GCF, also called HCF — highest common factor).

**Example.** Simplify $\dfrac{8}{12}$.

Factors of $8$: $1, 2, 4, 8$.
Factors of $12$: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$.
Greatest common factor: $4$.

$$\frac{8}{12} = \frac{8 \div 4}{12 \div 4} = \frac{2}{3}$$

**Example.** Simplify $\dfrac{18}{24}$.

Factors of $18$: $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$.
Factors of $24$: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24$.
Greatest common factor: $6$.

$$\frac{18}{24} = \frac{3}{4}$$

**Example.** Simplify $\dfrac{45}{60}$.

GCF of $45$ and $60$ is $15$.

$$\frac{45}{60} = \frac{3}{4}$$

### Step-by-step simplification

If you can't spot the GCF immediately, divide by smaller common factors repeatedly until no more divide both.

**Example.** Simplify $\dfrac{48}{72}$.

Step 1: Both even. Divide by $2$. $\dfrac{48}{72} = \dfrac{24}{36}$.
Step 2: Both still even. Divide by $2$. $\dfrac{24}{36} = \dfrac{12}{18}$.
Step 3: Both even. Divide by $2$. $\dfrac{12}{18} = \dfrac{6}{9}$.
Step 4: Both divisible by $3$. Divide by $3$. $\dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.
Step 5: $2$ and $3$ have no common factor (other than $1$). Done.

Final: $\dfrac{48}{72} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Simplify each fraction to lowest terms.**

**(a)** $\dfrac{15}{25}$
GCF$(15, 25) = 5$. So $\dfrac{15}{25} = \dfrac{3}{5}$.

**(b)** $\dfrac{36}{54}$
GCF$(36, 54) = 18$. So $\dfrac{36}{54} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.

**(c)** $\dfrac{105}{280}$
Divide both by $5$: $\dfrac{21}{56}$. Then by $7$: $\dfrac{3}{8}$.

**(d)** $\dfrac{144}{216}$
Divide both by $8$: $\dfrac{18}{27}$. Then by $9$: $\dfrac{2}{3}$.

**(e)** $\dfrac{75}{100}$
Divide both by $25$: $\dfrac{3}{4}$.
:::

## Comparing fractions

Which is bigger, $\dfrac{3}{4}$ or $\dfrac{5}{8}$? You can't easily tell at a glance because the denominators are different. There are two reliable methods.

### Method 1 — Common denominator

Convert both fractions to have the same denominator. Then compare the numerators.

**Example.** Compare $\dfrac{3}{4}$ and $\dfrac{5}{8}$.

The least common multiple (LCM) of $4$ and $8$ is $8$. Convert:
- $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{6}{8}$ (multiplied top and bottom by $2$)
- $\dfrac{5}{8}$ stays the same.

Now compare: $\dfrac{6}{8} > \dfrac{5}{8}$. So $\dfrac{3}{4} > \dfrac{5}{8}$.

### Method 2 — Cross-multiplication

For $\dfrac{a}{b}$ vs $\dfrac{c}{d}$: compare $a \times d$ with $b \times c$.
- If $a \times d > b \times c$, then $\dfrac{a}{b} > \dfrac{c}{d}$.
- If $a \times d < b \times c$, then $\dfrac{a}{b} < \dfrac{c}{d}$.
- If $a \times d = b \times c$, they are equal.

**Example.** Compare $\dfrac{3}{7}$ and $\dfrac{4}{9}$.

$3 \times 9 = 27$. $7 \times 4 = 28$. Since $27 < 28$, we have $\dfrac{3}{7} < \dfrac{4}{9}$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Compare each pair.**

**(a)** $\dfrac{2}{3}$ vs $\dfrac{5}{8}$.
Cross-multiply: $2 \times 8 = 16$, $3 \times 5 = 15$. $16 > 15$, so $\dfrac{2}{3} > \dfrac{5}{8}$.

**(b)** $\dfrac{3}{5}$ vs $\dfrac{4}{7}$.
Cross-multiply: $3 \times 7 = 21$, $5 \times 4 = 20$. $21 > 20$, so $\dfrac{3}{5} > \dfrac{4}{7}$.

**(c)** $\dfrac{7}{12}$ vs $\dfrac{5}{8}$.
Cross-multiply: $7 \times 8 = 56$, $12 \times 5 = 60$. $56 < 60$, so $\dfrac{7}{12} < \dfrac{5}{8}$.

**(d)** $\dfrac{11}{15}$ vs $\dfrac{4}{5}$.
Cross-multiply: $11 \times 5 = 55$, $15 \times 4 = 60$. $55 < 60$, so $\dfrac{11}{15} < \dfrac{4}{5}$.
:::

## Ordering several fractions

To order several fractions, find a common denominator for all of them, then compare numerators.

**Example.** Arrange in ascending order: $\dfrac{2}{3}, \dfrac{3}{4}, \dfrac{5}{6}, \dfrac{7}{12}$.

LCM of $3, 4, 6, 12$ is $12$. Convert each:
- $\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{8}{12}$
- $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{9}{12}$
- $\dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{10}{12}$
- $\dfrac{7}{12} = \dfrac{7}{12}$

Now order the numerators: $7 < 8 < 9 < 10$. So:

$$\frac{7}{12} < \frac{2}{3} < \frac{3}{4} < \frac{5}{6}$$

## Fractions in real life — Sierra Leonean examples

Fractions appear everywhere. Some examples:

- A mother divides $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of a kilo of rice between her two children. Each gets $\dfrac{1}{3}$ kilo.
- A farmer in Bo harvests $\dfrac{4}{5}$ of his cassava crop in October, leaving $\dfrac{1}{5}$ for November.
- A school survey shows $\dfrac{3}{5}$ of students prefer English over Maths. If there are $200$ students, that's $\dfrac{3}{5} \times 200 = 120$ students.
- A market trader sells $\dfrac{7}{8}$ of her stock by lunchtime, leaving $\dfrac{1}{8}$ for the afternoon.

Understanding fractions makes all these calculations natural.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A factory produces $\dfrac{5}{6}$ of its target on Monday. On Tuesday it produces $\dfrac{4}{5}$ of the target. Which day was more productive?**

Compare $\dfrac{5}{6}$ and $\dfrac{4}{5}$.

Cross-multiply: $5 \times 5 = 25$, $6 \times 4 = 24$. Since $25 > 24$, $\dfrac{5}{6} > \dfrac{4}{5}$.

Answer: Monday was more productive.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Aminata cuts a $48$ cm rope into pieces of length $\dfrac{3}{4}$ cm each. How many pieces does she get?**

Number of pieces $= 48 \div \dfrac{3}{4}$. (This is division of a whole number by a fraction, which the next lesson covers in full. For now, the answer is:)

$$48 \div \frac{3}{4} = 48 \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{192}{3} = 64 \text{ pieces}$$

Answer: $64$ pieces.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
At a local market in Bo, a vendor sells palm wine in $\dfrac{1}{4}$-litre bottles. If a customer buys $9$ bottles, how much palm wine has she bought?

Total = $9 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{9}{4} = 2\tfrac{1}{4}$ litres.

The customer has bought $2\tfrac{1}{4}$ litres. The vendor uses fraction arithmetic to keep track of stock — adding $\dfrac{1}{4}$ at a time, summing across many bottles. This is the same arithmetic WAEC tests, just disguised as everyday trade.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Common errors with fractions:**

**1. Confusing numerator with denominator.**
- $\dfrac{3}{4}$ is *three quarters*. The bottom number tells you the size of each part. Switching them gives $\dfrac{4}{3}$, which is a different fraction.

**2. Not simplifying.**
- $\dfrac{8}{12}$ is correct, but $\dfrac{2}{3}$ is in simpler form. WAEC may insist on simplest form for full marks.

**3. Comparing fractions by numerator alone.**
- $\dfrac{2}{3}$ is **not** smaller than $\dfrac{3}{8}$ just because $2 < 3$. The denominators matter. Cross-multiply or convert to common denominator.

**4. Adding numerators and denominators separately.**
- $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{3} \neq \dfrac{2}{5}$. You need a common denominator first (see the next lesson).

**5. Forgetting to convert mixed numbers when multiplying.**
- $2\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \neq 2 \times 3 \times \dfrac{1}{2}$. Convert $2\tfrac{1}{2}$ to $\dfrac{5}{2}$ first.
:::

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Fraction questions in WAEC fall into these patterns:

- **Simplify a fraction.** *Express $\dfrac{45}{60}$ in lowest terms.*
- **Compare fractions.** *Which is greater: $\dfrac{5}{8}$ or $\dfrac{7}{12}$?*
- **Convert mixed/improper.** *Express $3\tfrac{2}{5}$ as an improper fraction.*
- **Order fractions.** *Arrange in ascending order: $\dfrac{2}{3}, \dfrac{5}{6}, \dfrac{7}{8}$.*
- **Word problems.** Quantity problems disguised as fraction word stories.

The foundational skill is **always simplifying to lowest terms**. WAEC examiners check for this on every answer.
:::

## Practice problems

**1.** Simplify $\dfrac{24}{36}$.

**2.** Simplify $\dfrac{90}{135}$.

**3.** Convert $4\tfrac{2}{5}$ to an improper fraction.

**4.** Convert $\dfrac{29}{7}$ to a mixed number.

**5.** Compare: which is greater, $\dfrac{5}{8}$ or $\dfrac{7}{12}$?

**6.** Compare: $\dfrac{4}{9}$ vs $\dfrac{5}{11}$.

**7.** Are $\dfrac{8}{12}$ and $\dfrac{14}{21}$ equivalent?

**8.** Arrange in ascending order: $\dfrac{3}{4}, \dfrac{5}{6}, \dfrac{7}{12}, \dfrac{2}{3}$.

**9.** A bag contains $\dfrac{3}{5}$ kg of rice. If $\dfrac{1}{4}$ kg is used, what fraction of the original bag is left?

**10.** Find a fraction equivalent to $\dfrac{3}{8}$ with a denominator of $40$.

### Worked solutions

**1.** GCF$(24, 36) = 12$. So $\dfrac{24}{36} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.

**2.** GCF$(90, 135) = 45$. So $\dfrac{90}{135} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.

**3.** $4\tfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{(4 \times 5) + 2}{5} = \dfrac{22}{5}$.

**4.** $29 \div 7 = 4$ remainder $1$. So $\dfrac{29}{7} = 4\tfrac{1}{7}$.

**5.** Cross-multiply: $5 \times 12 = 60$; $8 \times 7 = 56$. $60 > 56$, so $\dfrac{5}{8} > \dfrac{7}{12}$.

**6.** Cross-multiply: $4 \times 11 = 44$; $9 \times 5 = 45$. $44 < 45$, so $\dfrac{4}{9} < \dfrac{5}{11}$.

**7.** Cross-multiply: $8 \times 21 = 168$; $12 \times 14 = 168$. Equal, so **yes** they are equivalent. (Both simplify to $\dfrac{2}{3}$.)

**8.** Common denominator: LCM$(4, 6, 12, 3) = 12$. Convert: $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{9}{12}$; $\dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{10}{12}$; $\dfrac{7}{12} = \dfrac{7}{12}$; $\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{8}{12}$. Order: $7 < 8 < 9 < 10$. Answer: $\dfrac{7}{12} < \dfrac{2}{3} < \dfrac{3}{4} < \dfrac{5}{6}$.

**9.** This requires subtraction (covered fully in the next lesson). Common denominator: LCM$(5, 4) = 20$. $\dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{12}{20}$. $\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{5}{20}$. Difference: $\dfrac{12 - 5}{20} = \dfrac{7}{20}$ kg left. As a fraction of the original $\dfrac{3}{5}$ kg bag: $\dfrac{7/20}{3/5} = \dfrac{7}{20} \times \dfrac{5}{3} = \dfrac{35}{60} = \dfrac{7}{12}$ of the bag is left.

**10.** $\dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{?}{40}$. Multiply both by $5$: $\dfrac{3 \times 5}{8 \times 5} = \dfrac{15}{40}$.
