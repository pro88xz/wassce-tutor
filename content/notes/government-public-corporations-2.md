Power cuts, burst water pipes, buses that break down and corporations that need government bail-outs year after year: the problems of public corporations are familiar across West Africa. Because they are owned by the public and spend public money, they must be controlled, and when they fail, governments look for reforms such as commercialisation and privatisation. This lesson explains how public corporations are controlled, why many perform poorly and how they can be reformed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain how public corporations are controlled
- explain the problems of public corporations
- define commercialisation and privatisation
- state the merits and demerits of privatisation
- suggest ways of improving public corporations

## Control of public corporations

![A public corporation and its board at the centre, controlled by the minister, Parliament, the Auditor-General, the courts, regulators, and consumers and the press](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/corporation-control.svg)

*Figure 1. Who keeps a public corporation in check? WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Control | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Ministerial control** | The minister appoints (and can remove) the board, gives general policy directions, approves major investments and borrowing |
| **Parliamentary control** | Parliament creates the corporation by Act and can amend or repeal it; debates its annual reports; questions the minister; its committees examine accounts |
| **Financial control** | The **Auditor-General** audits accounts; government approves budgets, loans and tariffs |
| **Judicial control** | As a legal person, the corporation can be **sued** for breach of contract or negligence, and courts can stop it acting beyond its powers |
| **Regulatory control** | Regulators set tariffs and service standards (for example, for electricity and water) |
| **Public control** | Consumers' complaints, the press and public opinion |

## Problems of public corporations

1. **Political interference** in appointments, contracts and pricing.
2. **Corruption and mismanagement** of funds.
3. **Overstaffing**, often through political patronage.
4. **Inefficiency** and poor service delivery.
5. **Inadequate funding** and dependence on government subsidies.
6. **Prices kept below cost** for political reasons, causing losses.
7. **Unpaid bills**, including by government ministries.
8. **Obsolete equipment** and poor maintenance.
9. **Lack of competition**: monopoly removes the pressure to improve.
10. **Lack of qualified managers** and frequent changes of boards.

## Commercialisation and privatisation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Commercialisation** means reorganising a public corporation so that it is run **on business lines**, covering its costs and making a profit, while remaining **state-owned**. **Privatisation** means **transferring the ownership or management** of a public enterprise, wholly or partly, to **private investors**.
:::

Forms of privatisation include **sale of shares** to the public or investors, **sale of the whole enterprise**, **management contracts** and **concessions** (a private firm runs a facility for a fixed period).

| Merits of privatisation | Demerits of privatisation |
|---|---|
| Improves efficiency and service | Higher prices for consumers |
| Reduces the burden on the government budget | Loss of jobs as firms cut staff |
| Raises revenue from the sale | Public assets may be sold cheaply |
| Attracts private and foreign investment | Strategic sectors may fall under foreign control |
| Encourages competition | Private monopolies may replace public ones |
| Reduces political interference and corruption | Unprofitable areas, such as rural communities, may be neglected |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone set up a **National Commission for Privatisation** under the **National Commission for Privatisation Act 2002** to oversee the reform of state enterprises. One of the best-known examples is the **container terminal at the Queen Elizabeth II Quay** in Freetown, which since **2011** has been run by a private operator under a long-term **concession**, while the **Ports Authority** remains the public landlord and regulator.
:::

## Improving public corporations

- **Appoint boards and managers on merit**, not party loyalty
- **Limit political interference** in day-to-day management
- **Charge realistic tariffs**, with targeted support for poor consumers
- **Make government ministries pay their bills**
- **Invest in modern equipment** and maintenance
- **Regular audits** and publication of accounts
- **Independent regulators** to protect consumers
- **Commercialise or privatise** where competition can improve service
- **Train staff** and reward performance

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**"Explain four ways public corporations are controlled."** Name the controller and the tool:

- **The minister** appoints the board and gives general directions.
- **Parliament** creates the corporation by Act and debates its reports.
- **The Auditor-General** audits its accounts.
- **The courts** hear suits brought by or against it.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Commercialisation and privatisation are **different**. A **commercialised** corporation stays **in government ownership** but is run like a business; a **privatised** one passes wholly or partly into **private ownership or management**.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Ministerial control**: the minister's power to appoint and remove a corporation's board, give it general policy directions and approve major investment and borrowing.
- **Judicial control**: control through the courts: as a legal person, a corporation can be sued, and courts can stop it acting beyond its powers.
- **Regulatory control**: control by regulators, who set tariffs and service standards, for example for electricity and water.
:::

## Key points

- Controls: **ministerial, parliamentary, financial (Auditor-General), judicial, regulatory and public**.
- Problems: **political interference, corruption, overstaffing, inefficiency, below-cost prices, unpaid bills, old equipment, monopoly**.
- **Commercialisation** keeps state ownership but runs on business lines; **privatisation** transfers ownership or management to private investors.
- Sierra Leone: **National Commission for Privatisation (2002)**; **Freetown container terminal concession (2011)**.

## WASSCE examination focus

Expect to **explain how public corporations are controlled**, to **state their problems**, to **distinguish commercialisation from privatisation**, and to **give the merits and demerits of privatisation**. Essay questions often ask why public corporations in West Africa perform poorly.
:::

## Quick check

1. How does Parliament control public corporations?
2. State three problems of public corporations.
3. Distinguish commercialisation from privatisation.
4. Give two demerits of privatisation.

**Answers:**

1. It creates them by Act and can amend or repeal that Act, debates their annual reports, questions the responsible minister, and examines their accounts through committees.
2. Any three: political interference; corruption; overstaffing; inefficiency; inadequate funding; prices below cost; unpaid bills; obsolete equipment; lack of competition; lack of qualified managers.
3. Commercialisation keeps the corporation in state ownership but runs it on business lines to cover costs and make a profit; privatisation transfers its ownership or management wholly or partly to private investors.
4. Any two: higher prices; job losses; assets sold cheaply; foreign control of strategic sectors; private monopolies; neglect of unprofitable areas.
