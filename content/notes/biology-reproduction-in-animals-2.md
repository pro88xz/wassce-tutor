# Reproduction in animals: life cycles, development and parental care

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- describe major stages in the life cycles of insects, fish, amphibians, birds and mammals
- distinguish complete and incomplete metamorphosis
- explain how eggs support embryonic development
- compare oviparous, viviparous and ovoviviparous reproduction
- describe the importance and forms of parental care
- explain how reproductive adaptations improve survival of offspring
- interpret life-cycle descriptions and diagrams in WASSCE questions

## From fertilisation to a new animal

After fertilisation, the zygote undergoes repeated mitotic cell divisions. These divisions produce many cells. Cells then become specialised through **differentiation**, forming tissues and organs. The developing animal is called an embryo during early development; the exact terms used later differ among animal groups.

The embryo needs:
- a supply of nutrients and energy for growth
- oxygen for aerobic respiration
- removal of carbon dioxide and other metabolic wastes
- suitable temperature, moisture and protection
- genetic instructions inherited from its parents

Different animal groups meet these needs in different ways. An embryo may develop in water, inside a shelled egg, or inside the reproductive tract of the mother.

## Oviparous, viviparous and ovoviviparous animals

**Oviparous animals** lay eggs, and embryonic development occurs outside the mother's body. Examples include most birds, many fish, frogs and many reptiles. The egg contains or is surrounded by protective structures and, in many species, a food reserve.

**Viviparous animals** give birth to live young after the embryo develops inside the mother's body. In most mammals, the developing fetus receives oxygen and nutrients from the mother through the placenta and umbilical cord. The embryo's wastes are transferred to the mother's blood for elimination.

**Ovoviviparous animals** retain eggs inside the parent's body until they hatch or are close to hatching. The embryo is nourished mainly by the yolk in its egg rather than by a placenta. This term is used for certain fish and reptiles. Reproductive biology is diverse, and some species show intermediate arrangements; use the definitions carefully.

| Mode | Where embryo develops | Main source of nourishment | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oviparous | In an egg laid outside the body | Yolk or other egg reserves | Hen, frog, many fish |
| Viviparous | Inside the mother's body | Usually maternal supply; placenta in most mammals | Human, cow |
| Ovoviviparous | In an egg retained inside the parent's body | Mainly yolk within the egg | Some sharks and snakes |

Do not classify every animal that gives birth to live young as a placental mammal. Some fish and reptiles retain eggs internally, and marsupials have a different pattern of development from placental mammals.

## Metamorphosis in insects

**Metamorphosis** is a marked change in body form during development. Insects show two major patterns.

### Complete metamorphosis

Complete metamorphosis has four stages:

**Egg → larva → pupa → adult**

The larva is very different from the adult in body form, feeding habits and often habitat. The larva feeds and grows, moulting as its exoskeleton becomes too small. It then forms a pupa, during which extensive reorganisation produces the adult body.

Examples include butterflies, moths, houseflies, mosquitoes and beetles.

In a butterfly:
- the egg is laid on or near a suitable food plant
- the caterpillar larva feeds, grows and moults several times
- the pupa or chrysalis is a non-feeding stage in which adult structures develop
- the adult butterfly emerges, expands and dries its wings, then feeds and reproduces

The larva and adult often use different food resources, reducing competition between young and adult stages.

### Incomplete metamorphosis

Incomplete metamorphosis has three main stages:

**Egg → nymph → adult**

The nymph resembles a small, immature adult but lacks fully developed wings and reproductive organs. It grows through a series of moults. There is no pupal stage.

Examples include grasshoppers, cockroaches and termites. In grasshoppers, the nymph usually lives in a similar habitat and feeds on similar food to the adult, although its size and abilities differ.

| Feature | Complete metamorphosis | Incomplete metamorphosis |
|---|---|---|
| Stages | Egg, larva, pupa, adult | Egg, nymph, adult |
| Pupal stage | Present | Absent |
| Young stage | Larva differs greatly from adult | Nymph resembles a small adult |
| Change to adult | Major reorganisation during pupal stage | Gradual change through moulting |
| Examples | Butterfly, mosquito, housefly | Grasshopper, cockroach |

## Moulting and growth in insects

Insects have an external skeleton made largely of chitin. This exoskeleton protects and supports the body but does not expand continuously as the animal grows. The insect must therefore **moult**: it sheds the old exoskeleton and expands before the new one hardens.

A newly moulted insect is vulnerable because its new exoskeleton is soft. It may hide until the covering hardens. Moulting occurs during larval or nymphal growth and should not be confused with metamorphosis. Moulting is the shedding of the exoskeleton; metamorphosis is the developmental transformation in body form.

## Life cycle of a frog

A typical frog has a life cycle involving external fertilisation and metamorphosis:

1. Adult frogs return to water to reproduce.
2. The female lays eggs, often in clusters or masses; the male releases sperm over them.
3. The fertilised eggs develop into embryos.
4. Tadpoles hatch. They are aquatic and usually breathe using gills.
5. Hind limbs and then forelimbs develop; lungs become functional.
6. The tail is gradually absorbed, and the young frog becomes adapted to life on land and in water.
7. The adult reaches sexual maturity and reproduces.

The tadpole and adult frog differ in feeding, breathing and locomotion. Tadpoles commonly feed on algae or plant material, while adult frogs are generally carnivorous and catch small animals. The exact diet varies by species and developmental stage.

## Development in birds

Bird embryos develop inside eggs laid by the female. The egg provides a protected environment and food reserves. The shell is strong enough to reduce damage but porous enough for gas exchange. The embryo uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide through pores in the shell.

Incubation keeps the egg within a suitable temperature range. If the temperature is too low or too high for too long, normal development may be disrupted. At hatching, the chick breaks the shell using a temporary structure called an **egg tooth** in many bird species. The egg tooth is later lost.

Some birds hatch in a relatively developed state and can move soon after hatching; these are often described as precocial. Others hatch helpless, with limited feathers and closed or partly closed eyes, and depend heavily on parental feeding; these are altricial. These terms describe different developmental strategies, not different types of fertilisation.

## Development in mammals

In most mammals, fertilisation is internal and the embryo develops in the uterus. After implantation in the uterine lining, the embryo develops membranes and, in placental mammals, a placenta.

The placenta:
- transfers oxygen and nutrients from maternal blood to fetal blood
- transfers carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes from fetal blood to maternal blood
- produces hormones that help maintain pregnancy
- provides an exchange surface while normally keeping maternal and fetal blood separate

The umbilical cord contains blood vessels linking the fetus to the placenta. The umbilical vein carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. The umbilical arteries carry blood containing fetal wastes from the fetus to the placenta. This is a common examination point: the names of umbilical vessels are based on direction of blood flow relative to the fetus, not oxygen content.

After birth, mammary glands produce milk to nourish the young. Mammalian parental care may also include warmth, protection, grooming and teaching behaviours.

## Parental care and survival

Parental care is behaviour by one or both parents that improves the survival or development of offspring. It may include:
- guarding eggs or nests
- incubating eggs to maintain a suitable temperature
- feeding young
- carrying or sheltering young
- defending young from predators
- teaching young to find food or avoid danger

Parental care is common in many birds and mammals, but also occurs in some fish, amphibians and insects. For example, some fish guard eggs, and some insects protect or provision their young.

Parental care can increase offspring survival, but it costs energy and may reduce the number of offspring a parent can produce. Species that provide little care often produce many eggs or young, while species that invest heavily in each offspring often produce fewer. These are general patterns rather than fixed rules.

## Reproductive adaptations and their significance

A reproductive adaptation is a feature or behaviour that improves the likelihood of successful fertilisation, development or survival of offspring.

Examples include:
- large numbers of eggs in many fish, increasing the chance that some survive
- jelly coverings around amphibian eggs, helping protect them in water
- a shell, albumen and yolk in bird eggs, protecting and nourishing the embryo on land
- internal fertilisation in mammals, reducing exposure of gametes to the environment
- a placenta in most mammals, allowing exchange between mother and fetus
- parental incubation and feeding in many birds, improving survival after egg-laying
- different larval and adult feeding niches in many insects, reducing competition within a species

When answering a question about an adaptation, state the feature and explain how it improves reproductive success. Do not merely list the feature.

## WASSCE examination focus

- Complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa, adult. Incomplete metamorphosis: egg, nymph, adult.
- A nymph resembles the adult; a larva often looks very different.
- Moulting is shedding the exoskeleton; it is not another name for metamorphosis.
- Oviparous animals lay eggs; viviparous animals give birth to live young; ovoviviparous animals retain eggs internally and embryos rely mainly on yolk.
- The placenta is an exchange organ, not a direct mixture of maternal and fetal blood.
- In the umbilical cord, the umbilical vein carries blood towards the fetus and the umbilical arteries carry blood away from the fetus.
- Link every reproductive adaptation to its function and survival advantage.

1. State the three main stages of incomplete metamorphosis.
2. Name the stage found in complete but not incomplete metamorphosis.
3. Distinguish moulting from metamorphosis.
4. What is the main source of nourishment for an ovoviviparous embryo?
5. State two functions of the placenta.
6. In which direction does the umbilical vein carry blood?
7. Give two examples of parental care in birds or mammals.
8. Explain why a butterfly larva and adult may feed on different foods.

**Answers**

1. Egg, nymph and adult.
2. The pupa.
3. Moulting is the shedding of the old exoskeleton; metamorphosis is the developmental change in body form.
4. The yolk stored in the retained egg.
5. It transfers oxygen and nutrients to the fetus and transfers carbon dioxide and other wastes to the mother.
6. From the placenta towards the fetus.
7. Examples include incubating eggs, feeding chicks or young, guarding nests and protecting offspring.
8. Different food sources reduce competition between the larva and adult stages.
