Why can two healthy parents have a child with sickle cell disease? Why are boys more often colour-blind than girls? Why does a couple with blood groups A and B sometimes have a child with blood group O? The answers come from **Mendelian genetics**, the rules of inheritance first worked out by Gregor Mendel in the 1860s with garden peas. These rules let you predict the chances of each characteristic in the offspring of any cross. WASSCE sets a genetics cross almost every year, and the marks depend on setting it out correctly. This lesson teaches Mendel's laws, the monohybrid cross, the test cross, codominance, blood groups, sex determination and sex linkage.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe Mendel's experiments and state his two laws
- set out a monohybrid cross fully, with a Punnett square
- give genotype and phenotype ratios and probabilities
- use a test cross to find an unknown genotype
- explain codominance and multiple alleles, using sickle cell and ABO blood groups
- explain sex determination and sex-linked inheritance

## Mendel's experiments

Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was a monk who bred **garden peas**. Peas were a good choice: they have clear contrasting characters (tall or dwarf, round or wrinkled seeds), grow quickly, produce many seeds, and can be self- or cross-pollinated under control.

He crossed pure-breeding **tall** plants with pure-breeding **dwarf** plants:

- **F₁** (first generation): **all tall**. The dwarf character seemed to disappear.
- **F₂** (F₁ self-pollinated): about **3 tall : 1 dwarf**. The dwarf character came back.

Mendel concluded that each character is controlled by a pair of "factors" (we now call them **alleles**), one from each parent, and that one can mask the other.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Mendel's first law (law of segregation):** the two alleles of a gene **separate** during gamete formation, so each gamete carries **only one** allele of each gene.

**Mendel's second law (law of independent assortment):** alleles of **different** genes are sorted into gametes independently of each other.
:::

## Setting out a monohybrid cross

A **monohybrid cross** follows **one** characteristic. Examiners award marks for each line, so always write all of them:

1. **Key** (state the symbols): T = tall (dominant), t = dwarf (recessive)
2. **Parental phenotypes**
3. **Parental genotypes**
4. **Gametes** (circle them)
5. **Punnett square** (or crossing lines)
6. **Offspring genotypes and phenotypes, with the ratio**

![A Punnett square for Tt × Tt: gametes T and t from each parent give TT, Tt, Tt and tt; genotype ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt; phenotype ratio 3 tall : 1 dwarf](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/monohybrid-cross.svg)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Two heterozygous tall pea plants are crossed. Show the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring.**

Key: T = tall, t = dwarf.

| Step | Working |
|---|---|
| parental phenotypes | tall × tall |
| parental genotypes | Tt × Tt |
| gametes | (T) (t) × (T) (t) |
| offspring genotypes | TT, Tt, Tt, tt |
| genotype ratio | 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt |
| phenotype ratio | **3 tall : 1 dwarf** |

The probability that any one offspring is dwarf is $\tfrac14$ (25%).
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Treating the ratio as an exact count.** A 3 : 1 ratio is a **probability**. Of 8 seeds, you might get 5 tall and 3 dwarf. Mendel needed hundreds of plants to see the ratio clearly (he counted 787 tall and 277 dwarf: about 2.8 : 1).
:::

## The test cross

A tall plant may be **TT** or **Tt**; they look the same. To find out, cross it with a **homozygous recessive** (tt) plant.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How can you tell whether a tall plant is TT or Tt?**

Cross it with a dwarf (tt) plant.

- If it is **TT**: TT × tt → all Tt → **all tall**.
- If it is **Tt**: Tt × tt → Tt, Tt, tt, tt → **1 tall : 1 dwarf**.

**If any dwarf offspring appear, the tall parent must be Tt.**
:::

## Codominance: sickle cell

In **codominance**, neither allele is dominant: **both** are expressed in the heterozygote.

Haemoglobin is controlled by the alleles **A** (normal haemoglobin) and **S** (sickle haemoglobin):

| Genotype | Phenotype |
|---|---|
| AA | normal haemoglobin |
| AS | **sickle cell trait** (carrier): both kinds of haemoglobin; usually healthy |
| SS | **sickle cell disease** (anaemia): red cells become sickle-shaped when oxygen is low, block capillaries and cause pain crises |

![A Punnett square for AS × AS: offspring AA, AS, AS and SS in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1; each child has a 1 in 4 chance of sickle cell disease](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/sickle-cell-cross.svg)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A man and a woman both have the genotype AS. What is the chance that their first child has sickle cell disease?**

AS × AS → AA, AS, AS, SS. **Probability of SS = $\tfrac14$ (25%)** for each child, every pregnancy. The chance is the same for the second child; it does not "use up".
:::

## Multiple alleles: ABO blood groups

The ABO blood group gene has **three** alleles: $I^A$ and $I^B$ are **codominant** with each other, and both are **dominant** to $I^O$.

| Blood group (phenotype) | Possible genotypes |
|---|---|
| A | $I^AI^A$ or $I^AI^O$ |
| B | $I^BI^B$ or $I^BI^O$ |
| AB | $I^AI^B$ |
| O | $I^OI^O$ |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A man of blood group A (heterozygous) marries a woman of blood group B (heterozygous). What blood groups can their children have?**

$I^AI^O \times I^BI^O$. Gametes: $I^A$, $I^O$ and $I^B$, $I^O$.

Offspring: $I^AI^B$ (AB), $I^AI^O$ (A), $I^BI^O$ (B), $I^OI^O$ (O).

**All four blood groups are possible, each with probability $\tfrac14$.**
:::

## Sex determination

![A Punnett square for XX × XY: eggs all carry X, sperm carry X or Y; offspring XX, XX, XY, XY, so half are girls and half boys](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/sex-determination.svg)

Females are **XX** and make only X eggs; males are **XY** and make X and Y sperm in equal numbers. **The sperm decides the sex** of the child, and each pregnancy has a $\tfrac12$ chance of a boy.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Blaming the mother for the sex of a child.** The mother always gives an X. Whether the child is a boy or a girl depends on whether the father's sperm carries a Y or an X.
:::

## Sex-linked inheritance

Some genes are on the **X chromosome** but not on the shorter Y. These are **sex-linked**. Examples: **haemophilia** (blood does not clot properly) and **red-green colour blindness**. Both are caused by **recessive** alleles.

- A female needs **two** recessive alleles to be affected; with one, she is a **carrier** ($X^HX^h$).
- A male has only **one** X, so **one** recessive allele makes him affected ($X^hY$).

That is why these conditions are much more common in males.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A carrier woman ($X^HX^h$) marries a man with normal blood clotting ($X^HY$). Predict their children.**

Gametes: eggs $X^H$ or $X^h$; sperm $X^H$ or $Y$.

| Egg | Sperm | Child |
|---|---|---|
| $X^H$ | $X^H$ | $X^HX^H$: normal girl |
| $X^H$ | $Y$ | $X^HY$: normal boy |
| $X^h$ | $X^H$ | $X^HX^h$: carrier girl |
| $X^h$ | $Y$ | $X^hY$: **haemophiliac boy** |

Each **son** has a $\tfrac12$ chance of haemophilia; no daughter is affected, but each has a $\tfrac12$ chance of being a carrier.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Genotype checks before marriage.** Counselling services in Freetown and other cities explain exactly the AS × AS cross above to couples. Knowing that each child of two carriers has a 1 in 4 chance of SS, a 2 in 4 chance of being a carrier and a 1 in 4 chance of AA lets families make informed decisions and plan for early care, such as clinic follow-up, malaria prevention and folic acid for children with sickle cell disease.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. In guinea pigs, black fur (B) is dominant to white (b). Cross a homozygous black with a white guinea pig. Give the F₁ genotype and phenotype."}
BB × bb → all **Bb**, all **black**.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Two F₁ guinea pigs from question 1 are crossed. Give the F₂ phenotype ratio. If 24 young are born, how many are expected to be white?"}
Bb × Bb → 3 black : 1 white. Expected white $= \tfrac14 \times 24 = 6$.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. A man with blood group O marries a woman with blood group AB. What blood groups can their children have?"}
$I^OI^O \times I^AI^B$ → $I^AI^O$ (A) and $I^BI^O$ (B). Children can be **A or B** only, each with probability $\tfrac12$.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. An AA man marries an AS woman. Can any child have sickle cell disease?"}
AA × AS → AA, AA, AS, AS. **No**: no child can be SS. Half are expected to be carriers.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Why are more men than women colour-blind?"}
The allele is **recessive** and on the **X chromosome**. A man has one X, so one allele makes him colour-blind; a woman needs two, which is much less likely.
:::

## Key points

- **Segregation**: each gamete gets one allele of each gene. **Independent assortment**: different genes sort independently.
- Set out every cross fully: key, phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, Punnett square, ratio.
- Heterozygote × heterozygote → **3 : 1** phenotype ratio, **1 : 2 : 1** genotype ratio.
- **Test cross** with the homozygous recessive reveals an unknown genotype.
- **Codominance**: both alleles show (AS; $I^AI^B$). ABO has **multiple alleles**.
- **Sex**: XX female, XY male; the sperm decides. **Sex-linked** recessive conditions (haemophilia, colour blindness) are commoner in males.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE regularly sets a full genetic cross: sickle cell between two carriers, ABO blood groups, sex determination or a monohybrid cross with a 3 : 1 ratio, with a question on probability or expected numbers. Marks are given for each line of the working, so a correct answer without the gametes and Punnett square loses marks. Also learn Mendel's laws and the reasons he used peas.
:::
