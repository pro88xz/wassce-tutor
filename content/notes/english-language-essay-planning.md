Students who start writing the moment they choose a question often run out of ideas halfway, repeat themselves, or forget half the question. **Five minutes of planning prevents all three.** This lesson gives you a planning method that works for every kind of essay, shows you how to build paragraphs that score, and follows one question all the way from the plan to a complete model answer.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- choose the right question from the options
- break a question into every part that must be answered
- plan an essay in about five minutes
- write paragraphs with a topic sentence, development and a link
- write introductions and conclusions that earn marks

## Step 1: choose your question

You will be offered several questions. Choose by asking three things:

| Ask | If the answer is no… |
|---|---|
| Do I understand **exactly** what it asks? | skip it: a misread question can cost the whole content mark |
| Do I know the **format**? (letter, speech, article, report, story) | skip it unless you are sure |
| Can I think of **four or five points** right now? | skip it: ideas rarely appear once you start |

:::callout{type=tip}
Students often choose the question with the most impressive topic. Choose the one where all three answers are **yes**. A simple topic, well developed, beats an ambitious one done badly.
:::

## Step 2: break the question down

Underline every part. Each part must be answered.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
*Write an article for publication in a national newspaper on **the causes of flooding** in **Freetown** and **what can be done** to prevent it.*

| Part | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| **an article for a national newspaper** | a title, a readable but serious tone, the writer's name |
| **Freetown** | local causes and places, not floods in general |
| **the causes** | at least three developed causes |
| **what can be done** | at least three developed solutions |

An essay that describes a flood in moving detail but never offers solutions has answered **half** the question.
:::

## Step 3: the five-minute plan

On the rough-work page:

1. **Brainstorm**: write every idea that comes, in a few words. Don't judge yet.
2. **Choose**: tick the four to six strongest; cross out the rest.
3. **Order**: number them in a logical order: most important first, causes before solutions, or time order for a story.
4. **Add one detail** to each: an example, a fact, a place, a short story.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Plan for the flooding article:**

| Paragraph | Idea | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | every rainy season, families lose homes | water pouring into houses in Kroo Bay |
| 1 | cause: trees cut on the hills | soil washes down; hills above Regent |
| 2 | cause: rubbish blocks drains | plastic bags, gutters on Kissy Road |
| 3 | cause: building in waterways | houses in streams and on drainage paths |
| 4 | solution: replant and protect the hills | community tree-planting; stop hillside building |
| 5 | solution: clean and widen drains | monthly cleaning days; waste collection |
| 6 | solution: enforce building rules | relocate people safely, with support |
| Conclusion | everyone's responsibility; act before the next rains | call to action |
:::

## Step 4: build each paragraph with TEEL

| Part | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **T**opic sentence | states the paragraph's one idea | *The first cause of flooding is the stripping of trees from the hills.* |
| **E**xplanation | explains how or why | *Without roots to hold it, the soil loosens and slides downhill when the heavy rains come.* |
| **E**xample | makes it concrete | *Hillsides above Regent that were forest twenty years ago are now covered with houses.* |
| **L**ink | connects to the next idea or the question | *Once this soil reaches the city, it meets a second problem: blocked drains.* |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**One-sentence paragraphs** and **page-long paragraphs** both lose organisation marks. A paragraph is **one idea, fully developed**: usually four to seven sentences.
:::

## Openings and endings

**Introductions** that work:

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| a striking fact | *Every rainy season, hundreds of Freetown families watch the water pour into their homes.* |
| a short scene | *The rain began at midnight. By morning, the street had become a brown, rushing river.* |
| a question | *Must we wait for another disaster before we act?* |

**Conclusions** that work: a fresh summing-up, a **call to action**, a hopeful or thoughtful final line. Never just repeat *"In conclusion, as I said…"*.

## The plan becomes an essay

Here is the article written from the plan above. Notice how each paragraph does one job.

::::model{title="Freetown's floods are man-made: so is the solution" label="Model article"}
**FREETOWN'S FLOODS ARE MAN-MADE: SO IS THE SOLUTION**

Every rainy season, the same pictures fill our screens: mothers wading waist-deep through brown water in Kroo Bay, children carried on shoulders, furniture floating out of front doors. We call these floods "natural disasters". They are not. Most of the water that destroys our homes is sent there by our own actions, and that means our own actions can stop it.

The first cause is the stripping of trees from the hills that surround the city. Roots hold soil in place and slow the rain as it runs downhill; without them, the earth loosens and slides away whenever a heavy storm falls. Hillsides above Regent and Kamayama that were thick forest within living memory are now crowded with houses, and every storm carries tonnes of their soil into the valleys below.

When that soil and water reach the city, they meet a second problem: blocked drains. Plastic bags, bottles and food waste are thrown into gutters along Kissy Road and in markets across the city, until the drains are choked solid. Water that should flow quietly to the sea has nowhere to go but into the streets and the homes beside them.

The third cause is building in the paths that water naturally takes. Houses have been put up inside streams and on drainage channels, sometimes with permits that should never have been granted. When the rains come, the water simply returns to the route it has always used, through people's living rooms.

The solutions follow from the causes. First, the hills must be protected and replanted. Further building on steep slopes should be stopped, and communities, schools and churches could adopt a hillside and plant trees on it, with the Forestry Division supplying seedlings.

Secondly, our drains must be kept clear. A monthly cleaning day, properly organised in every ward, would help, but it will only work if rubbish is collected regularly, so that people have somewhere to put it other than the gutter. Traders, too, have a part to play: a market that sweeps its waste into the drain in the evening floods its own stalls in the morning.

Thirdly, building rules must be enforced. No new house should be allowed in a waterway, and officials who grant permits for such places should answer for it. Families already living in the most dangerous spots should be helped to move to safer land, with support to rebuild, not simply evicted and left to settle somewhere just as risky.

None of this is beyond us, and none of it needs to wait for foreign help. Flooding is not a punishment from the sky; it is the result of choices we make every day, on the hills, in the markets and in the offices that approve our buildings. If we make different choices before the next rains, the pictures on our screens could be very different too.

**Fatmata Kargbo**
::::

What earns the marks:

- **Content**: three developed causes and three developed solutions, all about **Freetown**, matching the question exactly.
- **Organisation**: a title and writer's name (article format), an introduction that hooks, one idea per paragraph, links (*first*, *secondly*, *when that soil… they meet a second problem*), a conclusion with a call to action.
- **Expression**: precise, vivid words (*wading*, *choked solid*, *crowded*), varied sentences, a serious but readable tone.
- **Length**: about 490 words, comfortably over the minimum, with nothing padded.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Break down: 'Write a speech to be delivered at your school's speech day on the advantages and disadvantages of social media for students.'"}
- **Format**: a speech (vocative, introduction of self and topic, *thank you for listening*)
- **Audience**: speech day: principal, staff, parents, guests, students
- **Part 1**: advantages of social media **for students**
- **Part 2**: disadvantages **for students**
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Write a TEEL paragraph on one disadvantage of social media for students."}
*One serious disadvantage of social media is the time it steals from study. A student who opens WhatsApp "for five minutes" can easily lose an hour to group chats, videos and arguments about football, and that hour is usually taken from homework or sleep. Many teachers in Freetown report students arriving at school tired and unprepared after late nights online. If social media can take our sleep, it can also take our grades.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Improve this conclusion: 'In conclusion, as I said, social media has advantages and disadvantages.'"}
*Social media is a tool, and like a cutlass it can clear a farm or injure the hand that holds it. Used with discipline, it can connect us and teach us; used without control, it can quietly empty our time. The choice, every evening, is ours.*
:::

## Key points

- **Choose** the question you understand, whose format you know, and for which you already have ideas.
- **Break it down** and answer every part.
- Spend about **five minutes** on a plan: brainstorm, choose, order, add detail.
- Build paragraphs with **TEEL**: topic sentence, explanation, example, link.
- **Open** with impact and **close** with a fresh summing-up, a call to action or a reflection.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Organisation is worth **10 marks**, and good planning also lifts your content and expression marks. Examiners can see when an essay was planned: it answers the whole question, every paragraph has a purpose, and it ends strongly instead of simply stopping.
:::
