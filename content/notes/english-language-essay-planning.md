Students who start writing the moment they choose a question often run out of ideas halfway, repeat themselves, or forget part of the question. Five minutes of planning prevents all three. This lesson shows a planning method that works for every kind of essay, and how to build strong paragraphs from the plan.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- choose the right question from the options
- break a question into its parts
- plan an essay in five minutes
- write a paragraph with a topic sentence, development and a link
- write an introduction and a conclusion that earn marks

## Choosing your question

You will be offered several questions. Choose by asking:

1. **Do I understand exactly what it asks?**
2. **Do I know the format?** (letter, speech, article, report, story)
3. **Can I think of four or five points right now?**

Pick the question where the answer to all three is *yes*.

## Breaking down the question

Underline every part. Each part must be answered.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
*Write an article for publication in a national newspaper on **the causes of flooding** in **Freetown** and **what can be done** to prevent it.*

Parts:

- **Format**: an article for a **national newspaper** (title, formal-but-readable tone, writer's name)
- **Topic**: flooding in **Freetown** (not floods in general)
- **Part 1**: causes
- **Part 2**: what can be done

An essay that describes the 2017 mudslide in moving detail but never suggests solutions has answered only half the question.
:::

## The five-minute plan

On rough paper:

1. **Brainstorm**: write every idea that comes, in a few words.
2. **Group and choose**: pick the four or five strongest; cross out the rest.
3. **Order**: put them in a logical order (most important first, or causes before solutions, or time order for a story).
4. **Add one detail** to each: an example, a fact, a short story.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Plan for the flooding article:**

- Intro: rainy season, homes lost, people afraid
- Cause 1: building on hillsides, cutting trees → soil washes down
- Cause 2: rubbish blocks drains and gutters
- Cause 3: houses built in waterways
- Solution 1: replant trees, stop hillside building
- Solution 2: regular drain cleaning and rubbish collection
- Solution 3: enforce building rules, relocate people safely
- Conclusion: everyone's responsibility; act before next rains
:::

## Building a strong paragraph

Use **TEEL**:

| Part | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **T**opic sentence | states the paragraph's one main idea | *"The first cause of flooding is the stripping of trees from the hills."* |
| **E**xplanation | explains it | *"Without roots to hold it, the soil loosens and slides down when the heavy rains come."* |
| **E**xample or evidence | makes it real | *"Hillsides above Regent and Kamayama were once forest; today they are crowded with houses."* |
| **L**ink | connects to the next idea or back to the question | *"Once this soil reaches the city, it meets a second problem: blocked drains."* |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**One-sentence paragraphs** and **page-long paragraphs** both lose organisation marks. A paragraph is one idea, fully developed: usually four to seven sentences.
:::

## Introductions and conclusions

**A good introduction** does two jobs: it catches the reader's attention and it tells them what the piece is about. You can open with:

- a striking fact: *"Every rainy season, hundreds of Freetown families watch water pour into their homes."*
- a short scene: *"The rain began at midnight. By morning, the street had become a river."*
- a question: *"Must we wait for another disaster before we act?"*

**A good conclusion** does not just repeat. It may:

- sum up the main idea in a fresh sentence
- call the reader to action (articles, speeches)
- end a story with a resolution or reflection

:::callout{type=local-example}
Strong writers make points **concrete**. *"Pollution is bad for the environment"* is general. *"Plastic bags from the market choke the gutters along Kissy Road until the water has nowhere to go but into people's homes"* is concrete, local and memorable, and it earns content and expression marks.
:::

## Key points

- Choose the question you **understand**, whose **format** you know, and for which you have **ideas**.
- **Break down** the question and answer every part.
- Spend about **five minutes** on a plan: brainstorm, choose, order, add details.
- Build paragraphs with **TEEL**: topic sentence, explanation, example, link.
- Open with impact; close with a fresh summary, a call to action or a resolution.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Organisation is worth **10 marks** and good planning also raises your content marks. Examiners can see when an essay was planned: it answers the whole question, every paragraph has a purpose, and it ends strongly instead of stopping.
:::

## Quick check

1. Name the four parts of a TEEL paragraph.
2. The question asks for *"the advantages and disadvantages of mobile phones for students"*. How many parts must you answer?
3. Write a striking opening sentence for an essay on youth unemployment.
4. What is wrong with a conclusion that says *"In conclusion, as I said, there are many causes of flooding"*?

**Answers:** 1. Topic sentence, explanation, example, link. 2. Two: advantages and disadvantages. 3. Example: *"Every morning, thousands of young graduates in Freetown wake up with certificates in their bags and nowhere to go."* 4. It only repeats; it adds no fresh summary, call to action or reflection.
