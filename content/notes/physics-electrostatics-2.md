How strong is the push between two charges? Coulomb's law gives the answer, and the idea of an electric field explains how one charge "feels" another across empty space. This lesson also covers lightning, lightning conductors and the uses of static electricity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state and use Coulomb's law
- define electric field intensity and draw field patterns
- define electric potential and potential difference
- explain charge distribution, point action, lightning and the lightning conductor

## Coulomb's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Coulomb's law:** the force between two point charges is **directly proportional to the product of the charges** and **inversely proportional to the square of the distance** between them:

$$F = \dfrac{k\,q_1 q_2}{r^2}, \qquad k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2\ \text{C}^{-2}$$
:::

$\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}$ F m⁻¹ is the **permittivity of free space**. Like gravity, this is an **inverse-square law**: doubling the distance makes the force four times smaller.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Two charges of +2 μC and −3 μC are 0.3 m apart in air. Find the force between them.**

$$F = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 2 \times 10^{-6} \times 3 \times 10^{-6}}{0.3^2} = \dfrac{0.054}{0.09} = 0.6\ \text{N}$$

The charges are opposite, so the force is **attractive**.
:::

## Electric field

:::callout{type=definition}
An **electric field** is a region in which a charge experiences a force. The **electric field intensity** $E$ at a point is the **force per unit positive charge** placed there:

$$E = \dfrac{F}{q} \quad (\text{N C}^{-1})$$

For a point charge $Q$: $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$.
:::

![Electric field lines point radially outwards from an isolated positive charge, and run from a positive charge to a nearby negative charge](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/electric-field-lines.svg)

*Figure 1. Electric field patterns. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Rules for field lines:

- they start on **positive** charges and end on **negative** charges
- they **never cross**
- they are **closer together** where the field is **stronger**
- between two parallel charged plates the field is **uniform** (equally spaced parallel lines), and $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$

## Electric potential and potential difference

:::callout{type=definition}
The **electric potential** at a point is the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point. The **potential difference (p.d.)** between two points is the work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to the other:

$$V = \dfrac{W}{q} \quad (\text{volt: } 1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J C}^{-1})$$
:::

For a point charge: $V = \dfrac{kQ}{r}$ (a scalar, so potentials simply add).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How much work is done in moving a charge of 5 μC through a p.d. of 200 V?**

$$W = qV = 5 \times 10^{-6} \times 200 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{J}$$
:::

## Charge distribution and point action

On a charged conductor, charge lives on the **outside surface** only, and it gathers most densely at **sharp points** (where the surface curves most). The very strong field at a sharp point can ionise the nearby air: ions of opposite charge are attracted to the point and neutralise it, while like ions are repelled, forming an "electric wind". This is **point action** (corona discharge).

## Lightning and the lightning conductor

Clouds become charged as ice crystals and water droplets collide in strong air currents; the base of a thundercloud is usually **negative**. When the charge is large enough, the air breaks down and a huge spark — **lightning** — passes between cloud and ground (or between clouds). The rapid heating of the air produces **thunder**.

A **lightning conductor** is a thick copper strip running from **sharp spikes** on top of a tall building down to a metal plate buried in the **earth**:

1. By **point action**, the spikes release charge that partly neutralises the cloud, reducing the chance of a strike.
2. If lightning does strike, the thick copper provides an **easy, low-resistance path** for the charge to flow safely to earth instead of through the building.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Telecommunication masts, church towers and tall buildings in Freetown carry lightning conductors. During a thunderstorm, it is safer to stay inside a building or a car than to shelter under a lone tree, which can be struck.
:::

## Uses and hazards of static electricity

| Uses | Hazards |
|---|---|
| photocopiers and laser printers | sparks near petrol vapour can cause explosions |
| electrostatic paint spraying (even coating) | shocks from car doors and synthetic clothes |
| electrostatic precipitators removing smoke from chimneys | damage to electronic components |
| crop spraying | lightning strikes |

Fuel tankers are **earthed** (with a conducting strap or cable) before unloading, so charge cannot build up and cause a spark.

## Key points

- Coulomb's law: $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}$, $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$ N m² C⁻².
- Field intensity $E = F/q$; field lines go from + to −, never cross; uniform between plates ($E = V/d$).
- Potential difference $V = W/q$; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹.
- Charge collects at sharp points; point action protects buildings through lightning conductors.

## Quick check

1. If the distance between two charges is halved, what happens to the force between them?
2. What is the unit of electric field intensity?
3. Give one use of static electricity.

**Answers:** 1. It becomes four times larger. 2. N C⁻¹ (equivalently V m⁻¹). 3. For example: photocopiers, paint spraying or smoke precipitators.
