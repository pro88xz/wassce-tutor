Is light a stream of tiny particles or a wave? Newton thought particles; Huygens thought waves. The answer turned out to be "both", but the wave picture explains how light interferes, diffracts and can be polarised.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the speed of light and describe how its value is known
- outline the particle (corpuscular) and wave theories of light
- describe evidence that light is a wave (interference, diffraction, polarisation)
- use the double-slit formula to find the wavelength of light

## The speed of light

Light travels at about **$3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹** in a vacuum (and nearly as fast in air). It is slower in water (about $2.25 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹) and in glass (about $2.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹).

Light from the Sun takes about **8 minutes** to reach the Earth; from the Moon, about 1.3 s. Measurements by Rømer (using Jupiter's moons) and Fizeau (using a rotating toothed wheel) first gave its value.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How long does light take to travel from the Sun to the Earth, 1.5 × 10¹¹ m?**

$$t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{1.5 \times 10^{11}}{3.0 \times 10^{8}} = 500\ \text{s} \approx 8.3\ \text{minutes}$$
:::

## Theories of light

| Corpuscular (particle) theory — Newton | Wave theory — Huygens |
|---|---|
| light is a stream of tiny particles (corpuscles) | light is a wave spreading out from the source |
| explains straight-line travel and reflection | explains reflection, refraction, **interference**, **diffraction**, **polarisation** |
| predicts light travels **faster** in glass than in air | predicts light travels **slower** in glass — which **experiment confirms** |

In the 20th century the **photoelectric effect** showed that light also behaves as particles (**photons**) when it delivers energy. Light shows **wave–particle duality** (covered in modern physics).

## Evidence that light is a wave

### 1. Interference: Young's double-slit experiment

Light from a single narrow slit falls on two very close parallel slits. The two slits act as **coherent sources**. On a screen beyond them appear **alternate bright and dark bands (fringes)**:

- **bright fringes**: constructive interference (path difference $0, \lambda, 2\lambda, \ldots$)
- **dark fringes**: destructive interference (path difference $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}, \tfrac{3\lambda}{2}, \ldots$)

Only waves can cancel each other like this. The fringe spacing $y$ is

$$y = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$$

where $D$ is the slit-to-screen distance and $d$ the separation of the slits.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**In a double-slit experiment the slits are 0.5 mm apart and the screen is 1.0 m away. The bright fringes are 1.2 mm apart. Find the wavelength of the light.**

$$\lambda = \dfrac{yd}{D} = \dfrac{1.2 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.5 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 600\ \text{nm}$$

(orange light).
:::

### 2. Diffraction

Light passing through a very narrow slit spreads out, and the edges of shadows show faint fringes. Because the wavelength of light is so small (400–700 nm), diffraction is noticeable only with very narrow slits — which is why light **appears** to travel in straight lines. A CD or DVD splits light into colours by diffraction from its closely spaced tracks.

### 3. Polarisation

Light passed through one Polaroid filter becomes plane-polarised; a second Polaroid at 90° blocks it completely. Only **transverse** waves can be polarised, so light is a **transverse** wave.

## Wavelengths and colours of visible light

| Colour | Approximate wavelength |
|---|---|
| Red | 700 nm |
| Orange | 620 nm |
| Yellow | 580 nm |
| Green | 530 nm |
| Blue | 470 nm |
| Violet | 400 nm |

(1 nm = $10^{-9}$ m.) The wavelength decides the **colour**; the frequency is $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$ — about $4.3 \times 10^{14}$ Hz for red and $7.5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz for violet.

## Key points

- Speed of light in a vacuum: $3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹; slower in water and glass.
- Wave theory explains interference, diffraction and polarisation; light also behaves as photons (duality).
- Young's double slit: bright and dark fringes; $y = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$.
- Polarisation shows that light is transverse.

## Quick check

1. Which phenomenon shows that light is a transverse wave?
2. State the speed of light in a vacuum.
3. If the slit separation in a double-slit experiment is halved, what happens to the fringe spacing?

**Answers:** 1. Polarisation. 2. $3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹. 3. It doubles ($y \propto 1/d$).
