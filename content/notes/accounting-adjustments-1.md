**Accruals and prepayments** are year-end adjustments that make the final accounts follow the **accruals (matching) concept**: each year is charged with the expenses **incurred** and credited with the income **earned**, whatever cash was paid or received. An **accrued expense** is an expense **owing** at the year end (a current liability); a **prepaid expense** is an expense **paid in advance** for the next year (a current asset). Income can likewise be **accrued** (earned but not received) or **received in advance**. This lesson shows how to calculate each adjustment and record it in the ledger and the final accounts.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why accruals and prepayments are needed
- calculate the expense or income for the year after adjustment
- record accruals and prepayments in ledger accounts
- show the adjustments in the income statement and statement of financial position
- deal with income accrued and income received in advance

## The rule

$$\text{Expense for the year} = \text{Amount paid} + \text{Accrued (owing)} - \text{Prepaid (in advance)}$$

$$\text{Income for the year} = \text{Amount received} + \text{Accrued (owed to us)} - \text{Received in advance}$$

| Item | Income statement | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| **Accrued expense** | **added** to the expense | **current liability** |
| **Prepaid expense** | **deducted** from the expense | **current asset** |
| **Accrued income** | **added** to the income | **current asset** |
| **Income received in advance** | **deducted** from the income | **current liability** |

## Worked example: expenses

![A timeline for 2026 showing rent paid January to November with December owing as an accrual of 1,000, and insurance of 6,000 paid on 1 October with nine months, 4,500, paid in advance for 2027](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/accruals-prepayments.svg)

*Figure 1. Accruals and prepayments on a timeline. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Rent is NLe 1,000 a month. By 31 December 2026, NLe 11,000 had been paid. Insurance of NLe 6,000 was paid on 1 October 2026 for the twelve months to 30 September 2027.**

- Rent charge $= 11\,000 + 1\,000 = 12\,000$; **accrued rent** of 1,000 is a current liability.
- Insurance for 2026 covers October–December: $\frac{3}{12} \times 6\,000 = 1\,500$.
- **Prepaid insurance** $= 6\,000 - 1\,500 = 4\,500$, a current asset.

The ledger accounts after the year-end adjustments:

![Rent account with bank 11,000 and accrued c/d 1,000 on the debit side, income statement 12,000 on the credit side and accrued b/d 1,000 brought down as a credit; insurance account with bank 6,000 on the debit side, income statement 1,500 and prepaid c/d 4,500 on the credit side and prepaid b/d 4,500 brought down as a debit](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/rent-insurance-accounts.svg)

*Figure 2. Rent and insurance accounts with an accrual and a prepayment. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*
:::

The accrued rent is **brought down as a credit** (a liability) and the prepaid insurance **as a debit** (an asset). Next year, these opening balances make sure that the payments are charged to the correct year.

## Worked example: income

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A business sublets part of its shop for NLe 800 a month. During 2026 it received NLe 8,800. It also received commission of NLe 3,000, of which NLe 600 relates to 2027.**

- Rent receivable for the year $= 12 \times 800 = 9\,600$; received $8\,800$; **accrued income** $= 800$ (current asset).
- Commission for 2026 $= 3\,000 - 600 = 2\,400$; **commission received in advance** $= 600$ (current liability).
:::

## Adjustments in the final accounts

In a trial balance question, the adjustments are listed **below** the trial balance. Make each adjustment **twice**:

1. in the **income statement** (adjust the expense or income), and
2. in the **statement of financial position** (show the asset or liability).

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many Sierra Leonean landlords ask for **rent a year or two in advance**. A shop that pays NLe 24,000 on 1 July for two years has, at its 31 December year end, used only six months (NLe 6,000). The other NLe 18,000 is a **prepayment**, a current asset, not an expense of this year.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
An **accrued expense** is a **liability**, not an asset: the business **owes** it. A **prepaid expense** is an **asset**: the business has paid for a benefit it has **not yet received**. Mixing these up puts both the profit and the statement of financial position wrong.
:::

## Key points

- Accruals and prepayments apply the **accruals (matching) concept**.
- **Expense = paid + accrued − prepaid**; **income = received + accrued − received in advance**.
- **Accrued expenses** and **income in advance**: current **liabilities**. **Prepaid expenses** and **accrued income**: current **assets**.
- Each adjustment affects **both** final statements.

## Quick check

1. Wages paid were NLe 40,000 and NLe 2,500 is owing at the year end. Find the wages charge.
2. Where does prepaid rent appear in the statement of financial position?
3. Insurance of NLe 4,800 was paid on 1 April for twelve months. The year ends on 31 December. Find the prepayment.
4. What is accrued income? Give an example.
5. Why are accruals and prepayments needed?

**Answers:**

1. $40\,000 + 2\,500 = 42\,500$: NLe 42,500.
2. As a current asset.
3. Nine months used (April–December), three months prepaid: $\frac{3}{12} \times 4\,800 = 1\,200$: NLe 1,200.
4. Income earned but not yet received at the year end, such as rent receivable owing from a tenant.
5. To apply the accruals concept, so each year is charged with the expenses incurred and credited with the income earned.
