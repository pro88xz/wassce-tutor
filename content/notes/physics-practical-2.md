The same dozen experiments return to the WASSCE practical paper year after year in different forms. Knowing how each is set up, what to measure, what to plot and what precautions to state is the surest way to a high practical score.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the standard WASSCE practical experiments in mechanics, optics, electricity and heat
- state for each the graph to plot and the quantity found from it
- calculate percentage errors and propagate them simply
- avoid the commonest mistakes in practical write-ups

## The standard experiments

| Experiment | Measure | Plot | Deduce |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple pendulum** | length $l$, time for 20 oscillations | $T^2$ against $l$ | $g = 4\pi^2/\text{gradient}$ |
| **Loaded spring (Hooke's law)** | load $F$, extension $e$ | $F$ against $e$ (or $e$ against $F$) | spring constant $k$ |
| **Oscillating spring** | mass $m$, period $T$ | $T^2$ against $m$ | $k = 4\pi^2/\text{gradient}$ |
| **Principle of moments (metre rule)** | balance distances | e.g. $1/d$ against $m$ | mass of the rule or unknown mass |
| **Refraction (glass block)** | angles $i$ and $r$ | $\sin i$ against $\sin r$ | refractive index $n$ = gradient |
| **Converging lens** | object distance $u$, image distance $v$ | $1/v$ against $1/u$ (or $uv$ against $u + v$) | focal length $f$ |
| **Concave mirror** | $u$ and $v$ (no-parallax method) | $1/v$ against $1/u$ | focal length $f$ |
| **Ohm's law / resistance** | p.d. $V$, current $I$ | $V$ against $I$ | resistance $R$ = gradient |
| **Resistance of a wire** | length $l$, resistance $R$ | $R$ against $l$ | resistivity $\rho$ = gradient × $A$ |
| **Metre bridge** | balance length $l$ | e.g. $1/l$ against $1/R$ | unknown resistance |
| **Potentiometer** | balance lengths | — | ratio of e.m.f.s |
| **Cooling curve** | temperature θ, time $t$ | θ against $t$ | rate of cooling, melting point |
| **Specific heat capacity** | masses, temperatures | — | $c$ from heat balance |
| **Resonance tube** | resonance lengths, fork frequency | $L$ against $1/f$ | speed of sound, end correction |

## Worked example: resistance of a wire

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A student measures the resistance $R$ of different lengths $l$ of a wire of diameter 0.40 mm and plots $R$ against $l$. The gradient is 8.0 Ω m⁻¹. Find the resistivity of the wire.**

Area $A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.20 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 1.26 \times 10^{-7}$ m².

Since $R = \dfrac{\rho}{A}l$, gradient $= \dfrac{\rho}{A}$:

$$\rho = 8.0 \times 1.26 \times 10^{-7} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \Omega\ \text{m}$$

(close to nichrome).
:::

## Errors and uncertainties

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Absolute error (uncertainty)**: the possible error in a reading, usually half the smallest scale division (e.g. ±0.05 cm on a metre rule read to 0.1 cm).
- **Relative (fractional) error** $= \dfrac{\text{absolute error}}{\text{measured value}}$.
- **Percentage error** $= \text{relative error} \times 100\%$.
:::

**Combining errors (simple rules):**

- When quantities are **added or subtracted**, add their **absolute** errors.
- When quantities are **multiplied or divided**, add their **percentage** errors.
- When a quantity is **raised to a power**, multiply its percentage error by the power.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The length of a pendulum is measured as (80.0 ± 0.1) cm and its period as (1.80 ± 0.02) s. Estimate the percentage error in g, where $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2}$.**

Percentage error in $l$ $= \dfrac{0.1}{80.0} \times 100\% = 0.125\%$.

Percentage error in $T$ $= \dfrac{0.02}{1.80} \times 100\% = 1.11\%$, so in $T^2$: $2 \times 1.11 = 2.22\%$.

Percentage error in $g$ $\approx 0.125 + 2.22 = 2.3\%$. The timing dominates — which is why we time many oscillations.
:::

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. Plotting the axes the **wrong way round**.
2. Choosing **awkward scales** (3 or 7 units per square) or a graph that uses only a corner of the paper.
3. **Joining the dots** instead of drawing a line of best fit.
4. Finding the gradient with a **small triangle** or from two **table values** that are not on the line.
5. Forgetting **units** in headings, gradients and final answers.
6. Giving **vague precautions**.
7. Recording readings with **inconsistent precision**.
8. Not **converting units** before calculating (cm to m, g to kg, minutes to seconds).

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many schools in Sierra Leone share limited apparatus, so students may get only one session per experiment before the exam. Practise the write-up structure — table, graph, gradient, deduction, precautions — on past practical questions at home, so that the session itself is spent measuring carefully.
:::

## Key points

- Learn the standard experiments: what to measure, what to plot and what the gradient gives.
- Percentage errors add for products and quotients; multiply by the power for powers.
- Timing errors usually dominate; reduce them by timing many oscillations.
- Avoid the routine mistakes: axes, scales, best-fit line, units, precautions.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The practical paper typically asks you to **set up**, **take readings**, **tabulate**, **plot**, **find a gradient**, **deduce a physical quantity** and **state two precautions**. Some questions add a short theory part ("define…", "state the law verified"). Error calculations may appear in theory questions on measurement.
:::

## Quick check

1. In the glass-block experiment, what graph do you plot and what does its gradient give?
2. A length of 50.0 cm has an absolute error of 0.1 cm. What is the percentage error?
3. If the percentage error in T is 1%, what is the percentage error in T²?

**Answers:** 1. $\sin i$ against $\sin r$; the gradient is the refractive index. 2. $0.1 \div 50.0 \times 100\% = 0.2\%$. 3. 2%.
