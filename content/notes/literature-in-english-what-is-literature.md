A grandmother in Kailahun tells a story about a clever spider and a greedy king, and the children laugh, then fall quiet when the king gets what he deserves. A student in Bo reads Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* and feels Okonkwo's fear of looking weak as if it were her own. A crowd at a funeral in Freetown sings a song that turns grief into something they can carry. All three are **literature**. This lesson explains what literature is, what makes a piece of writing *literary*, and how literature differs from the other writing you read every day.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define literature and explain where the word comes from
- state the main features that make a text literary
- distinguish literary writing from non-literary writing
- distinguish oral literature from written literature, with examples
- explain the difference between the literal and the figurative meaning of words

## What is literature?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Literature** is imaginative writing, or imaginative spoken composition, in which language is used with skill and care to express human experience: what people feel, think, fear, hope and do. Its main forms are **prose**, **drama** and **poetry**.
:::

The word comes from the Latin *littera*, "a letter of the alphabet", so at first it simply meant "writing". Today it means something narrower. A phone bill and a novel are both writing, but only the novel is literature. What separates them is not the paper or the ink, but **what the writer is doing with language** and **why**.

Three ideas sit inside every good definition:

1. **Imagination.** Literature creates a world, a character or a feeling. Even when it is based on real events, the writer selects, shapes and invents.
2. **Artistic use of language.** Words are chosen for their sound, their rhythm and their associations, not only for their dictionary meaning.
3. **Human experience.** Literature is about life: love, ambition, injustice, death, family, war, faith, change.

## Features of a literary text

| Feature | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Imaginative (fictional) content** | People, places or events are created or reshaped | A village, a family and a war invented by a novelist |
| **Figurative language** | Words mean more than their literal sense | *"My heart is a drum at night"* |
| **Form and structure** | The text is shaped as a novel, a play or a poem, with chapters, scenes or stanzas | Acts and scenes in a play |
| **Universal themes** | Ideas that matter to readers anywhere, in any age | Pride, greed, loyalty, the cost of ambition |
| **Emotional and aesthetic appeal** | It moves the reader and gives pleasure through its beauty or skill | The sound of a well-made line of verse |
| **More than one meaning** | Readers can interpret it in different, defensible ways | Is the hero brave, or only proud? |
| **Lasting value** | It can be reread with fresh meaning years later | Shakespeare's plays, still performed after 400 years |

Not every literary text shows every feature equally, but a text that has none of them is not literature.

:::callout{type=tip}
When a WASSCE question asks you to "identify the features of literature", give a feature **and** a short example or explanation for each. A bare list such as "imagination, language, theme" earns little.
:::

## Literary and non-literary writing

Most of what you read each day is **non-literary**: textbooks, newspaper reports, instructions, letters from the bank, government notices. Its job is to inform or instruct, so its language is **literal**, direct and exact. One meaning is the goal.

Literary writing does something different. It wants you to **feel and imagine**, so its language is often **figurative** and open to interpretation.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The same event, written two ways.**

*Non-literary (a news report):* "Heavy rainfall on Tuesday caused flooding in parts of Freetown. Several houses at Kroo Bay were damaged and residents moved to higher ground."

*Literary (a poem):*
> The sky broke open over Kroo Bay,\
> and the sea came walking up the hill\
> to borrow our beds.

**What changes.** The report gives facts: when, where, what happened. The poem gives an *experience*. The sea is **personified** ("came walking", "to borrow our beds"), which makes the flood feel like an uninvited guest and shows how helpless the people are. There is no date and no number of houses, yet the reader feels more.
:::

| | Non-literary writing | Literary writing |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | To inform, instruct or record | To move, delight and reveal human experience |
| **Language** | Literal, plain, exact | Figurative, rich in imagery and sound |
| **Meaning** | One clear meaning | Often more than one possible meaning |
| **Examples** | Textbook, report, manual, notice | Novel, short story, play, poem |

Some writing sits between the two. A well-written **biography**, **autobiography** or **essay** tells the truth about real people but uses literary skill to do it. This is often called **literary non-fiction**.

## Literal and figurative meaning

:::callout{type=definition}
The **literal** (denotative) meaning of a word is its plain dictionary meaning. Its **figurative** (connotative) meaning is the extra sense, feeling or association it carries in context.
:::

*"The minister is a lion"* is false literally (he is not an animal). Figuratively it says he is brave, powerful, perhaps dangerous. Reading literature well means noticing these extra meanings and asking **why** the writer chose them. You will study the figures of speech in detail in later lessons.

## Oral and written literature

Literature did not begin with books. Long before writing reached most of West Africa, communities composed, performed and remembered their literature by mouth.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Oral literature** (sometimes called **orature**) is literature composed and passed on by word of mouth: folktales, myths, legends, proverbs, riddles, songs, chants, praise poetry and epics. **Written literature** is composed and preserved in writing: novels, short stories, printed plays and poems.
:::

| Oral literature | Written literature |
|---|---|
| Performed for a live audience, often with music, gesture and response | Read privately or studied from a fixed text |
| Changes a little with each teller and each performance | The words are fixed once printed |
| Usually no single known author; it belongs to the community | Usually has a named author |
| Examples: Mende and Temne folktales, Krio proverbs, funeral dirges, praise songs at a chief's installation | Examples: Syl Cheney-Coker's novel *The Last Harmattan of Alusine Dunbar*, Wole Soyinka's play *The Lion and the Jewel* |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Think of a folktale told at night in your village or compound. The teller changes voices for each animal, the listeners answer with a chorus, and the story ends with a lesson about greed, pride or patience. That is literature: it has **characters**, a **plot**, a **setting**, a **theme** and a **moral**, all delivered through skilful use of language. Many African novelists and playwrights, Achebe among them, built proverbs and folktales into their written work.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**"Literature means books."** Oral literature is literature too, and WASSCE objective questions often test terms such as *folktale*, *legend*, *myth*, *dirge* and *praise poetry*. Do not write that literature "must be written".
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Define literature in one sentence."}
Literature is imaginative writing or spoken composition in which language is used artistically to express human experience, in the forms of prose, drama and poetry.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Is a science textbook literature? Give a reason."}
No. Its purpose is to inform, its language is literal and exact, and it aims at one clear meaning. It is non-literary writing.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give two differences between oral and written literature."}
Any two: oral literature is passed on by word of mouth and performed, written literature is fixed in print; oral literature changes with each performance, written literature does not; oral literature usually has no known single author, written literature usually does.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. 'Her words were knives.' Give the literal and the figurative meaning."}
Literally the statement is impossible: words are not blades. Figuratively, her words were cruel and deeply hurtful, as painful as being cut.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Name four forms of oral literature."}
Any four: folktale, myth, legend, proverb, riddle, song, chant, dirge, praise poetry, epic.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Figurative language**: words used to mean more than their literal sense.
- **Universal theme**: an idea that matters to readers anywhere, in any age.
- **Genre**: a type of literature: prose, drama or poetry.
- **Aesthetic appeal**: the pleasure a work gives through its beauty or skill.
:::

## Key points

- **Literature** is imaginative writing or spoken composition that uses language artistically to express human experience.
- Its features include imagination, **figurative language**, form, universal themes, emotional appeal, more than one meaning and lasting value.
- **Non-literary** writing informs in literal language; **literary** writing moves and reveals in figurative language.
- **Oral literature** (folktales, proverbs, songs, praise poetry) is passed on by mouth; **written literature** is fixed in print.
- The three genres are **prose**, **drama** and **poetry**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Paper 1 (objective questions) tests the meaning of literary terms directly: what literature is, the difference between oral and written forms, and terms such as *folktale*, *legend* and *figurative*. Every essay in Papers 2 and 3 also rewards students who can explain **how** a writer's language creates meaning, so the difference between literal and figurative meaning in this lesson is a skill you will use in every answer.
:::
