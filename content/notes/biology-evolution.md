:::callout{type=definition}
**Evolution** is the gradual change in the inherited characteristics of a population over many generations, which over very long periods has produced the huge variety of life on Earth.
:::

It is happening around us now: malaria parasites that no longer respond to old drugs, mosquitoes that survive insecticide on bed nets, bacteria that resist antibiotics. Biology explains these changes with **natural selection**, the idea published by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in 1858. This lesson explains the theories of evolution, the evidence for it, how natural selection works, and examples you can see today.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define evolution and distinguish it from changes within one lifetime
- state and compare the theories of Lamarck and Darwin
- explain natural selection step by step
- describe the evidence for evolution
- explain examples of natural selection, including resistance and sickle cell

## Two theories

### Lamarck: use and disuse

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1809) proposed that:

1. organs that are **used** a lot become larger and stronger; organs **not used** shrink (**use and disuse**);
2. these changes, acquired during life, are **passed on** to offspring (**inheritance of acquired characteristics**).

His famous example: giraffes stretched their necks to reach high leaves, so their necks grew longer and their young were born with longer necks.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Using Lamarck's idea as the correct explanation.** Characteristics acquired during life are **not** inherited, because they do not change the DNA in the gametes. A farmer's strong arms are not passed on. Lamarck's theory has been **rejected**; Darwin's is accepted.
:::

### Darwin: natural selection

Charles Darwin, after his voyage on HMS *Beagle* (1831–1836), and Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same idea. Darwin set it out in *On the Origin of Species* (1859). In Darwin's explanation of the giraffe, giraffes **varied** in neck length; those with slightly longer necks reached more food in dry seasons, survived better and had more young, and these passed on the genes for longer necks.

## How natural selection works

![Six steps of natural selection: variation, over-production, struggle for existence, survival of the fittest, inheritance of useful alleles, and change over generations](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/natural-selection.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
1. **Variation**: members of a population differ, and some of the differences are genetic.
2. **Over-production**: organisms produce more offspring than can survive.
3. **Struggle for existence**: individuals compete for food, water, space and mates, and face predators and disease.
4. **Survival of the fittest**: individuals with variations that suit the environment are more likely to **survive and reproduce**. ("Fittest" means best adapted, not strongest.)
5. **Inheritance**: they pass the useful alleles to their offspring.
6. **Change**: over many generations, the useful alleles become **more common** in the population.
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
In an exam answer, use the key words in order: **variation → competition → survival → reproduction → pass on alleles → allele becomes more common**. Each step usually earns a mark.
:::

## Natural selection in action

### Insecticide resistance in mosquitoes

Insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor spraying killed most *Anopheles* mosquitoes. A few, by chance, carried a mutation that made them **resistant**. They survived, bred, and passed on the resistance allele. After several years of use, many mosquito populations in West Africa are now resistant to the pyrethroid insecticides on the nets. New nets combine more than one chemical to slow this down.

### Drug resistance in malaria parasites

Chloroquine once cured most malaria. Resistant *Plasmodium* parasites survived treatment and multiplied, and chloroquine stopped working across West Africa. Treatment changed to **artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs)**, which use two drugs together so that a parasite would need two rare mutations at once to survive.

### Antibiotic resistance in bacteria

When a patient stops a course of antibiotics early, the **most resistant** bacteria are the ones left alive. They multiply and can spread. This is why doctors say: **complete the full course**, and why antibiotics should not be bought and taken without a prescription.

### Sickle cell and malaria

Here natural selection acts on humans. People with **SS** often suffer severe illness, but **AS** carriers are **less likely to die of severe malaria** than AA people. Where malaria is common, AS people survive and have children more often, so the S allele stays common. Where there is no malaria, AS gives no advantage and the S allele is rare. This is why the sickle cell allele is common in West Africa.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Saying an organism "changes because it needs to".** Mosquitoes did not become resistant **because** they needed to. The resistant ones were already present, by chance mutation, and the insecticide **selected** them by killing the others. Individuals do not evolve; **populations** do.
:::

## Evidence for evolution

| Evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **fossil record** | fossils in older rock layers are simpler; we can trace gradual changes (e.g. the horse, from a small four-toed ancestor to the modern one-toed horse) |
| **comparative anatomy** | **homologous structures**: the human arm, bat wing, whale flipper and bird wing have the same **pentadactyl** (five-digit) bone plan, adapted to different uses, which points to a common ancestor |
| **vestigial organs** | reduced organs with little or no use, such as the human appendix and coccyx (tail bone) |
| **comparative embryology** | early embryos of fish, birds and mammals look very alike (e.g. all have gill pouches) |
| **molecular biology** | closely related species have more similar DNA and proteins |
| **geographical distribution** | species on isolated islands resemble those on the nearest mainland but have their own adaptations (Darwin's finches on the Galápagos) |
| **observed change** | resistance in insects, parasites and bacteria happens within years |

## Artificial selection

Humans also select, on purpose. Farmers choose the **best** plants or animals to breed from, generation after generation: rice with more grains, cassava resistant to mosaic disease, cattle that give more milk, chickens that lay more eggs. This is **artificial selection** (selective breeding). It works by the same principle as natural selection, but the selecting is done by people.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Improved rice in Sierra Leone.** Researchers and farmers have selected rice varieties suited to local conditions, such as varieties for inland valley swamps, and the NERICA ("New Rice for Africa") lines bred from African and Asian rice. Each variety came from repeated selection of the plants with the most useful traits (yield, drought or disease resistance, short growing time), which is artificial selection.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Define evolution."}
The gradual change in the **inherited** characteristics of a **population** over many generations.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. State two ways Darwin's theory differs from Lamarck's."}
Darwin: variation already exists and the environment selects the best adapted; acquired characteristics are not inherited. Lamarck: organisms change through use and disuse, and acquired changes are inherited.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Explain, using natural selection, how a population of bacteria becomes resistant to an antibiotic."}
Bacteria vary; a few have a mutation giving resistance. The antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria; the resistant ones survive and multiply, passing on the resistance allele. Over many generations most of the population is resistant.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. What are homologous structures? Give an example."}
Structures with the **same basic plan** but **different functions**, showing a common ancestor: e.g. the pentadactyl limb in a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Why is the sickle cell allele common in West Africa but rare in places without malaria?"}
AS carriers are protected against severe malaria, so in malaria areas they survive and reproduce more, keeping the S allele common. Without malaria there is no advantage, and SS is harmful, so the allele becomes rare.
:::

## Key points

- **Evolution** = change in the inherited characteristics of a population over generations.
- **Lamarck** (use and disuse; inheritance of acquired characteristics) is **rejected**.
- **Darwin**: variation, over-production, struggle, survival of the fittest, inheritance, change.
- Evidence: **fossils, homologous and vestigial structures, embryology, molecular similarity, distribution**.
- Natural selection today: **insecticide, drug and antibiotic resistance**; **sickle cell and malaria**.
- **Artificial selection** is selective breeding by people.
