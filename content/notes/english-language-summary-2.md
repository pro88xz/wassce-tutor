Finding the points is half of the summary. The other half is writing them so the examiner can give you every mark: one complete sentence per point, in your own words, with nothing extra. This lesson turns a list of points into a model summary answer.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- write each summary point as one clear, complete sentence
- express points in your own words without changing their meaning
- avoid introductions, conclusions and extra material
- check a summary answer before moving on

## The rules for writing the answer

1. **One point, one sentence.** If the question says *"in six sentences, one for each"*, write exactly six sentences, each carrying one point.
2. **Complete sentences.** Each sentence needs a subject and a finite verb. Notes and fragments lose marks.
3. **Your own words.** Change the passage's wording where you can, but **keep the meaning exact**.
4. **No introduction, no conclusion.** Begin with your first point. Do not write *"The writer says that there are many reasons…"*.
5. **No examples.** You cut them when you found the points; do not add them back.
6. **Number your sentences** if the question allows it; it helps the examiner see each point.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Lifting** is copying long stretches of the passage word for word. A few key words from the passage are acceptable (you cannot rename *"malaria"*), but whole lifted sentences show that you have not made a summary, and the examiner may withhold marks for expression.
:::

## From a note to a sentence

| Note on rough paper | Weak sentence | Good sentence |
|---|---|---|
| youths: farming low-status | Young people. | Young people see farming as work for the poor and uneducated. |
| no modern tools | Because of no tools, farming, hoe and cutlass for small plots. | Farmers lack modern tools, so they can cultivate only small areas. |
| bad roads: produce rots | The roads are bad and the produce rots and also the prices are low and the traders cheat them. | Bad roads prevent farmers from getting their produce to market before it spoils. |

The weak versions are either fragments or **overloaded**: the last one squeezes in extra ideas the question did not ask for, and two of them may not even be in the passage.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Joining two points in one sentence.** If you write *"Young people despise farming and farmers lack tools"*, the examiner may count it as one sentence carrying two points. You have used up two points but written only one sentence, and the next point may be missing. Keep them apart.
:::

## Putting it in your own words

Use these techniques, and keep checking that the meaning has not changed:

- **Synonyms**: *"decline"* → *"fall"*; *"inadequate"* → *"not enough"*.
- **Change the word class**: *"the destruction of forests"* → *"forests are destroyed"*.
- **Generalise a list**: *"rice, cassava, groundnuts and palm oil"* → *"food crops"*.
- **Restructure the sentence**: active to passive or the reverse.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Question:** In four sentences, one for each, state the measures the writer suggests for reducing road accidents in Sierra Leone.

**Points listed:** licence testing; speed limits enforced; vehicle checks; road-safety education.

**Answer:**

1. Drivers should pass a proper test before they are given a licence.
2. The police should enforce speed limits strictly.
3. Vehicles should be inspected regularly to make sure they are roadworthy.
4. Road users, including okada riders and pedestrians, should be taught about road safety.

Four sentences, four points, no introduction, no examples, in the student's own words.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
If the passage says *"commercial motorcyclists, popularly known as okada riders"*, you may use **okada riders**: it is the clearest name and the passage itself uses it. Changing every single word is not the aim. The aim is a **clear, accurate sentence** that is not a copy of the passage.
:::

## Checking your summary

Before moving on, check:

- [ ] the **number** of sentences matches the question
- [ ] each sentence has **one** point, of the **right kind**
- [ ] every sentence is **complete** (subject and verb)
- [ ] no introduction, conclusion or example
- [ ] spelling and tense are correct

## Key points

- Write **one point per complete sentence**, exactly as many as the question asks for.
- Use **your own words** where you can, but keep the meaning **exact**; a few key terms may stay.
- No **introduction**, **conclusion**, **examples** or **extra points**.
- **Check** number, completeness and accuracy before moving on.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The 30 summary marks reward **correct points** and **good expression**. Examiners penalise **lifting**, **fragments**, **irrelevant material** and **joined points**. A short, accurate, numbered list of complete sentences is the safest answer.
:::

## Quick check

1. Rewrite as a summary sentence: *"lack of drugs in clinics"*.
2. What is wrong with: *"Firstly, the writer explains that there are many reasons why students fail."*
3. Put in your own words: *"The scarcity of qualified teachers in rural schools is alarming."*
4. The question asks for five sentences. You have written six. What should you do?

**Answers:** 1. Clinics do not have enough medicines. 2. It is an introduction and states no point. 3. Rural schools have far too few trained teachers. 4. Check whether two sentences carry the same point or one is not of the right kind, and remove one.
