Finding the points is half the summary. The other half is writing them so that the examiner can give you every mark: **one complete sentence per point, in your own words, with nothing extra**. Many students lose marks at this stage with fragments, joined points, introductions and copied sentences. This lesson shows you how to turn a list of points into a model answer, with a full worked example and a passage to practise on.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- write each point as one clear, complete sentence
- put a point into your own words without changing its meaning
- avoid introductions, conclusions, examples and joined points
- check a summary answer quickly and reliably

## The six rules

1. **One point, one sentence.** If the question says *"in six sentences, one for each"*, write exactly six sentences, each with one point.
2. **Complete sentences.** Each needs a subject and a finite verb. Notes and fragments lose marks.
3. **Your own words**, as far as possible, but with the **meaning kept exact**.
4. **No introduction and no conclusion.** Start with your first point.
5. **No examples.** You cut them when you found the points; do not let them back in.
6. **Number your sentences.** It helps the examiner see each point.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Lifting** is copying long stretches of the passage word for word. Some key words from the passage are unavoidable (you cannot rename *"malaria"* or *"fertiliser"*), but copying whole sentences shows you have not summarised, and it costs marks for expression.
:::

## From note to sentence

| Note on rough paper | Weak sentence | What's wrong | Good sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| youths: farming low-status | Young people. | a fragment: no verb, no point | Young people regard farming as work for the poor. |
| old tools: small, hard | Because of the hoe and cutlass that their grandparents used they can only farm a small plot. | lifted, and starts with *because* (not a sentence) | Outdated tools make farming exhausting and keep farms small. |
| bad roads: rots | The roads are bad and the produce rots and the middlemen cheat them and the banks won't lend. | **four** points joined: three are lost | Poor roads stop produce reaching markets before it spoils. |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Joining points with "and".** If you write *"Roads are bad and banks will not lend"*, the examiner may count **one** sentence with **two** points. You have used two points but written only one sentence, and your answer is now a point short. Keep each point in its own sentence.
:::

## Putting points into your own words

Four techniques. Check after each one that the **meaning** has not changed.

| Technique | Passage | Your version |
|---|---|---|
| **Synonym** | *the decline of farming* | the fall in farming |
| **Change the word class** | *the destruction of forests* | forests are being destroyed |
| **Generalise a list** | *rice, cassava, groundnuts and palm oil* | food crops |
| **Restructure** | *Banks rarely lend to farmers.* | Farmers find it very hard to get loans. |

:::callout{type=tip}
You do not have to change **every** word. *"Okada riders"*, *"middlemen"* and *"fertiliser"* are the clearest names for those things. The aim is a **clear, accurate sentence that is not a copy**, not a sentence where every word has been swapped.
:::

## A full model answer

From the passage in the previous lesson (*Why young people are leaving the farms*), the question asked for **six reasons**.

::::model{title="Reasons young people are leaving farming" label="Model summary answer"}
1. Young people regard farming as work for the poor and uneducated.
2. Outdated tools make farm work exhausting and keep farms small.
3. Poor roads prevent farmers from getting their produce to market before it spoils.
4. Middlemen force farmers to sell their crops at very low prices.
5. Farmers cannot obtain loans to start or expand their farms.
6. Villages lack the amenities and social life that draw young people to the towns.
::::

Why it scores:

- **six** sentences for **six** points
- each sentence is **complete** and carries **one** reason
- no introduction (*"The writer gives many reasons…"*), no conclusion, no Moyamba boy, no list of crops
- the wording is the student's own, but key terms (*middlemen*, *loans*) are kept

## A practice passage

::::model{label="Practice passage" title="Road accidents"}
Road accidents now kill more young people in Sierra Leone than many diseases. Most of them, experts agree, could be prevented.

Too many drivers are on the road without ever having been properly tested. In some cases, a licence can be obtained without the holder ever having driven with an examiner beside them.

Speed is another killer. On the long straight stretches of the Masiaka highway, drivers race each other to be the first to collect passengers at the next town, and the police rarely stop them.

Many of the vehicles themselves are not fit to be on the road. Worn tyres, failed brakes and missing lights are common, especially on old lorries carrying heavy loads at night.

Finally, many road users simply do not know the rules. Pedestrians cross without looking, okada riders carry three or four passengers, and children play beside busy roads. A campaign to teach road safety in schools and on the radio would save lives.
::::

**Question:** *In four sentences, one for each, state the measures the writer suggests or implies for reducing road accidents.*

:::reveal{title="Try it, then compare with the model answer"}
1. Drivers should pass a proper test before they are given a licence.
2. The police should enforce speed limits strictly.
3. Vehicles should be inspected regularly to make sure they are roadworthy.
4. Road users, including okada riders and pedestrians, should be taught road safety.

**Notice:** the passage describes **problems**, but the question asks for **measures**. Each problem has been turned into the measure it implies. The figures and the Masiaka highway were cut as examples.
:::

## Checking your answer

Before you move on, check:

- [ ] the **number** of sentences matches the question exactly
- [ ] each sentence has **one** point, of the **right kind**
- [ ] each sentence is **complete** (subject and verb)
- [ ] there is no introduction, conclusion or example
- [ ] spelling and tenses are correct

## More practice

:::reveal{title="1. Rewrite as a summary sentence: 'lack of drugs in clinics'"}
**Clinics do not have enough medicines.**
:::

:::reveal{title="2. What is wrong with: 'Firstly, the writer explains that there are many reasons why students fail.'"}
It is an **introduction** and contains **no point**. It wastes a sentence.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. In your own words: 'The scarcity of qualified teachers in rural schools is alarming.'"}
**Rural schools have far too few trained teachers.**
:::

:::reveal{title="4. The question asks for five sentences. You have written six. What do you do?"}
Check whether two sentences carry the **same** point, or one is of the **wrong kind** or an **example**, and remove it.
:::

## Key points

- Write **one point per complete sentence**, exactly as many as the question asks.
- Use **your own words** where you can, but keep the meaning **exact**; key terms may stay.
- No **introduction**, **conclusion**, **examples** or **joined points**.
- Turn **problems into measures** (or the reverse) when the question asks for the other side.
- **Check** number, completeness and accuracy before moving on.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The 30 summary marks reward **correct points** and **good expression**. Examiners penalise **lifting**, **fragments**, **irrelevant material** and **joined points**. A short, numbered list of complete, accurate sentences is the safest and highest-scoring form of answer.
:::
