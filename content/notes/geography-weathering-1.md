**Weathering** is the breaking down of rocks **in place** (where they are, without being moved) by the weather, chemicals and living things. There are three types of weathering: **physical (mechanical) weathering**, which breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals; **chemical weathering**, which changes the minerals themselves; and **biological weathering**, which is caused by plants, animals and people. This lesson explains each type, with examples, and the factors that control how fast rocks weather.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define weathering and distinguish it from erosion
- describe the processes of physical, chemical and biological weathering
- explain why chemical weathering dominates in Sierra Leone and physical weathering in hot deserts
- state the factors that affect the type and rate of weathering
- explain the importance of weathering

## Weathering and erosion

:::callout{type=definition}
**Weathering** is the breakdown of rock **in situ** (in its original place). **Erosion** is the **wearing away and removal** of rock material by moving agents: rivers, waves, wind and ice. Weathering prepares the loose material; erosion carries it away.
:::

## Physical (mechanical) weathering

Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces **without changing its chemical composition**.

| Process | How it works | Where common |
|---|---|---|
| **Exfoliation (onion weathering)** | by day the outer layers of rock heat and expand; at night they cool and contract; stress makes them peel off like onion skins | hot deserts; bare granite domes |
| **Block disintegration** | repeated expansion and contraction opens joints, splitting rock into blocks | hot deserts |
| **Granular disintegration** | different minerals expand by different amounts, so grains loosen and crumble | coarse rocks such as granite |
| **Frost action (freeze–thaw)** | water in cracks freezes, expands by about 9%, and forces the crack wider | high mountains, cold regions |
| **Salt crystallisation** | salt crystals growing in pores push grains apart | deserts and coasts |
| **Pressure release (unloading)** | when overlying rock is eroded away, the rock below expands and cracks into sheets | granite exposed by erosion |

## Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering **changes the minerals** in rock, usually through reactions with water, oxygen and carbon dioxide. It is fastest where it is **hot and wet**.

| Process | How it works | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Solution** | some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve in water | rock dissolves away |
| **Carbonation** | rainwater with dissolved carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid, which dissolves limestone | caves and pits in limestone areas |
| **Oxidation** | oxygen reacts with iron in rocks (rusting) | red and brown rocks and soils (laterite) |
| **Hydration** | minerals absorb water, swell and weaken | rock crumbles |
| **Hydrolysis** | water reacts with minerals such as feldspar in granite | feldspar turns to **clay** (kaolin) |

## Biological weathering

Biological weathering is caused by **living organisms**:

- **Plant roots** grow into cracks and widen them as they thicken.
- **Burrowing animals** (termites, earthworms, rodents) open the rock and soil to air and water.
- **Lichens, mosses and bacteria** release acids that attack rock surfaces.
- **Human activities**: quarrying, mining, road building and farming expose and break up rock.

## Factors affecting weathering

1. **Climate:** hot, wet climates favour **chemical** weathering; hot, dry climates with large daily temperature ranges favour **physical** weathering; cold climates favour **frost action**.
2. **Rock type:** limestone is weathered quickly by carbonation; quartzite resists; rocks with many joints weather faster.
3. **Vegetation:** roots and decaying plants (which release acids) speed up weathering.
4. **Relief (slope):** on steep slopes weathered material is removed quickly, exposing fresh rock.
5. **Time:** the longer rock is exposed, the deeper it is weathered.
6. **Human activities**, as above.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's hot, wet climate makes **chemical weathering** very active. Rocks are often rotted to depths of many metres, and **oxidation** of iron gives the red **laterite** soils seen along roads across the country. Over very long periods, intense weathering has also concentrated **bauxite** (aluminium ore), mined near Mokanji in Moyamba District.
:::

## Importance of weathering

1. **Soil formation:** weathered rock (regolith) is the parent material of soil.
2. **Mineral deposits:** weathering concentrates minerals such as bauxite and laterite iron ore.
3. **Landforms:** weathering shapes features such as inselbergs, tors and limestone caves.
4. **Preparation for erosion:** weathered material is easily carried away.
5. **Negative effects:** weathering damages buildings, monuments and roads.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Weathering and erosion are not the same. If material is **moved**, by a river, the sea, wind or ice, the process is **erosion**. Weathering happens **in place**.
:::

## Key points

- **Weathering:** breakdown of rock **in situ**; **erosion:** wearing away and removal by moving agents.
- **Physical:** exfoliation, block and granular disintegration, freeze–thaw, salt crystallisation, pressure release.
- **Chemical:** solution, carbonation, oxidation, hydration, hydrolysis; fastest in **hot, wet** climates such as Sierra Leone's.
- **Biological:** roots, burrowing animals, acids from lichens and bacteria, human activity.
- Factors: **climate, rock type, vegetation, relief, time, people**.

## Quick check

1. Define weathering.
2. Which type of weathering is most common in hot deserts, and why?
3. What is carbonation?
4. Give two ways living things cause weathering.
5. Why are many roads in Sierra Leone lined with red soil?

**Answers:**

1. The breakdown of rocks in their original place by physical, chemical and biological processes.
2. Physical weathering (such as exfoliation), because the large difference between hot days and cold nights makes rocks expand and contract.
3. The dissolving of limestone by rainwater containing carbon dioxide (weak carbonic acid).
4. Any two: roots widening cracks; burrowing animals; acids from lichens and bacteria; human quarrying and mining.
5. Oxidation of iron in the rocks and soil, in the hot, wet climate, produces red laterite.
