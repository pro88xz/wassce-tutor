Atoms are far too small to count one by one, so chemists count them in huge, fixed batches, just as traders count kola nuts by the hundred or eggs by the crate. The chemist's batch is the **mole**. Almost every calculation in WASSCE Chemistry passes through it, so this lesson is worth mastering.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the mole and the Avogadro constant
- calculate relative molecular mass and relative formula mass
- define molar mass and convert between mass and moles
- convert between moles and numbers of particles
- use the mole "bridge" to move between quantities

## The mole and the Avogadro constant

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **mole** is the **amount of substance** that contains as many particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons) as there are atoms in exactly **12 g of carbon-12**.
- That number is the **Avogadro constant**, $L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles per mole.
:::

One mole of anything contains $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles:

- 1 mol of carbon atoms = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ C atoms
- 1 mol of water molecules = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ H₂O molecules
- 1 mol of sodium chloride contains $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ Na⁺ ions **and** $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ Cl⁻ ions

Always say **which particle** you mean: one mole of oxygen **atoms** (O) is not the same as one mole of oxygen **molecules** (O₂).

## Relative molecular mass and formula mass

- **Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ)**: the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a **molecule**.
- **Relative formula mass**: the same idea for **ionic** compounds, which have no molecules.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Using H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, S = 32, Ca = 40, Cu = 64:**

| Substance | Working | Mᵣ |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 2(1) + 16 | 18 |
| CO₂ | 12 + 2(16) | 44 |
| H₂SO₄ | 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) | 98 |
| CaCO₃ | 40 + 12 + 3(16) | 100 |
| Na₂CO₃ | 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) | 106 |
| CuSO₄·5H₂O | 64 + 32 + 4(16) + 5(18) | 250 |
:::

## Molar mass

:::callout{type=definition}
The **molar mass (M)** of a substance is the **mass of one mole** of it, in **g/mol** (g mol⁻¹). It is numerically equal to the relative atomic, molecular or formula mass.
:::

So the molar mass of water is 18 g/mol, of CO₂ 44 g/mol, of CaCO₃ 100 g/mol.

$$n = \dfrac{m}{M} \qquad \text{moles} = \dfrac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g/mol)}}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Mass and moles.**

1. How many moles are in 25 g of calcium carbonate? $n = \dfrac{25}{100} = 0.25$ mol.
2. What is the mass of 0.2 mol of sodium hydroxide, NaOH (M = 40)? $m = n \times M = 0.2 \times 40 = 8$ g.
3. 0.5 mol of a substance has a mass of 32 g. What is its molar mass? $M = \dfrac{32}{0.5} = 64$ g/mol.
:::

## Moles and numbers of particles

$$N = n \times L \qquad \text{number of particles} = \text{moles} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Counting particles.**

1. How many molecules are in 0.5 mol of water?

$$N = 0.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23} \text{ molecules}$$

2. How many **atoms** are in that water? Each H₂O molecule has 3 atoms:

$$3 \times 3.01 \times 10^{23} = 9.03 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms}$$

3. What is the mass of $1.204 \times 10^{24}$ carbon atoms?

$$n = \dfrac{1.204 \times 10^{24}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 2 \text{ mol} \qquad m = 2 \times 12 = 24 \text{ g}$$
:::

## The mole bridge

![The mole at the centre: mass divided by molar mass gives moles; moles times the Avogadro constant gives particles; gas volume divided by 22.4 dm³ gives moles; concentration times volume gives moles](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/mole-bridge.svg)

*Figure 1. Every conversion passes through moles. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

To change one quantity into another, **go through moles**:

- mass → moles (÷ M) → particles (× L)
- mass → moles (÷ M) → gas volume (× 22.4 dm³) (next lesson)
- concentration and volume → moles (C × V) → mass (× M) (next lesson)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How many oxygen molecules are in 8 g of oxygen gas, O₂?**

1. Molar mass of O₂ = 32 g/mol.
2. Moles: $n = 8 \div 32 = 0.25$ mol.
3. Molecules: $0.25 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.505 \times 10^{23}$.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
A trader selling sugar by the cup does not count the grains; the cup is a fixed "batch". Chemists do the same with the mole. One mole of table sugar, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (M = 342 g/mol), is about 342 g, roughly a cupful, and holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ sugar molecules.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not use 16 for the molar mass of oxygen **gas**. Oxygen gas is **O₂**, so its molar mass is **32 g/mol**. The same applies to H₂ (2), N₂ (28) and Cl₂ (71).
:::

## Key points

- One **mole** contains **6.02 × 10²³** particles (the Avogadro constant), the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
- **Mᵣ** = sum of the relative atomic masses in the formula; **molar mass** has the same number in g/mol.
- $n = m \div M$ and $N = n \times L$.
- Always state the **particle** (atoms, molecules, ions) and use the right formula (O₂, not O).
- Convert any quantity to **moles first**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define the mole**, to **calculate Mᵣ** (including hydrated salts), and to **convert between mass, moles and numbers of particles**. Questions often ask for the number of **atoms** or **ions**, not molecules, so read carefully and multiply by the number in the formula.
:::

## Quick check

1. Calculate the relative molecular mass of ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (N = 14, H = 1, O = 16).
2. How many moles are in 9.8 g of H₂SO₄?
3. How many molecules are in 0.1 mol of carbon dioxide?
4. What is the mass of 3.01 × 10²³ atoms of sodium (Na = 23)?

**Answers:**

1. $14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80$.
2. $9.8 \div 98 = 0.1$ mol.
3. $0.1 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 6.02 \times 10^{22}$ molecules.
4. 0.5 mol × 23 = 11.5 g.
