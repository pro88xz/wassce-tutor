A teacher's salary rises by 10%, but prices in the market rise by 25%. Is she better off? Workers join trade unions to push wages up, governments set minimum wages, and still many young people cannot find work at all. This lesson covers real wages, how wages are paid, trade unions and unemployment.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish nominal (money) wages from real wages, and time rates from piece rates
- outline the main theories of wages
- explain the functions and methods of trade unions and their effect on wages
- explain the effect of a minimum wage
- describe the types, causes, effects and remedies of unemployment

## Money wages and real wages

:::callout{type=definition}
- The **nominal (money) wage** is the amount of money a worker receives.
- The **real wage** is what that money can buy: the quantity of goods and services the wage commands at current prices.
:::

$$\text{real wage index} = \dfrac{\text{money wage index}}{\text{price index}} \times 100$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A teacher's money wage rises from NLe 3,000 to NLe 3,300 a month (10%), while the price index rises from 100 to 125.**

Money wage index $= \dfrac{3300}{3000} \times 100 = 110$.

$$\text{real wage index} = \dfrac{110}{125} \times 100 = 88$$

Her **real wage has fallen by 12%**: she can buy less than before, even though she is paid more money.
:::

## Ways of paying wages

| | Time rate | Piece rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Paid by** | Time worked (hour, day, month) | Output produced (per bag, per garment) |
| **Suits** | Work hard to measure: teaching, nursing, office work | Work easy to count: tailoring, harvesting, brick-making |
| **Advantages** | Steady income; care and quality not rushed | Rewards effort; faster workers earn more; less supervision |
| **Disadvantages** | No reward for extra effort; needs supervision | Quality may suffer; overwork; income varies |

## Theories of wages

1. **The subsistence theory** (the "iron law of wages"): wages tend towards the minimum needed to keep a worker and family alive. If wages rise above it, population grows, more workers compete and wages fall back. It ignores rising productivity and trade unions.
2. **The marginal productivity theory**: a firm employs workers up to the point where the **wage equals the value of the extra output** (marginal revenue product) of the last worker. More productive workers can therefore be paid more.
3. **The bargaining theory**: wages are settled by **negotiation** between employers and workers (often through trade unions), between an upper limit (what the worker adds to the firm) and a lower limit (the subsistence level).

## Trade unions

:::callout{type=definition}
A **trade union** is an association of workers formed to protect and improve their wages, working conditions and other interests.
:::

**Functions of trade unions:**

1. To bargain for **higher wages** and better allowances.
2. To press for **better working conditions**: safety, hours, leave.
3. To protect members from **unfair dismissal** and settle disputes.
4. To provide **welfare benefits** and education for members.
5. To represent workers' views to the **government** on labour laws and economic policy.

**Methods:** collective bargaining (negotiation), petitions, **work-to-rule**, **go-slow**, overtime bans and, as a last resort, **strikes**.

**Effect on wages:** a union can raise wages above the free-market level, especially when the demand for the product (and so for labour) is inelastic, when labour is a small part of total cost, or when the firm makes large profits. But if wages rise too far, employers may hire fewer workers or replace them with machines.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Most trade unions in Sierra Leone belong to the **Sierra Leone Labour Congress (SLLC)**, which negotiates with employers and the government on issues such as the national minimum wage and public-sector pay.
:::

## A minimum wage

:::callout{type=definition}
A **minimum wage** is a legal wage floor.
:::

If it is set **above** the equilibrium wage (Figure 1 in the previous lesson), more people want to work at that wage than employers want to hire:

- workers who keep their jobs **earn more**, which reduces poverty
- but there may be **unemployment** ($L_s - L_d$), especially in small firms
- some employers may hire workers **informally**, below the legal wage

## Unemployment

:::callout{type=definition}
**Unemployment** exists when people who are **able and willing to work** at the current wage **cannot find jobs**.
:::

$$\text{unemployment rate} = \dfrac{\text{number unemployed}}{\text{labour force}} \times 100$$

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Frictional** | People between jobs, or new entrants still searching | A graduate looking for a first job |
| **Seasonal** | Work available only in certain seasons | Farm labourers after the harvest |
| **Structural** | Decline of an industry, or a mismatch of skills | Workers laid off when a mine closes |
| **Cyclical (demand-deficient)** | A general fall in spending in a recession | Many firms cutting staff at once |
| **Technological** | Machines replacing workers | Mechanised rice harvesting |
| **Disguised (underemployment)** | More workers than the work needs; extra workers add nothing | Five family members doing work that two could do |

**Causes of high unemployment in Sierra Leone and West Africa:** rapid population growth; slow growth of industry; rural–urban migration; an education system that produces school leavers without the skills employers want; lack of capital and credit for self-employment; and dependence on a few export industries.

**Effects:** loss of output; poverty and dependence on relatives; crime and social unrest; loss of skills; lower tax revenue; emigration of young people.

**Remedies:**

1. Expanding **agriculture** and agro-processing, which employ many people.
2. Encouraging **industry** and foreign investment.
3. **Technical and vocational training** matched to the jobs available.
4. **Credit and support for small businesses** and self-employment.
5. Public **works programmes** (roads, housing).
6. Developing **rural areas** to slow migration to cities.
7. Policies to **raise total spending** in a recession.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A student in full-time education, or a retired person, is **not** unemployed: they are outside the labour force. Unemployed people must be **willing and able to work and looking for work**.
:::

## Key points

- **Real wage** = money wage adjusted for prices; a money wage rise smaller than inflation is a real wage cut.
- **Time rates** pay for time; **piece rates** pay for output.
- Wage theories: **subsistence**, **marginal productivity**, **bargaining**.
- **Trade unions** bargain for higher wages and better conditions; methods range from negotiation to strikes.
- A **minimum wage** above equilibrium raises some wages but may cause unemployment.
- Unemployment can be **frictional, seasonal, structural, cyclical, technological or disguised**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Common questions: **distinguish money wages from real wages** (often with a calculation), **compare time and piece rates**, **state the functions of trade unions** and **how they raise wages**, and **explain the types, causes and remedies of unemployment**, often "in your country". Give Sierra Leonean examples, and for remedies explain *how* each measure creates jobs.

## Quick check

1. A worker's money wage index is 120 and the price index is 150. What is the real wage index?
2. Give one advantage of a piece rate to an employer.
3. Name two methods a trade union may use to press its demands.
4. What type of unemployment affects farm labourers after the harvest?

**Answers:** 1. $\dfrac{120}{150} \times 100 = 80$: real wages have fallen by 20%. 2. It rewards effort, so output rises with less supervision. 3. Any two: collective bargaining, work-to-rule, go-slow, overtime ban, strike. 4. Seasonal unemployment.
