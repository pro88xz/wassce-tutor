# The Earth's internal structure and major rock groups

## Learning objectives
You should be able to describe the main internal layers of the Earth, classify rocks into three major groups, and relate rock properties to their formation.

## Internal structure of the Earth
The Earth is arranged in layers that differ in composition, density and physical behaviour.

**Crust:** The thin, solid outer layer. Continental crust is generally thicker and less dense than oceanic crust. It is made mainly of silicate rocks. The crust and the rigid uppermost mantle together form the lithosphere.

**Mantle:** A very thick layer beneath the crust, extending to about 2,900 km depth. It consists of hot, solid rock that can deform and flow slowly over geological time. Heat-driven movement in the mantle contributes to the processes that move tectonic plates.

**Outer core:** A liquid layer composed mainly of iron and nickel. Movement of electrically conducting liquid metal helps generate the Earth's magnetic field.

**Inner core:** A solid, dense central region composed mainly of iron and nickel. It remains solid despite very high temperatures because of the immense pressure.

The boundary between the crust and mantle is the Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho). The mantle–outer core boundary is the Gutenberg discontinuity, and the outer–inner core boundary is the Lehmann discontinuity.

## What is a rock?
A rock is a naturally occurring solid aggregate of one or more minerals or mineral-like materials. Minerals have characteristic chemical compositions and physical properties; rocks are classified mainly by how they form.

### Igneous rocks
Igneous rocks form when molten material cools and solidifies. Magma cools below the surface; lava cools at the surface.
- Intrusive rocks cool slowly underground and commonly develop large crystals. Granite is an example.
- Extrusive rocks cool rapidly at or near the surface and commonly have fine crystals. Basalt is an example.

### Sedimentary rocks
Sedimentary rocks form when sediments are deposited, compacted and cemented, or when minerals precipitate from water or organic material accumulates. Sandstone, shale and many limestones are examples. Sedimentary rocks often occur in layers and may contain fossils.

### Metamorphic rocks
Metamorphic rocks develop when existing rocks are changed by heat, pressure and chemically active fluids without completely melting. Shale may become slate; limestone may become marble; granite may become gneiss. Foliation or banding may develop when minerals align under directed pressure.

## Comparing the three groups

| Group | Main formation process | Common evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Igneous | Cooling and solidification of magma or lava | Interlocking crystals; sometimes vesicles |
| Sedimentary | Deposition, compaction, cementation or precipitation | Bedding, grains, fossils |
| Metamorphic | Solid-state change under heat, pressure or fluids | Recrystallisation, foliation or banding |

## Why rocks matter to people
Rock type influences relief, soil development, groundwater storage, construction materials and mineral resources. Resistant rocks may form hills and escarpments, while weaker rocks may be eroded into valleys. Weathering of different parent rocks produces soils with different textures and mineral contents.

## WASSCE examination focus
Describe the layers from the crust to the inner core in the correct order. Classify a named rock by its formation, not simply by its colour. Granite is intrusive igneous; basalt is extrusive igneous; sandstone is sedimentary; marble is metamorphic.
