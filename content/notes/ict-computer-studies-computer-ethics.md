:::callout{type=definition}
**Computer ethics** is the set of moral principles that guide how people should behave when they use computers, the Internet and information: respecting other people's privacy, property and work, and using technology honestly and responsibly.
:::

A computer makes it easy to copy a song, read someone else's messages or post a hurtful comment, but being easy does not make it right. This lesson explains **computer ethics** and the main **ethical issues in ICT**: **privacy**, **intellectual property** (copyright, patents and trademarks), **software piracy**, **plagiarism**, **cyberbullying**, **netiquette** and your **digital footprint**, with the laws and codes of conduct that guide responsible use.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define computer ethics and explain why it matters
- explain privacy and data protection, and how personal data can be misused
- describe the types of intellectual property and how copyright applies to software, music and writing
- explain software piracy and plagiarism, and why they are wrong
- describe cyberbullying, netiquette and the digital footprint
- state rules of responsible and ethical computer use

## Why computer ethics matters

Laws say what is **legal**; ethics asks what is **right**. Something can be legal and still unkind or unfair, such as sharing an embarrassing photo of a classmate. Ethical behaviour protects other people, builds trust, and keeps you out of trouble with schools, employers and the law.

The **Ten Commandments of Computer Ethics** (Computer Ethics Institute, 1992) sum up the main ideas, among them: do not use a computer to harm other people; do not snoop in other people's files; do not use a computer to steal; do not copy software you have not paid for; and think about the social effects of the programs you write.

## Privacy and personal data

:::callout{type=definition}
**Privacy:** the right of people to control what information about themselves is collected, how it is used and who sees it.

**Personal data:** any information that identifies a living person, such as a name, phone number, address, photo, ID number or medical record.

**Data protection:** the laws and practices that make organisations collect personal data fairly, keep it secure and use it only for the purpose it was given for.
:::

**Ways privacy is broken:** reading someone's messages or emails without permission; posting a person's photos or details without consent; selling customers' data; tracking people's location secretly; and careless loss of records.

**Organisations that hold personal data should:** collect only what they need; tell people why they need it; keep it **accurate** and **secure**; not keep it longer than necessary; and not pass it on without permission.

## Intellectual property

:::callout{type=definition}
**Intellectual property:** creations of the mind, such as writing, music, inventions, designs and software, that the law lets their owners control and profit from.

**Copyright:** the legal right of the creator of an original work, such as a book, song, film, photograph or computer program, to decide who may copy, share or sell it.

**Patent:** a legal right, granted for a limited number of years, that stops others from making, using or selling a new invention without the inventor's permission.

**Trademark:** a name, logo, symbol or slogan that identifies a company's goods or services, registered so that no one else may use it.
:::

Copyright exists as soon as a work is created; the © symbol reminds people of it. Using a small part of a work for study, criticism or news, with credit to its author, is usually allowed as **fair use** (fair dealing).

### Software piracy

:::callout{type=definition}
**Software piracy:** copying, installing, sharing or selling software without the permission (licence) of its owner.

**Licence agreement (EULA):** the legal terms, accepted when software is installed, that say how many computers it may be used on and what the user may and may not do with it.
:::

**Forms of piracy:** installing one licensed copy on many computers; buying "cracked" programs on CDs or flash drives; downloading paid software, films or music free from illegal sites; and using a fake key to unlock software.

**Why piracy is wrong and risky:** it **steals** from the people who made the software; pirated copies often carry **viruses and spyware**; they get **no updates or support**; and businesses caught with them can be **fined**. Free and **open-source** alternatives, such as LibreOffice, GIMP and Linux, can be used legally instead.

### Plagiarism

:::callout{type=definition}
**Plagiarism:** presenting another person's words, ideas or work as your own without saying where they came from.
:::

Copying text from a website into an assignment without quotation marks and a reference is plagiarism, even if you change a few words. To avoid it: write in **your own words**, put exact quotes in **quotation marks**, and **cite your source** (author, title, website and date).

## Behaviour online

:::callout{type=definition}
**Cyberbullying:** using phones, social media or other online tools to threaten, embarrass, insult or exclude someone, often repeatedly.

**Netiquette:** the rules of polite and respectful behaviour on the Internet, in email, chats and social media.

**Digital footprint:** the permanent trail of information a person leaves online, such as posts, photos, comments, searches and the data that websites collect about them.
:::

**Rules of netiquette:**

- be polite; do not insult, threaten or spread rumours
- do not type in CAPITAL LETTERS, which reads as **shouting**
- respect other people's privacy: ask before posting their photos
- check that information is **true** before sharing it, so you do not spread false news
- keep messages short and give emails a clear subject
- do not send spam or forward chain messages

**If you are cyberbullied:** do not reply; **save evidence** (screenshots); **block** the person; **report** it to the platform and tell a parent, teacher or trusted adult.

Your digital footprint can be seen by future universities and employers, so **think before you post**: what goes online can stay there.

## Codes of conduct and responsible use

:::callout{type=definition}
**Acceptable use policy (AUP):** a set of rules, written by a school or organisation, that users agree to follow when using its computers, network and Internet connection.
:::

A typical school AUP says: use your **own account** and keep your password secret; use the computers **for school work**; do not install software or change settings; do not visit harmful or illegal sites; do not eat or drink near the computers; and report any problem to the teacher. Professional bodies of ICT workers also have **codes of conduct** that require honesty, competence and respect for privacy.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**An ethical choice at the cyber café.** Fatmata finds a website offering a "free" copy of an expensive design program. Installing it would be **software piracy**, could load **malware** onto her computer, and breaks the program's **licence**. She uses free, open-source **GIMP** instead, legally and safely.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Thinking that anything on the Internet is free to copy.** Most text, photos, music and videos online are protected by **copyright**. Being able to download something does not give you the right to reuse or sell it.
:::

The **moral principles** that guide the right use of computers and information. Examples (any two): **software piracy**, **plagiarism**, **cyberbullying**, reading others' messages without permission, spreading **false news**, misusing **personal data**.

:::reveal{title="2. Distinguish between copyright and a patent."}
**Copyright** protects **original creative works**, such as books, music and software, and exists as soon as the work is made. A **patent** protects a **new invention** and must be applied for; it lasts a limited number of years.

Any three: installing one licensed copy on **many computers**; buying or selling **cracked copies**; **downloading** paid software free from illegal sites; using **fake licence keys**.

Write in **your own words**; put exact words in **quotation marks**; and **cite the source** (author, title, website and date) of every idea or quotation used.

:::reveal{title="5. State four rules of netiquette."}
Any four: be **polite**; do not type in **capitals** (shouting); respect **privacy**; check facts before sharing; keep messages **short** with a clear subject; do not send **spam**.
:::

## Key points

- **Computer ethics** is about using computers in ways that are **right**, not just legal.
- **Privacy** and **data protection**: collect, keep and use personal data fairly and securely.
- **Intellectual property**: **copyright** (works), **patent** (inventions), **trademark** (brands), **trade secret**.
- **Software piracy** and **plagiarism** steal other people's work; use licensed or open-source software and cite sources.
- Online: avoid **cyberbullying**, follow **netiquette**, and remember your **digital footprint**.
- Schools and organisations set rules in an **acceptable use policy**.
