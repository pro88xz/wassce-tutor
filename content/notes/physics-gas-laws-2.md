Real problems rarely keep one quantity fixed. A weather balloon rises into colder, thinner air; gas in a cylinder is heated and squeezed at the same time. The general gas law combines all three laws into one.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- use the general gas law $\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$
- convert to and from standard temperature and pressure (s.t.p.)
- explain the gas laws using the kinetic theory
- describe an experiment to verify Boyle's law or Charles's law

## The general gas law

Combining the three laws for a fixed mass of gas:

$$\dfrac{pV}{T} = \text{constant} \qquad \dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$$

When one of the three quantities is constant, it cancels and the equation reduces to Boyle's, Charles's or the pressure law.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A gas occupies 500 cm³ at 27 °C and 100 kPa. Find its volume at 127 °C and 200 kPa.**

$T_1 = 300$ K, $T_2 = 400$ K.

$$V_2 = \dfrac{p_1V_1T_2}{T_1p_2} = \dfrac{100 \times 500 \times 400}{300 \times 200} = 333\ \text{cm}^3$$
:::

## Standard temperature and pressure

:::callout{type=definition}
**s.t.p.** means a temperature of **0 °C (273 K)** and a pressure of **760 mmHg** ($1.01 \times 10^{5}$ Pa).
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A gas has a volume of 380 cm³ at 27 °C and 750 mmHg. Find its volume at s.t.p.**

$$V_2 = 380 \times \dfrac{750}{760} \times \dfrac{273}{300} = 380 \times 0.9868 \times 0.91 = 341\ \text{cm}^3$$
:::

## Kinetic explanations

| Law | Kinetic explanation |
|---|---|
| **Boyle's law** | At constant temperature the molecules' average speed is fixed. Reducing the volume makes them hit each unit area of the walls **more often**, so the pressure rises. |
| **Pressure law** | Raising the temperature makes the molecules move **faster**; they hit the walls **more often and harder**, so the pressure rises (the volume cannot change). |
| **Charles's law** | Faster molecules at a higher temperature would raise the pressure; to keep it constant, the gas **expands** so the molecules hit each unit area less often. |
| **Absolute zero** | The molecules have their minimum kinetic energy; they no longer exert pressure. |

## Experiment: verifying Boyle's law

1. Trap a column of dry air in a thick-walled glass tube by a thread of mercury (or with oil, using a Bourdon pressure gauge and a pump).
2. Change the pressure in steps and, after waiting for the temperature to settle, record the pressure $p$ and the length of the air column (proportional to volume $V$).
3. Plot $p$ against $\dfrac{1}{V}$: a **straight line through the origin** verifies Boyle's law. (Alternatively, show that $pV$ is constant.)

**Precautions:** change the pressure slowly so the temperature stays constant; read the column at eye level; the air must be dry.

## Experiment: verifying Charles's law

1. Trap air in a capillary tube by a short thread of concentrated sulphuric acid (which also keeps it dry), with the open end upwards so the pressure stays atmospheric.
2. Place the tube beside a thermometer in a water bath. Heat in stages, stirring, and record the temperature and the length of the air column at each stage.
3. Plot length (volume) against temperature in °C: a straight line which, extended, meets the temperature axis at about −273 °C.

## Key points

- General gas law for a fixed mass: $\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$ (temperatures in kelvin).
- s.t.p. = 273 K and 760 mmHg.
- Kinetic theory explains pressure as molecules striking the walls; temperature sets their speed.
- Boyle: plot $p$ against $1/V$; Charles: plot $V$ against θ and extrapolate to −273 °C.

## Quick check

1. Write the general gas law.
2. What are the values of standard temperature and pressure?
3. Explain, using the kinetic theory, why the pressure of a gas in a sealed can rises when it is heated.

**Answers:** 1. $\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$. 2. 273 K (0 °C) and 760 mmHg ($1.01 \times 10^{5}$ Pa). 3. The molecules move faster, so they strike the walls more often and with greater force.
