When teachers in Sierra Leone want better pay, they do not form a political party and try to win an election. They act through their union, meeting ministers, issuing statements and, as a last resort, going on strike. Groups like this are **pressure groups**: they try to influence what government does without trying to become the government. This lesson explains the types, methods and functions of pressure groups and how they differ from political parties.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a pressure group
- classify pressure groups into types, with Sierra Leonean examples
- explain the methods pressure groups use
- state the functions, merits and demerits of pressure groups
- distinguish pressure groups from political parties
- explain the factors that determine a pressure group's success

## What is a pressure group?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **pressure group** (interest group) is an organised group of people who share common interests or aims and who try to **influence government policy and decisions** in their favour, **without seeking to form a government** or contest elections.
:::

## Types and methods

![Four types of pressure group (interest, promotional, institutional, anomic) with examples, and eight methods: lobbying, petitions, press and radio, strikes, demonstrations, court action, boycotts and funding candidates](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/pressure-groups.svg)

*Figure 1. Types and methods of pressure groups. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Type | Meaning | Sierra Leone examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest (sectional, protective) groups** | Protect the interests of their own members | Sierra Leone Teachers' Union, Sierra Leone Labour Congress, Sierra Leone Bar Association, Medical and Dental Association, Market Women's associations, Sierra Leone Chamber of Commerce |
| **Promotional (cause) groups** | Promote a cause or idea that benefits society, not only members | The **50/50 Group** (more women in politics), human rights and anti-corruption organisations |
| **Institutional groups** | Bodies that are part of the state or of society's institutions and press their views from within | The **Inter-Religious Council of Sierra Leone**, the armed forces, the civil service |
| **Anomic groups** | Spontaneous, unorganised outbursts | Sudden street protests over prices or a local incident |

**Methods of pressure groups:**

1. **Lobbying**: meeting and persuading ministers, MPs and officials.
2. **Petitions and memoranda** to government.
3. **Media campaigns**: press conferences, radio discussions, social media.
4. **Strikes and go-slows** by workers' unions.
5. **Peaceful demonstrations and protest marches.**
6. **Court action** to challenge laws or decisions.
7. **Boycotts** of goods, services or events.
8. **Sponsoring or supporting candidates** who favour their cause.
9. **Giving expert advice** to government and serving on committees.

## Functions of pressure groups

- **Articulate interests**: bring the needs of particular groups to government's attention.
- **Provide information** and expert advice for policy-making.
- **Check government** and defend citizens' rights.
- **Political education** of members and the public.
- **Promote participation** between elections.
- **Protect minorities** whose voices may be ignored.

**Demerits:** powerful groups may win at the expense of the public; strikes disrupt services and the economy; demonstrations can become violent; some groups serve narrow or selfish interests; well-funded groups can corrupt officials.

## Pressure groups and political parties compared

| Pressure groups | Political parties |
|---|---|
| Seek to **influence** government | Seek to **control** government |
| Do not contest elections | Contest elections |
| Usually concerned with **one or a few** issues | Have policies on **all** national issues |
| Membership may be limited to a group (such as teachers) | Membership open to all citizens |
| Not responsible for running government | Responsible for government when in power |
| May work with any government | Compete against other parties |

## Factors that determine success

- **Size and unity** of membership
- **Funds** available
- **Quality of leadership** and organisation
- **Strategic importance** of members (a strike by doctors or power workers bites hard)
- **Public support** for the cause
- **Attitude of the government** (democratic governments listen more than dictatorships)
- **Access to the media** and to decision-makers

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **50/50 Group**, founded in **2000**, campaigned for many years for equal representation of women in Sierra Leonean politics, using lobbying, training of women candidates and media campaigns. Its cause was advanced by the **Gender Equality and Women's Empowerment Act 2022**, which set a minimum 30% share for women in many positions. The **Inter-Religious Council** played a notable mediating role during the civil war and its peace talks.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A pressure group can **support** candidates in elections, but it does **not** put up its own candidates to win power. If a group starts contesting elections to form a government, it has turned into a **political party**.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Interest group** (sectional group): a pressure group that protects the interests of its own members.
- **Promotional group** (cause group): a pressure group that promotes a cause or idea that benefits society, not only its members.
- **Institutional group**: a body that is part of the state or of society's institutions and presses its views from within.
- **Anomic group**: a spontaneous, unorganised outburst of protest.
- **Lobbying**: meeting and persuading ministers, MPs and officials in order to influence decisions.
:::

## Key points

- A **pressure group** tries to **influence** government without seeking to **form** one.
- Types: **interest (sectional)**, **promotional (cause)**, **institutional** and **anomic**.
- Methods: **lobbying, petitions, media, strikes, demonstrations, court action, boycotts, supporting candidates**.
- Success depends on **size, funds, leadership, strategic position, public support and the government's attitude**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define pressure groups**, to **classify them with examples**, to **explain their methods and functions**, and to **distinguish them from political parties**. A common question asks for "five factors that determine the effectiveness of pressure groups".
:::

## Quick check

1. What is the main difference between a pressure group and a political party?
2. Give an example of a promotional group in Sierra Leone.
3. State three methods used by pressure groups.
4. What is an anomic group?

**Answers:**

1. A pressure group seeks to influence government policy without trying to form the government; a political party seeks to win elections and control the government.
2. The 50/50 Group (or any human rights or anti-corruption campaign group).
3. Any three: lobbying; petitions; media campaigns; strikes; demonstrations; court action; boycotts; sponsoring candidates; giving expert advice.
4. A spontaneous, unorganised group that arises suddenly, such as a street protest that breaks out over an incident.
