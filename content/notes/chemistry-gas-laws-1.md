A bicycle pump gets harder to push as the air inside is squeezed; a car tyre's pressure rises after a long drive in the sun; a balloon left in a cold room shrinks. Gases follow simple, predictable laws linking their **pressure, volume and temperature**. This lesson covers Boyle's law, Charles' law, the pressure law and the equations that combine them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the units of pressure and convert between them
- state and apply Boyle's law, Charles' law and the pressure law
- explain each law using the kinetic theory
- use the general gas equation and convert volumes to s.t.p.
- use the ideal gas equation PV = nRT

## Pressure and its units

Gas pressure is caused by gas particles **colliding with the walls** of their container.

| Unit | Standard atmospheric pressure |
|---|---|
| millimetres of mercury (mmHg) | 760 mmHg |
| atmosphere (atm) | 1 atm |
| pascal (Pa) = N/m² | 101 325 Pa ≈ 101 kPa |

**Standard temperature and pressure (s.t.p.):** 0 °C (273 K) and 760 mmHg (1 atm).

All gas-law calculations use temperature in **kelvin**: $T = \theta + 273$.

## Boyle's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Boyle's law:** the volume of a fixed mass of gas is **inversely proportional** to its pressure, provided the **temperature is constant**.

$$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$$
:::

![Left: a Boyle's law curve of pressure against volume. Right: a Charles' law straight line of volume against temperature, extended back to zero volume at −273 °C](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/boyle-charles.svg)

*Figure 1. Boyle's law (left) and Charles' law (right). WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Kinetic explanation:** if the volume is halved, the same particles are packed into half the space, so they hit the walls **twice as often**, and the pressure doubles.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**200 cm³ of a gas is at 750 mmHg. What is its volume at 1500 mmHg, at the same temperature?**

$$V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{750 \times 200}{1500} = 100 \text{ cm}^3$$

Doubling the pressure halves the volume.
:::

## Charles' law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Charles' law:** the volume of a fixed mass of gas is **directly proportional** to its **absolute (kelvin) temperature**, provided the **pressure is constant**.

$$\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$$
:::

Extending the Charles' law graph backwards, the volume would reach zero at **−273 °C**. This temperature is **absolute zero** (0 K), the starting point of the kelvin scale.

**Kinetic explanation:** heating gives particles more kinetic energy. They hit the walls harder and more often, so the gas expands until the pressure falls back to its original value.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A gas occupies 300 cm³ at 27 °C. Find its volume at 127 °C, at constant pressure.**

$T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300$ K, $T_2 = 127 + 273 = 400$ K.

$$V_2 = \dfrac{V_1 \times T_2}{T_1} = \dfrac{300 \times 400}{300} = 400 \text{ cm}^3$$

Note that the volume does **not** become 300 × 127 ÷ 27. Always use kelvin.
:::

## The pressure law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Pressure law (Gay-Lussac's law of pressures):** the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is **directly proportional** to its **absolute temperature**, provided the **volume is constant**.

$$\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A tyre is at 200 kPa at 27 °C. After a long drive its temperature is 57 °C. What is the new pressure, if the volume is unchanged?**

$$P_2 = \dfrac{P_1 \times T_2}{T_1} = \dfrac{200 \times 330}{300} = 220 \text{ kPa}$$
:::

## The general gas equation

Combining the three laws:

$$\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$

It is used to correct gas volumes to **s.t.p.**, so that volumes measured on different days can be compared.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A gas occupies 500 cm³ at 27 °C and 700 mmHg. Find its volume at s.t.p.**

$P_1 = 700$, $V_1 = 500$, $T_1 = 300$ K; $P_2 = 760$, $T_2 = 273$ K.

$$V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1T_2}{T_1P_2} = \dfrac{700 \times 500 \times 273}{300 \times 760}$$

$$V_2 \approx 419 \text{ cm}^3$$
:::

## The ideal gas equation

$$PV = nRT$$

where P is pressure in **Pa**, V is volume in **m³**, n is the number of **moles**, T is temperature in **K** and R is the **molar gas constant**, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**What pressure is exerted by 0.5 mol of gas in a 10 dm³ container at 27 °C?**

V = 10 dm³ = 0.010 m³; T = 300 K.

$$P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{0.5 \times 8.314 \times 300}{0.010}$$

$$P \approx 1.25 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$$
:::

**Ideal and real gases.** The gas laws assume an **ideal gas**: particles with no volume and no attractions between them. Real gases behave almost ideally at **high temperature and low pressure**. They deviate at **high pressure** (particle volume matters) and **low temperature** (attractions matter, and gases liquefy).

:::callout{type=local-example}
Gas cylinders used for cooking in Sierra Leone carry warnings not to leave them in the sun. By the pressure law, the pressure inside rises as the temperature rises, and a hot cylinder can leak or burst. Tyres are best checked when cool, before a journey.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Never put temperatures in **°C** into the gas laws. Charles' law and the pressure law work only with **kelvin**, because 0 °C is not "zero energy". Convert first: K = °C + 273.
:::

## Key points

- **s.t.p.**: 273 K and 760 mmHg (1 atm, 101 kPa).
- **Boyle's law:** $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ at constant temperature.
- **Charles' law:** $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$ at constant pressure; absolute zero is −273 °C.
- **Pressure law:** $P_1/T_1 = P_2/T_2$ at constant volume.
- **General gas equation:** $P_1V_1/T_1 = P_2V_2/T_2$. **Ideal gas equation:** $PV = nRT$.
- Always use **kelvin**.

## Quick check

1. State Boyle's law.
2. A gas has a volume of 60 cm³ at 2 atm. What is its volume at 3 atm, at constant temperature?
3. A gas occupies 250 cm³ at 77 °C. Find its volume at 7 °C, at constant pressure.
4. What is absolute zero in °C?

**Answers:**

1. The volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure at constant temperature.
2. $V_2 = \dfrac{2 \times 60}{3} = 40$ cm³.
3. $T_1 = 350$ K, $T_2 = 280$ K: $V_2 = \dfrac{250 \times 280}{350} = 200$ cm³.
4. −273 °C.
