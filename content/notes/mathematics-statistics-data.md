Statistics starts with **data**: numbers collected to answer a question. How do students travel to school? What marks did a class score? How much rain fell each month in Bo? Raw data is just a list; statistics organises it into **tables** and **charts** so that a pattern can be seen at a glance. WASSCE sets a statistics question every year, usually starting with a frequency table and a chart before asking for averages or spread. This lesson covers collecting and organising data, frequency tables, and the four main charts: bar chart, pie chart, histogram and frequency polygon.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish types of data and organise raw data in a frequency table
- group data into classes and find class boundaries, class width and class midpoints
- draw and read bar charts and pie charts
- draw a histogram and a frequency polygon
- interpret charts to answer questions

## Types of data

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **qualitative** (categorical) | described in words | colour, mode of transport, favourite subject |
| **discrete** | counted, whole numbers | number of siblings, goals scored |
| **continuous** | measured, any value in a range | height, mass, time, rainfall |

## Frequency tables

The **frequency** of a value is how many times it occurs. A **tally** helps you count without missing any: make one stroke for each value, and draw every fifth stroke across the four before it, so the strokes can be counted in fives.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The numbers of people in 20 households in a Kono village are:**

3, 5, 4, 6, 3, 4, 5, 7, 4, 3, 5, 4, 6, 4, 8, 5, 4, 3, 6, 5

| Household size | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 3 | 4 |
| 4 | 6 |
| 5 | 5 |
| 6 | 3 |
| 7 | 1 |
| 8 | 1 |
| **total** | **20** |

The total must equal the number of data values: $4 + 6 + 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 20$ ✓
:::

### Grouped data

When there are many different values, group them into **classes** such as $1$–$10$, $11$–$20$, $21$–$30$.

:::callout{type=definition}
For the class $21$–$30$ (whole-number marks):

- **class boundaries**: $20.5$ and $30.5$ (halfway to the next class)
- **class width**: upper boundary − lower boundary $= 30.5 - 20.5 = 10$
- **class midpoint** (class mark): $\dfrac{21 + 30}{2} = 25.5$
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Class width from the class limits.** For $21$–$30$, the width is $10$, not $30 - 21 = 9$. Always use the **boundaries**.
:::

## Bar charts and pie charts

![How 40 students travel to school: walk 18, okada 10, poda-poda 8, keke 4, shown as a bar chart and as a pie chart with angles 162°, 90°, 72° and 36°](https://wasscetutor.com/img/mathematics/stats-bar-pie.svg)

A **bar chart** shows each category as a bar whose **height** is the frequency. Bars have **equal widths** and **gaps** between them.

A **pie chart** shows each category as a **sector** of a circle. The angles add up to $360°$.

:::callout{type=definition}
$$\text{sector angle} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}} \times 360°$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Draw a pie chart for: walk 18, okada 10, poda-poda 8, keke 4.**

Total $= 40$, so each student is worth $\dfrac{360°}{40} = 9°$.

| Transport | Frequency | Angle |
|---|---|---|
| walk | 18 | $18 \times 9 = 162°$ |
| okada | 10 | $90°$ |
| poda-poda | 8 | $72°$ |
| keke | 4 | $36°$ |
| **total** | **40** | **$360°$** ✓ |
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Reading a pie chart.** In a pie chart of a farmer's crops, cassava has an angle of $120°$. The farm is 6 hectares. What area is cassava?

$$\frac{120}{360} \times 6 = 2 \text{ hectares}$$
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
In pie-chart questions, first find **what one degree is worth** (or what one unit is worth in degrees). Every other part of the question then takes one step.
:::

## Histograms and frequency polygons

A **histogram** is used for **continuous or grouped** data. It looks like a bar chart, but:

- the bars **touch**, with **no gaps**;
- each bar runs between the **class boundaries**;
- with equal class widths, the **height** is the frequency.

A **frequency polygon** joins the **midpoints of the tops** of the bars with straight lines, closed at each end by joining to the axis at the midpoint of an empty class.

![A histogram of the marks of 40 students in classes 1–10 to 51–60 with frequencies 3, 7, 12, 10, 6, 2; the bars run between the class boundaries 0.5 to 60.5 and the frequency polygon joins the midpoints of their tops](https://wasscetutor.com/img/mathematics/histogram-polygon.svg)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The marks of 40 students are grouped below. Draw a histogram and frequency polygon.**

| Marks | 1–10 | 11–20 | 21–30 | 31–40 | 41–50 | 51–60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3 | 7 | 12 | 10 | 6 | 2 |

1. Mark the **class boundaries** $0.5, 10.5, 20.5, \dots, 60.5$ on the horizontal axis.
2. Draw each bar from boundary to boundary with height equal to the frequency.
3. Plot the frequency polygon at the midpoints $5.5, 15.5, 25.5, 35.5, 45.5, 55.5$, and close it at $-4.5$ and $65.5$.

**Reading:** the **modal class** (the tallest bar) is $21$–$30$; $10 + 6 + 2 = 18$ students scored more than 30.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Gaps between histogram bars**, or bars drawn at the class limits ($1$, $10$, $11$, $20$…) instead of the boundaries. A histogram shows continuous data, so the bars must touch at the boundaries.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Rainfall in Bo.** A weather station records monthly rainfall. A bar chart of the twelve months shows the rainy season clearly: low bars from December to March, rising through May, the tallest bars in July and August, then falling in October. A farmer planning when to plant rice reads this chart in seconds, while the same information in a list of numbers takes much longer to understand.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Is the time taken to walk to school discrete or continuous?"}
**Continuous**: time is measured and can take any value in a range.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. For the class 15–19 (whole numbers), give the class boundaries, width and midpoint."}
Boundaries $14.5$ and $19.5$; width $5$; midpoint $17$.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. In a survey of 60 people, 25 prefer football. What angle represents football on a pie chart?"}
$\dfrac{25}{60} \times 360° = 150°$.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. A pie chart shows a family's monthly spending of NLe 3600. Food has an angle of 140°. How much is spent on food?"}
$\dfrac{140}{360} \times 3600 = $ NLe 1400.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. In the marks table above, how many students scored 20 or less? What percentage is that?"}
$3 + 7 = 10$ students; $\dfrac{10}{40} \times 100 = 25\%$.
:::

## Key points

- Data can be **qualitative**, **discrete** or **continuous**.
- A **frequency table** (with tallies) organises raw data; check the total.
- For a class: **boundaries** halfway between classes, **width** = difference of boundaries, **midpoint** = average of the limits.
- **Bar chart**: gaps between bars. **Pie chart**: angle $= \dfrac{f}{\text{total}} \times 360°$.
- **Histogram**: no gaps, bars between boundaries. **Frequency polygon**: join the midpoints of the bar tops.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Statistics questions in Paper 2 usually begin with a table: complete a frequency or cumulative frequency table, draw a pie chart or histogram to a given scale, then calculate an average or read a value. Objective questions test pie-chart angles, reading charts and class boundaries. Label axes, give charts a title and use the scale stated in the question.
:::
