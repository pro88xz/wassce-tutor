# Transport in lower organisms: exchange surfaces and specialised adaptations

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- explain how Amoeba and Paramecium exchange materials
- describe gas exchange in simple aquatic organisms
- relate body size and surface-area-to-volume ratio to transport needs
- explain why larger organisms need specialised exchange and transport systems
- interpret simple experiments involving diffusion and osmosis

## Exchange across the cell surface

Amoeba is a unicellular freshwater organism. Its cell membrane forms the boundary between cytoplasm and the surrounding water. Dissolved oxygen diffuses through the membrane into the cell and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Soluble nutrients can enter through the cell surface, while undigested food is expelled. Because the organism is only one cell thick, diffusion distances are very short.

Paramecium is also unicellular but has a more definite shape and is covered by cilia. The cilia move the organism through water and help direct food particles towards the oral groove. Digestion occurs in food vacuoles. Dissolved gases still exchange across the cell surface by diffusion. Contractile vacuoles collect and remove excess water, helping maintain water balance.

These organisms do not have lungs, gills, blood or a heart. Their small size and direct contact between the cell membrane and the environment make specialised organs unnecessary.

## Surface-area-to-volume ratio

Surface area is the area available for exchange with the environment. Volume represents the amount of living material that requires supplies and produces waste. As an organism becomes larger in a similar shape, its volume increases more rapidly than its surface area. Its surface-area-to-volume ratio therefore decreases.

A small organism has a relatively large surface area compared with its volume. It can often obtain enough oxygen and nutrients and remove wastes by diffusion across its body surface. A large organism has a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio and many cells are far from the external surface. Diffusion alone would be too slow to meet the needs of all cells. Large organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces, ventilation mechanisms and transport systems.

## Features of efficient exchange surfaces

An efficient exchange surface generally has:

- a large surface area to provide more space for exchange
- a thin barrier to reduce diffusion distance
- a moist surface so gases can dissolve before crossing
- a steep concentration gradient maintained by movement of the medium or transport of substances away from the surface

For example, the lungs of mammals contain many alveoli. Their walls are thin and moist, and they are surrounded by capillaries. Ventilation brings fresh air into the lungs, while blood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide to the alveoli. Fish gills use a large area of thin filaments and lamellae, with water flowing over the exchange surface and blood flowing through it.

## Diffusion in practical investigations

A common school investigation uses agar jelly containing an indicator that changes colour when acid diffuses into it. Cubes of different sizes can be placed in the same acid solution for the same time. Smaller cubes usually show a greater proportion of their volume affected because they have a larger surface-area-to-volume ratio and shorter diffusion distances.

For a fair comparison, keep the agar composition, acid concentration, temperature and exposure time the same. Measure the depth or proportion of the cube that changes colour. A useful calculation is:

percentage penetrated = (penetrated volume ÷ total cube volume) × 100

The experiment models diffusion into tissues, but agar is not living tissue and the model does not reproduce active transport, circulation or metabolism.

## Comparing simple and complex organisms

Amoeba exchanges gases directly across its cell membrane. A human exchanges gases through the lungs and transports them using blood and the circulatory system. The difference is not that diffusion stops working in humans: diffusion still moves oxygen from alveoli into blood and from blood into body cells. The difference is that specialised systems bring the external medium and blood close to cells and maintain concentration gradients over long distances.

## Quick check

1. Why can Amoeba exchange oxygen without a respiratory organ?
2. What happens to surface-area-to-volume ratio as a similarly shaped organism becomes larger?
3. State three features of an efficient gas-exchange surface.
4. In an agar-cube experiment, why must temperature and acid concentration be controlled?

**Answers:** 1. It is unicellular, has a large surface-area-to-volume ratio and a short diffusion distance. 2. It decreases. 3. Large area, thin barrier, moist surface and maintained concentration gradient (any three). 4. They affect diffusion rate and would make comparisons unfair if they varied.
