Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the earth's crust, yet 200 years ago it was more precious than gold, because nobody could extract it cheaply. Electrolysis changed that. This lesson covers how aluminium is extracted from bauxite: first purifying the ore, then electrolysing it. It shows why the process needs so much electricity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the occurrence of aluminium and name its main ore
- explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis
- describe the purification of bauxite (the Bayer process)
- describe the electrolytic extraction of aluminium, including the role of cryolite
- write the electrode equations and explain why the anodes are replaced
- calculate the mass of aluminium produced using Faraday's laws

## Occurrence

- About **8%** of the earth's crust: the most abundant metal.
- Never found free, because it is too reactive.
- Main ore: **bauxite**, impure hydrated aluminium oxide, **Al₂O₃·2H₂O**. Its main impurities are **iron(III) oxide** (which colours it red-brown) and **silica** (sand).
- Also in **clays** and many rocks, but extraction from these is not economic.

## Why electrolysis?

Aluminium is **above carbon** in the reactivity series, so carbon cannot reduce aluminium oxide at a practical temperature. Its oxide is very stable. It must be extracted by **electrolysis**.

## Stage 1: purifying bauxite (Bayer process)

The electrolysis needs **pure aluminium oxide (alumina)**. Bauxite is purified using the **amphoteric** nature of aluminium oxide:

1. Crushed bauxite is heated under pressure with **concentrated sodium hydroxide** solution. Aluminium oxide dissolves (it is amphoteric), forming sodium aluminate:

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaAl(OH)}_4\text{(aq)}$$

2. **Iron(III) oxide** does not dissolve (it is basic). It is **filtered off** as "red mud".
3. The solution is cooled and **seeded** with crystals of aluminium hydroxide, which precipitates:

$$\text{NaAl(OH)}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Al(OH)}_3\text{(s)} + \text{NaOH(aq)}$$

4. The aluminium hydroxide is filtered, washed and **heated** to give pure alumina:

$$2\text{Al(OH)}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$$

## Stage 2: electrolysis (Hall–Héroult process)

![Electrolysis cell for aluminium: alumina dissolved in molten cryolite at about 950 °C, carbon anodes dipping into the melt, carbon-lined steel tank as cathode, molten aluminium collecting at the bottom](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/aluminium-cell.svg)

*Figure 1. Extracting aluminium by electrolysis. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Feature | Detail and reason |
|---|---|
| **Electrolyte** | Alumina dissolved in **molten cryolite** (Na₃AlF₆) |
| **Why cryolite?** | Pure alumina melts at about **2050 °C**. Dissolved in cryolite, it can be electrolysed at about **950 °C**, saving a great deal of energy, and the molten mixture conducts better |
| **Cathode (−)** | **Carbon lining** of the steel tank |
| **Anodes (+)** | Large **carbon (graphite) blocks** |
| **Product** | Molten aluminium sinks to the bottom and is **tapped off** |

**Electrode reactions:**

- **Cathode (reduction):** Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
- **Anode (oxidation):** 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

**Overall:** 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂

**Why the anodes must be replaced:** at about 950 °C, the oxygen formed at the anodes **reacts with the carbon**, burning them away as carbon(IV) oxide (C + O₂ → CO₂). The anodes become thinner and are replaced regularly.

## Energy and cost

Each mole of aluminium (27 g) needs **3 moles of electrons** (3 faradays), and the cell must be kept molten. The process uses **enormous amounts of electricity**, so aluminium smelters are built near **cheap electricity**, especially **hydroelectric** power stations.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**What mass of aluminium is produced by a current of 100 000 A flowing for 1 hour?** (Al = 27, F = 96 500 C/mol)

1. $Q = It = 100\,000 \times 3600 = 3.6 \times 10^8$ C
2. Moles of electrons $= 3.6 \times 10^8 \div 96\,500 \approx 3731$ mol
3. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al, so moles of Al $= 3731 \div 3 \approx 1244$ mol
4. Mass $= 1244 \times 27 \approx 33\,600$ g $\approx 33.6$ kg

One cell running for an hour makes only about 34 kg of aluminium.
:::

**Recycling** aluminium needs only about **5% of the energy** used to extract it from bauxite, which is why collecting old cans and scrap pays.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone mines **bauxite** in the **Moyamba district**, and the ore is **exported** for processing. Building a smelter would need a very large and reliable electricity supply, far more than the country now generates, which is why the value-adding step happens abroad. The red-brown colour of the ore comes from its **iron(III) oxide** impurity, the "red mud" removed in the Bayer process.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Cryolite is not the ore of aluminium, and it is not used up. It is a **solvent** that lowers the working temperature from about 2050 °C to about 950 °C.
:::

## Key points

- Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the crust; its ore is **bauxite**, Al₂O₃·2H₂O.
- It is above carbon in the reactivity series, so it is extracted by **electrolysis**.
- **Bayer process:** bauxite + hot conc. NaOH → sodium aluminate; Fe₂O₃ filtered off; Al(OH)₃ precipitated and heated to **pure Al₂O₃**.
- **Electrolysis:** Al₂O₃ in molten **cryolite** at about 950 °C; **carbon** anodes and cathode lining. Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.
- **Anodes burn away** (C + O₂ → CO₂) and must be replaced.
- The process uses **huge amounts of electricity**; recycling saves about 95% of the energy.

## Quick check

1. Why is aluminium not extracted by heating its oxide with carbon?
2. What is the purpose of cryolite in the extraction?
3. Write the equation for the reaction at the cathode.
4. Why must the carbon anodes be replaced regularly?

**Answers:**

1. Aluminium is more reactive than carbon (above it in the reactivity series), so carbon cannot reduce its oxide.
2. It dissolves the alumina and lowers the working temperature from about 2050 °C to about 950 °C, saving energy.
3. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al.
4. The oxygen formed at the anodes reacts with the hot carbon and burns it away as CO₂.
