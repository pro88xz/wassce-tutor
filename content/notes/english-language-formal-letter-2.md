Once the format is automatic, the marks in a formal letter come from **what you say** and **how you say it**: a clear purpose, well-developed points, and a tone that is polite, firm and businesslike. This lesson covers the four kinds of formal letter WAEC sets most often, the phrases that open and close them well, and a complete letter to the editor that you can study paragraph by paragraph.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- recognise the four common kinds of formal letter and what each must contain
- write in a formal register: polite, firm, clear, with no slang or contractions
- open a formal letter with a sentence that states its purpose at once
- develop each point in its own paragraph
- end with a clear request or recommendation

## The four kinds WAEC sets most

| Kind | Written to | It must |
|---|---|---|
| **Complaint** | an official or organisation responsible for a problem | describe the problem, its effects, and what you want done |
| **Request / appeal** | someone who can grant something | say what you want, why, and how it will help |
| **Application** | an employer, a school, a scholarship board | say what you are applying for, why you are suitable, and when you are available |
| **Letter to the editor** | the editor of a newspaper, for publication | give your view on a public issue, with reasons and solutions |

:::callout{type=tip}
A **letter to the editor** is written for **the newspaper's readers**, not just the editor. Address it to the Editor (*Dear Sir,*), but write so that the public will be persuaded.
:::

## The formal register

:::callout{type=definition}
**Register** is the level of formality in your language. A formal letter uses a **formal register**: complete sentences, standard vocabulary, a polite and respectful tone, and no slang, contractions or text-message spellings.
:::

| Informal: avoid | Formal: use |
|---|---|
| I'm, don't, can't, it's | I am, do not, cannot, it is |
| a lot of, loads of | many, a great deal of, a large number of |
| kids, guys | children, young people, residents |
| get, got | obtain, receive, become |
| fix it quick | repair it promptly |
| I want you to… | I would be grateful if you could… / I appeal to you to… |
| Thanks a lot! | Thank you for your kind consideration. |

### Polite but firm

A complaint can be firm without being rude. Compare:

| Rude | Weak | Polite and firm |
|---|---|---|
| *You people are useless and lazy.* | *Maybe you could look at it sometime if you have time.* | *I must express my deep concern that, despite three reports, the burst pipe has not been repaired.* |

## Opening sentences that work

Your first sentence should tell the reader **why you are writing**. Then the reader knows how to read everything that follows.

| Kind | Opening |
|---|---|
| Complaint | *I am writing to complain about the irregular supply of water to Kissy Mess-Mess, which has now continued for six weeks.* |
| Request | *On behalf of the students of Bo School, I write to request the use of the town hall for our inter-house debate on 14th June.* |
| Application | *I wish to apply for the post of Sales Assistant advertised in Awoko of 2nd May, 2026.* |
| Letter to the editor | *Please allow me space in your widely read newspaper to draw public attention to the growing danger of kush among our young people.* |

## Closing paragraphs that work

End with what you want to happen, then a polite final line.

- *I therefore appeal to the Corporation to repair the main pipe without further delay.*
- *I would be grateful if my application could be considered favourably.*
- *I hope that the authorities will act before more lives are lost.*
- *Thank you for your kind consideration. / I look forward to your favourable reply.*

## A complete letter to the editor

::::model{title="Letter to the Editor on the danger of kush" label="Model formal letter"}
:::right
14 Fort Street,\
Freetown.

20th May, 2026.
:::

The Editor,\
Awoko Newspaper,\
Freetown.

Dear Sir,

**KUSH IS DESTROYING OUR YOUTH: WE MUST ALL ACT**

Please allow me space in your widely read newspaper to draw public attention to the growing use of kush among the young people of this country, and to suggest what each of us can do about it.

Kush is a cheap synthetic drug, sold in small wraps for a few leones, and it has spread with frightening speed through our cities and towns. Anyone who walks through certain streets in the east of Freetown in the evening will see young men bent double or standing motionless for long minutes, unaware of the traffic around them. Many of them were students only a year or two ago, and some were the best footballers, musicians and scholars of their streets.

The first and most serious effect of the drug is on health. Users suffer confusion, violent mood swings, swollen legs and, in many cases, lasting mental illness. Doctors at the psychiatric hospital report that their wards are overcrowded with young patients, some of them barely fifteen years old. Families who cannot afford treatment watch helplessly as their sons and daughters waste away, and every week brings news of another young life lost.

The second effect is on education and work. A young person in the grip of kush stops attending school, abandons an apprenticeship or a trade, and becomes a burden on the family instead of its hope. Parents who sold land or took loans to pay school fees see that sacrifice thrown away, and younger brothers and sisters lose the example they should have followed. The talent the nation needs for its future is being lost on street corners.

Thirdly, the drug is driving crime. Users who have spent all their money steal phones, bags and even household goods to buy the next wrap. Whole communities now lock their doors early and live in fear, and the users themselves are often beaten, or worse, when they are caught, which only adds violence to violence.

What can be done? The Government has rightly declared kush a national emergency, but a declaration is not enough. The police must target the suppliers rather than only the users, who need treatment more than punishment. Rehabilitation centres should be opened in every district, not only in the capital. Schools, mosques and churches should talk openly about the drug, and the musicians and footballers whom young people admire could lend their voices to the campaign. Above all, parents should watch their children closely and seek help early rather than hiding the problem out of shame.

Kush is not someone else's problem. It is in our streets, our schools and, too often, our homes. If we act together now, we can still save a generation.

:::right
Yours faithfully,

*(signature)*

**Mohamed Turay**
:::
::::

### How it earns the marks

| Paragraph | Its job | Why it scores |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | purpose: *"Please allow me space…"* | the classic, correct opening of a letter to the editor |
| 2 | background: what kush is, what you see | a concrete, local picture, not a general statement |
| 3 | effect 1: health | topic sentence, details, a source (doctors), human cost |
| 4 | effect 2: education and work | links the problem to the nation's future |
| 5 | effect 3: crime | shows the community is affected, not only users |
| 6 | solutions | specific, practical, aimed at police, government, schools, families |
| 7 | conclusion | a memorable call to action |

It is about **450 words in the body**, with formal language throughout (*rightly declared*, *rehabilitation*, *seek help early*) and not one contraction.

## An application letter: the key paragraphs

An application letter follows the same format. Its body has a fixed order:

1. **The post** and where you saw it advertised.
2. **Your qualifications**: school, examinations, results.
3. **Your experience and qualities**: why you are suitable.
4. **Your availability**, and an offer to attend an interview.
5. **A polite close.**

:::callout{type=worked-example}
*I wish to apply for the post of Sales Assistant advertised in Awoko of 2nd May, 2026.*

*I completed my secondary education at Government Secondary School, Bo, last year, and I passed six subjects in the WASSCE, including credits in English Language, Mathematics and Commerce.*

*During the long vacations I have helped in my aunt's provisions shop at Bo Main Market, where I serve customers, keep the daily sales record and check deliveries against invoices. I am honest, punctual and comfortable speaking Krio, Mende and English with customers.*

*I am available to start work immediately and would welcome the opportunity to attend an interview at your convenience.*
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Begging instead of applying.** *"Please, sir, I am suffering, help me with this job, God will bless you"* shows no qualifications and sounds desperate. An application **presents your strengths**. Let the employer decide that you deserve the job.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Make these sentences formal."}
| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| We're really fed up with the noise. | We are deeply concerned about the noise. |
| Can you fix the road quick? | We appeal to the Council to repair the road promptly. |
| There's loads of rubbish everywhere. | Large quantities of refuse have accumulated in the area. |
| Thanks a lot for reading this. | Thank you for your kind consideration. |
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Write an opening sentence for a complaint to EDSA about frequent power cuts in your area."}
*I am writing to complain about the frequent and unannounced power cuts in Lumley, which have now affected residents and businesses for over a month.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Write the closing paragraph of a request to your principal for a school football pitch to be levelled."}
*We therefore humbly request that the pitch be levelled before the inter-house sports competition in March. The sports prefects are willing to organise students to clear stones and grass to reduce the cost. Thank you for your kind consideration of this request.*
:::

## Key points

- Know the four kinds: **complaint, request, application, letter to the editor**, and what each must contain.
- Write in a **formal register**: complete sentences, standard words, **no contractions or slang**.
- Be **polite and firm**, never rude and never pleading.
- **Open** by stating your purpose; give **one point per paragraph**; **close** with what you want done.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Examiners mark the **content** (relevant, developed points), the **organisation** (format and paragraphs) and especially the **expression**: is the tone right for an official reader? A letter that is perfectly set out but written like a text message to a friend loses heavily under Expression.
:::
