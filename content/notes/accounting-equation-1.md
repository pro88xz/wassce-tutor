The **accounting equation** states that **Assets = Capital + Liabilities**. Everything a business **owns** (its assets) has been provided either by the **owner** (capital) or by **outsiders** (liabilities). Because both sides describe the same resources from two points of view, the equation **always balances**, and it is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping and of the statement of financial position. This lesson defines assets, liabilities and capital, rearranges the equation, and uses it to find missing figures.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the accounting equation and explain why it always balances
- define assets, liabilities and capital, and classify items correctly
- distinguish non-current from current assets, and non-current from current liabilities
- rearrange the equation to find capital, assets or liabilities
- explain how profit, drawings and new capital change the owner's capital

## The equation

![A balance with assets of NLe 50,000 on one side and capital of NLe 35,000 plus liabilities of NLe 15,000 on the other, perfectly level](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/accounting-equation.svg)

*Figure 1. The accounting equation as a balance. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Capital} + \text{Liabilities}$$

It can be rearranged:

$$\text{Capital} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$$

$$\text{Liabilities} = \text{Assets} - \text{Capital}$$

**Capital** is the owner's claim on the business, also called **owner's equity** or **net assets** (assets minus liabilities).

## Assets, liabilities and capital

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets** | resources owned or controlled by the business, expected to bring future benefit | land, buildings, motor vehicles, equipment, stock, trade receivables, cash at bank, cash in hand |
| **Liabilities** | amounts the business **owes** to outsiders | loans, bank overdraft, trade payables, accrued expenses |
| **Capital** | the amount the business **owes to the owner**: money and assets invested plus profits kept in the business | capital introduced, retained profits |

### Classifying assets and liabilities

| Non-current assets | Current assets |
|---|---|
| held for **long-term use** (more than a year), not for resale | held for a **short time**; change regularly |
| land, buildings, machinery, motor vehicles, furniture and fittings | inventory (stock), trade receivables, prepayments, cash at bank, cash in hand |

| Non-current liabilities | Current liabilities |
|---|---|
| due after **more than one year** | due **within one year** |
| long-term bank loan, mortgage | trade payables, bank overdraft, accrued expenses |

## Using the equation

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Musa's business has: premises NLe 90,000, motor van NLe 30,000, inventory NLe 12,000, trade receivables NLe 6,000, cash at bank NLe 7,000, a bank loan of NLe 40,000 and trade payables of NLe 9,000. Find his capital.**

Assets $= 90\,000 + 30\,000 + 12\,000 + 6\,000 + 7\,000 = 145\,000$

Liabilities $= 40\,000 + 9\,000 = 49\,000$

Capital $= 145\,000 - 49\,000 = 96\,000$

Check: $96\,000 + 49\,000 = 145\,000$ ✓
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A business has capital of NLe 60,000 and liabilities of NLe 25,000. What are its total assets?**

Assets $= 60\,000 + 25\,000 = 85\,000$
:::

## How capital changes

Capital at the end of a period is:

$$\text{Closing capital} = \text{Opening capital} + \text{Capital introduced} + \text{Profit} - \text{Drawings}$$

- **Profit** increases capital; a **loss** reduces it.
- **Drawings** (cash or goods taken by the owner for private use) reduce capital.
- **Capital introduced** (new money put in by the owner) increases capital.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Opening capital was NLe 80,000. During the year the owner introduced NLe 10,000 more, made a profit of NLe 34,000 and took drawings of NLe 18,000. Find the closing capital.**

Closing capital $= 80\,000 + 10\,000 + 34\,000 - 18\,000 = 106\,000$
:::

This relationship is also used to **find profit** when only the capital at the start and end is known: see the lesson on incomplete records.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **bank overdraft** is a **liability**, not an asset: the business owes the bank. And **trade receivables** (debtors) are **assets**, because they are amounts owed **to** the business.
:::

## Key points

- **Assets = Capital + Liabilities**, so **Capital = Assets − Liabilities**.
- **Assets:** what the business owns. **Liabilities:** what it owes outsiders. **Capital:** what it owes the owner.
- **Non-current** items last or fall due after more than a year; **current** items within a year.
- **Closing capital = opening capital + capital introduced + profit − drawings.**
- The equation always balances because it describes the same resources in two ways.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **state the accounting equation**, to **calculate a missing figure** (usually capital), to **classify items** as assets or liabilities, current or non-current, and to **calculate closing capital** from profit and drawings. Objective questions on the equation are very common.
:::

## Quick check

1. State the accounting equation.
2. Classify: bank overdraft, motor vehicle, trade receivables, long-term loan.
3. Assets are NLe 70,000 and liabilities NLe 22,000. Find the capital.
4. Opening capital NLe 50,000, profit NLe 20,000, drawings NLe 12,000. Find the closing capital.
5. Why does the accounting equation always balance?

**Answers:**

1. Assets = Capital + Liabilities.
2. Bank overdraft: current liability; motor vehicle: non-current asset; trade receivables: current asset; long-term loan: non-current liability.
3. $70\,000 - 22\,000 = 48\,000$: NLe 48,000.
4. $50\,000 + 20\,000 - 12\,000 = 58\,000$: NLe 58,000.
5. Because every asset has been provided either by the owner (capital) or by outsiders (liabilities), so both sides measure the same resources.
