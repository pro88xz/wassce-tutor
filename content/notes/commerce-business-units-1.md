Most businesses in Sierra Leone start small: one person with a stall, a kiosk or a workshop. As they grow, owners often take in partners to raise more money and share the work. This lesson covers the two simplest forms of business ownership, the sole proprietorship and the partnership, and the trade-offs each involves.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a business unit and distinguish the private and public sectors
- describe the features, advantages and disadvantages of a sole proprietorship
- describe the features, advantages and disadvantages of a partnership
- state the contents of a partnership deed and name the types of partners
- explain why small businesses survive alongside large firms

## Business units

:::callout{type=definition}
A **business unit** is an organisation set up to produce or distribute goods and services.
:::

Business units are found in two sectors:

| Private sector | Public sector |
|---|---|
| Owned by individuals or groups | Owned by the government |
| Sole proprietorships, partnerships, companies, cooperatives | Government departments, public corporations |
| Main aim: profit | Main aim: service to the public |

## The sole proprietorship (sole trader)

:::callout{type=definition}
A **sole proprietorship** is a business **owned, financed and controlled by one person**, who takes all the profit and bears all the losses.
:::

It is the oldest and most common form of business: market traders, kiosk owners, tailors, hairdressers, mechanics and small shopkeepers.

**Features:**

1. Owned by **one person** (who may employ others).
2. **Unlimited liability**: the owner's personal property can be taken to pay business debts.
3. Capital comes mainly from **personal savings**, family and small loans.
4. Easy to start, with few legal formalities (registration of a business name).
5. No **separate legal entity**: the owner and the business are the same in law.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Easy and cheap to form | **Unlimited liability** |
| Owner keeps **all the profit** | **Limited capital**, so growth is slow |
| **Quick decisions**; flexible | Owner bears **all losses** and risks |
| **Close contact** with customers | Long hours; no one to share the work |
| Personal attention and **secrecy** of affairs | **Lack of continuity**: the business may end with the owner's illness or death |
| Strong motivation to work hard | Limited skills: one person cannot be expert in everything |

## The partnership

:::callout{type=definition}
A **partnership** is an association of **two to twenty** persons who agree to carry on a business together with a view to profit, sharing the capital, management and profits or losses.
:::

Partnerships are common among lawyers, accountants, doctors, estate agents and family businesses.

### The partnership deed

Partners should sign a **partnership deed (agreement)** setting out:

1. the name and address of the business and the partners
2. the nature of the business
3. the **capital** contributed by each partner
4. how **profits and losses** are shared
5. the **duties and powers** of each partner
6. interest on capital and drawings, and partners' salaries
7. how disputes are settled
8. how a partner may **leave** and how new partners are admitted
9. what happens on **dissolution**

If there is no deed, the law usually treats partners as sharing profits and losses **equally**.

### Types of partners

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Active (general) partner** | Contributes capital and takes part in managing the business; unlimited liability |
| **Sleeping (dormant) partner** | Contributes capital but takes no part in management |
| **Nominal partner** | Lends their name (reputation) but contributes no capital and takes no profit |
| **Limited partner** | Liability limited to their capital; cannot take part in management (only in a limited partnership, which must have at least one general partner) |
| **Minor** | A person under the age of majority admitted to the benefits of a partnership |

| Advantages of a partnership | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **More capital** than a sole trader | **Unlimited liability** (for general partners) |
| **Shared work, skills and ideas** | Profits are shared |
| Losses are shared | **Disagreements** can paralyse the business |
| Easy to form | **Slow decisions**: partners must agree |
| Continuity is better than a sole trader's | **Limited life**: death or withdrawal of a partner may dissolve it |
| Business affairs remain private | Capital is still limited (maximum of 20 partners) |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Sharing partnership profit.** Fatu and Musa run a shop. Fatu contributed NLe 30,000 and Musa NLe 20,000. Their deed says profits are shared in the **ratio of capital**. The profit for the year is NLe 15,000.

Ratio $= 30000 : 20000 = 3 : 2$.

- Fatu's share $= \dfrac{3}{5} \times 15000 = $ NLe 9,000.
- Musa's share $= \dfrac{2}{5} \times 15000 = $ NLe 6,000.
:::

## Why small businesses survive

Despite competition from supermarkets and large companies, small firms remain the majority because:

1. they need **little capital** to start
2. they give **personal service** and often sell on **credit** to regular customers
3. they open **long hours** and are close to customers' homes
4. they sell in **very small quantities** (a single cube of seasoning, a cup of rice)
5. some goods and services need **individual attention** (tailoring, hairdressing, repairs)
6. the **market may be too small** for large firms
7. many people prefer to be **their own boss**

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Unlimited liability means the **owner** can lose personal property, not that the **business** has unlimited money. A sole trader with a debt of NLe 50,000 and business assets worth NLe 20,000 may have to sell personal property to pay the remaining NLe 30,000.
:::

## Key points

- A **sole proprietorship** has one owner, unlimited liability, quick decisions and limited capital.
- A **partnership** has 2 to 20 partners, more capital and skill, but unlimited liability and possible disagreements.
- A **partnership deed** sets out capital, profit-sharing, duties and dissolution.
- Partners may be **active, sleeping, nominal or limited**.
- Small businesses survive through **personal service, credit, convenience and low capital needs**.
