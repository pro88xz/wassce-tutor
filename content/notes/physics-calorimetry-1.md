Put an empty iron pot and a pot of water on two identical fires. The empty pot is too hot to touch in a minute; the water is barely warm. Different substances need very different amounts of heat to warm up — and that difference is measured by specific heat capacity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define heat capacity and specific heat capacity and state their units
- use $Q = mc\Delta\theta$ and $Q = C\Delta\theta$
- explain the importance of the high specific heat capacity of water
- solve problems involving heaters, power and temperature rise

## Heat capacity

:::callout{type=definition}
The **heat capacity** $C$ of a body is the quantity of heat needed to raise the temperature of the **whole body** by **1 K** (or 1 °C):

$$C = \dfrac{Q}{\Delta\theta} \quad (\text{unit: J K}^{-1})$$
:::

Heat capacity belongs to a particular **object**: a large pot of water has a larger heat capacity than a cup of water.

## Specific heat capacity

:::callout{type=definition}
The **specific heat capacity** $c$ of a substance is the quantity of heat needed to raise the temperature of **1 kg** of the substance by **1 K**:

$$c = \dfrac{Q}{m\,\Delta\theta} \quad (\text{unit: J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1})$$
:::

So the heat absorbed (or given out) when a mass $m$ changes temperature by $\Delta\theta$ is

$$Q = mc\Delta\theta \qquad\text{and}\qquad C = mc$$

| Substance | $c$ / J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| Water | 4200 |
| Ice | 2100 |
| Aluminium | 900 |
| Glass | 670 |
| Iron / steel | 450 |
| Copper | 400 |
| Lead | 130 |
| Mercury | 140 |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of 2 kg of water from 25 °C to 100 °C? ($c = 4200$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)**

$$Q = mc\Delta\theta = 2 \times 4200 \times 75 = 630\,000\ \text{J} = 630\ \text{kJ}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 500 g copper block absorbs 4000 J of heat. By how much does its temperature rise? ($c = 400$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)**

$$\Delta\theta = \dfrac{Q}{mc} = \dfrac{4000}{0.5 \times 400} = 20\ \text{K}$$

The same 4000 J would warm 0.5 kg of water by only $\dfrac{4000}{0.5 \times 4200} = 1.9$ K.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Always convert grams to **kilograms** before using $Q = mc\Delta\theta$ with $c$ in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. And $\Delta\theta$ is the **change** in temperature: a rise from 20 °C to 60 °C is 40 K, not 60.
:::

## Heating with an electric heater

An electric heater of power $P$ supplies energy $Pt$ in time $t$. If all of it goes into the substance:

$$Pt = mc\Delta\theta$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 2 kW electric kettle holds 1.5 kg of water at 20 °C. How long does it take to bring the water to 100 °C, assuming no heat loss?**

$$t = \dfrac{mc\Delta\theta}{P} = \dfrac{1.5 \times 4200 \times 80}{2000} = 252\ \text{s} \approx 4.2\ \text{minutes}$$

In practice it takes a little longer, because some heat warms the kettle and escapes to the surroundings.
:::

## Why water's high specific heat capacity matters

Water has one of the **highest** specific heat capacities of any common substance. This means it absorbs a lot of heat for a small temperature rise and releases a lot as it cools.

- **Cooling systems**: car radiators and generator engines use water to carry heat away.
- **Hot-water bottles and heating** systems store a lot of heat in water.
- **Climate**: the sea warms and cools slowly, so coastal places such as Freetown have milder temperature changes between day and night than inland places far from the sea.
- **Sea and land breezes** are driven partly by land heating faster than sea (covered in heat transfer).
- **The body**: being mostly water, it resists sudden temperature changes.

:::callout{type=local-example}
At midday on Lumley Beach, the sand burns your feet while the sea is pleasantly cool. Sand has a much lower specific heat capacity than water, so the same sunshine raises its temperature far more.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Heat capacity**: the heat needed to raise the temperature of a body by 1 K.
- **Specific heat capacity**: the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K.
:::

## Key points

- Heat capacity $C = Q/\Delta\theta$ (J K⁻¹) belongs to an object; specific heat capacity $c = Q/(m\Delta\theta)$ (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) belongs to a substance.
- $Q = mc\Delta\theta$; $C = mc$.
- With an electric heater (no losses): $Pt = mc\Delta\theta$.
- Water's high $c$ (4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) makes it an excellent coolant and moderates coastal climates.

## Quick check

1. State the unit of specific heat capacity.
2. Find the heat needed to warm 0.5 kg of aluminium by 40 K ($c = 900$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).
3. Why is water used as a coolant in engines?

**Answers:** 1. J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. 2. $0.5 \times 900 \times 40 = 18\,000$ J. 3. Its high specific heat capacity lets it absorb a lot of heat with only a small rise in temperature.
