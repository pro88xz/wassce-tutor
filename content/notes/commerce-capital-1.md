Every business, from a roadside kiosk to a mining company, needs money before it can earn any: to buy stock, rent premises and pay workers. That money is the business's capital. This lesson explains the different kinds of capital and where a business can get it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define business capital
- distinguish fixed, working, owned, borrowed and employed capital
- explain the types of share capital in a company
- describe the internal and external sources of business capital
- classify sources of finance as short, medium or long term

## What is business capital?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Business capital** is the money and other assets used to start and run a business. It includes cash, stock, equipment, buildings and vehicles owned by the business.
:::

## Types of capital

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed capital** | Assets kept for a long time and used to produce or sell; not bought to resell | Buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture |
| **Working (circulating) capital** | Current assets minus current liabilities: the funds that keep day-to-day operations going | Stock, debtors and cash, less creditors and overdraft |
| **Liquid capital** | Cash and assets quickly turned into cash | Cash in hand and at the bank |
| **Capital owned** | The owner's investment in the business: total assets minus liabilities | The trader's own money plus retained profit |
| **Borrowed capital** | Funds obtained from outsiders that must be repaid | Bank loans, debentures |
| **Capital employed** | Fixed assets plus working capital: the total funds used in the business | |

### Share capital (companies)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Authorised (nominal or registered) capital** | The maximum share capital the company may issue, stated in its Memorandum of Association |
| **Issued capital** | The part of the authorised capital actually offered to shareholders |
| **Called-up capital** | The part of the issued capital that shareholders have been asked to pay so far |
| **Paid-up capital** | The amount actually paid by shareholders |
| **Uncalled capital** | Issued capital not yet called up |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A company's share capital.** A company is authorised to issue 1,000,000 shares of NLe 1 each. It issues 600,000 shares and calls up NLe 0.75 per share; shareholders have paid NLe 420,000.

- Authorised capital = NLe 1,000,000
- Issued capital = NLe 600,000
- Called-up capital = $600000 \times 0.75 = $ NLe 450,000
- Paid-up capital = NLe 420,000
- Calls in arrear (called but unpaid) $= 450000 - 420000 = $ NLe 30,000
- Uncalled capital $= 600000 - 450000 = $ NLe 150,000
:::

## Sources of business capital

### Internal sources (from within the business)

1. **Personal savings** of the owner.
2. **Retained (ploughed-back) profit**: profit kept in the business instead of being withdrawn or paid as dividends.
3. **Sale of unwanted assets**.
4. **Depreciation provisions** set aside to replace assets.

### External sources (from outside)

| Source | How it works | Period |
|---|---|---|
| **Family and friends** | Informal loans or gifts | Short to medium |
| **Bank overdraft** | Permission to withdraw more than is in a current account, up to a limit | Short |
| **Bank loan** | A fixed sum repaid with interest over an agreed period | Short to long |
| **Trade credit** | Suppliers allow goods now and payment later (for example 30 days) | Short |
| **Hire purchase** | Use an asset while paying in instalments; ownership passes at the end | Medium |
| **Leasing** | Renting an asset (vehicle, machine) instead of buying it | Medium |
| **Shares** | Selling part-ownership of a company to investors | Long (permanent) |
| **Debentures** | Long-term loans to a company at a fixed interest | Long |
| **Microfinance and cooperatives** | Small loans to traders and small firms, often in groups | Short to medium |
| **Government grants and schemes** | Grants or cheap loans for priority sectors | Medium to long |
| **Venture capital** | Investors fund new, risky firms in exchange for a share of ownership | Long |

### Choosing a source

A business considers: the **amount** needed; the **purpose** and **period** (a building needs long-term funds; stock needs short-term); the **cost** (interest, dividends); **security** demanded by the lender; and whether the owners are willing to **share control** (selling shares brings in new owners).

:::callout{type=local-example}
A woman who sells cooking gas in Bo may start with her own savings, add stock on **trade credit** from the depot, take a **microfinance** loan for a second set of cylinders, and later borrow from a **commercial bank** to buy a delivery tricycle on **hire purchase**. As her firm grows, so do the sources open to her.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not confuse **working capital** with **capital employed**. Working capital is only current assets minus current liabilities; capital employed adds the fixed assets as well.
:::

## Key points

- **Capital** is the money and assets used to run a business.
- Types: **fixed, working (circulating), liquid, owned, borrowed, employed**; companies also have **authorised, issued, called-up and paid-up** capital.
- **Internal sources**: savings, retained profit, sale of assets. **External sources**: overdrafts, loans, trade credit, hire purchase, leasing, shares, debentures, microfinance, grants.
- Sources are **short, medium or long term**; the choice depends on amount, purpose, cost, security and control.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Common questions: **define and distinguish types of capital** (especially fixed and working capital, or authorised, issued, called-up and paid-up capital), and **explain the sources of capital** for a sole trader or a company. Calculations on share capital often appear; set out each figure clearly with its name.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is working capital?
2. Distinguish called-up capital from paid-up capital.
3. Give two internal and two external sources of capital for a sole trader.
4. Why is an overdraft unsuitable for buying a building?

**Answers:** 1. Current assets minus current liabilities. 2. Called-up capital is the amount shareholders have been asked to pay; paid-up capital is the amount they have actually paid. 3. Internal: personal savings, retained profit (or sale of assets). External: any two of bank loan, overdraft, trade credit, hire purchase, family loans, microfinance. 4. An overdraft is short-term and repayable on demand, while a building is a long-term asset that needs long-term finance.
