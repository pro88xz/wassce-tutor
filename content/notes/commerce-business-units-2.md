When a business needs millions of leones, more than a few partners can provide, it becomes a company and sells shares. Farmers who want better prices form cooperatives. Some essential services are run by the government itself. This lesson covers companies, cooperatives, public enterprises and how firms combine.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe private and public limited companies and how a company is formed
- explain the advantages and disadvantages of joint-stock companies
- describe the types, features and problems of cooperative societies
- describe public enterprises and their merits and demerits
- explain business combinations: mergers, takeovers and holding companies

## Joint-stock companies

:::callout{type=definition}
A **joint-stock (limited liability) company** is a business owned by **shareholders**, who contribute capital by buying **shares**. It is a **separate legal entity** from its owners, and the shareholders have **limited liability**.
:::

| | Private limited company (Ltd) | Public limited company (Plc) |
|---|---|---|
| **Members** | Minimum of 1 or 2 (depending on the law), with a maximum set by law | Minimum set by law; no maximum |
| **Shares** | Cannot be offered to the public; transfer is restricted | Offered to the public; freely transferable, often on the stock exchange |
| **Capital** | Moderate | Very large |
| **Publication of accounts** | Limited | Must publish audited accounts |
| **Example** | A family transport or construction firm | A listed bank or brewery |

### Forming a company

1. **Promoters** prepare the documents.
2. The **Memorandum of Association** (the company's external constitution): its name, registered office, objects, limited liability clause and share capital.
3. The **Articles of Association** (the internal rules): rights of shareholders, appointment of directors, meetings, dividends.
4. The documents are filed with the registrar (in Sierra Leone, the **Corporate Affairs Commission**), and a **Certificate of Incorporation** is issued: the company now exists in law.
5. A public company issues a **prospectus** inviting the public to buy shares, and may need a **certificate to commence business (trading certificate)**.

| Advantages of companies | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Limited liability** encourages investors | Costly and complex to form |
| **Large capital** from many shareholders | Must publish accounts (Plc), so less secrecy |
| **Continuity**: the company continues when shareholders die or sell | **Slow decisions**; separation of ownership and control |
| Can employ specialist managers | Shareholders have little say in running it |
| Economies of scale | Double taxation of profits (company tax, then tax on dividends) |
| Shares are easy to transfer (Plc) | Risk of large, impersonal organisations |

## Cooperative societies

:::callout{type=definition}
A **cooperative society** is a voluntary association of people who join together to **serve their mutual interests**, contributing capital and sharing benefits, with **democratic control** (one member, one vote).
:::

**Types:**

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer (retail) cooperative** | Members buy goods in bulk and sell to members at fair prices | A staff cooperative shop |
| **Producer (marketing) cooperative** | Producers pool and market their output | Cocoa or rice farmers' cooperative |
| **Credit and thrift (savings) cooperative** | Members save together and borrow from the pool at low interest | Teachers' or traders' savings societies (*osusu*-style groups formalised) |
| **Multipurpose cooperative** | Combines several of these functions | A village cooperative that markets crops and supplies fertiliser |

**Features:** voluntary membership; one member, one vote; surplus shared according to **purchases or patronage**, not capital; limited interest on capital; service, not profit, as the main aim.

**Problems:** shortage of capital; poor management and record keeping; dishonesty and mismanagement of funds; disloyal members who sell outside the cooperative; competition from private traders; lack of government support.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many Sierra Leonean traders belong to rotating savings groups (*osusu*), in which members contribute a fixed sum regularly and take turns to receive the whole pot. A registered **credit and thrift cooperative** formalises this idea, keeps records and lends to members.
:::

## Public enterprises

:::callout{type=definition}
A **public enterprise (public corporation)** is a business **owned, financed and controlled by the government**, set up by law to provide essential services, and run by a board appointed by the government.
:::

*Examples in Sierra Leone include the Guma Valley Water Company, the Electricity Distribution and Supply Authority (EDSA), the Sierra Leone Ports and Harbours Authority and the Sierra Leone Road Transport Corporation.*

**Reasons for public enterprises:** to provide essential services (water, power, ports); to provide services private firms find unprofitable; to prevent private monopolies exploiting consumers; to control strategic industries; to create jobs.

**Problems:** inefficiency and waste; political interference; losses covered by taxpayers; poor service; corruption. These problems led many governments to **privatise** or **commercialise** public enterprises (see *Government Policies and Business*).

## Business combinations

Firms join together to grow, cut costs or reduce competition.

| Combination | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Merger (amalgamation)** | Two or more firms agree to join and form one new firm |
| **Takeover (acquisition)** | One firm buys a controlling interest in another |
| **Horizontal integration** | Firms at the **same stage** of production join (two breweries) |
| **Vertical integration** | Firms at **different stages** join: backward (towards raw materials) or forward (towards consumers) |
| **Lateral (conglomerate) integration** | Firms in **unrelated** industries join |
| **Holding company** | A company that owns enough shares to control other companies (subsidiaries) |
| **Cartel** | Independent firms agree to fix prices or share markets |

![A rice mill and the stages of the rice trade: joining rice farms is backward vertical integration, joining a wholesaler or retail shops is forward vertical integration, joining another rice mill is horizontal integration, and joining a soap factory is lateral integration](https://wasscetutor.com/img/commerce/business-combinations.svg)

*Figure 1. Horizontal, vertical and lateral integration, seen from a rice mill. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Reasons:** economies of scale; reduced competition; secure supplies or outlets; diversification. **Dangers:** monopoly power, higher prices, job losses.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **public limited company** (Plc) is **privately owned** by shareholders; "public" means its shares are offered to the public. A **public corporation** is **owned by the government**.
:::

## Key points

- **Companies** are owned by shareholders with **limited liability**; they are separate legal entities with continuity.
- Formation needs a **Memorandum and Articles of Association** and a **Certificate of Incorporation**.
- **Cooperatives** serve members, with **one member, one vote**, and share surplus by patronage.
- **Public enterprises** are government-owned and service-oriented.
- Firms combine through **mergers, takeovers, horizontal, vertical and lateral integration**.

## Quick check

1. What is the difference between the Memorandum and the Articles of Association?
2. Give two features of a cooperative society.
3. What is vertical integration?
4. Why might a government run an electricity supply company?

**Answers:** 1. The Memorandum sets out the company's external constitution (name, objects, capital, liability); the Articles set out its internal rules (directors, meetings, rights of shareholders). 2. Any two: voluntary membership, one member one vote, surplus shared by patronage, limited interest on capital, service motive. 3. A combination of firms at different stages of production of the same good. 4. Electricity is an essential service, may be unprofitable in some areas, and a private monopoly might exploit consumers.
