A farmer in Sierra Leone can lose a large part of a harvest before it is ever eaten: caterpillars stripping maize leaves, birds eating rice in the field, mosaic disease stunting cassava, weevils and rats in the store. **Pests** and **diseases** of crops reduce yields, lower quality and cost farmers money, and they threaten the food supply of the whole country. This lesson classifies crop pests and diseases, describes important examples on local crops, explains the damage they cause, and sets out the methods of control, ending with **integrated pest management**, which combines them safely.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define pest, disease and pathogen in agriculture
- classify insect pests by their mouthparts and the damage they cause
- describe important pests and diseases of local crops and stored produce
- describe cultural, biological, chemical, mechanical and legislative control
- explain integrated pest management and the safe use of pesticides

## Definitions

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **pest** is any organism that damages crops, stored produce or livestock and causes economic loss: insects, nematodes, birds, rodents, and weeds.
- A **crop disease** is a condition in which the normal growth or functioning of a plant is upset, usually by a **pathogen** (a fungus, bacterium or virus).
- A **vector** carries a pathogen from plant to plant (e.g. whiteflies carry cassava mosaic virus).
:::

## Insect pests

Insects are grouped by how they feed:

| Type | How they feed | Damage | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **biting and chewing** | jaws bite off and chew plant tissue | holes in leaves, stems and fruits; whole leaves eaten | grasshoppers, locusts, caterpillars, **fall armyworm**, beetles, termites |
| **piercing and sucking** | mouthparts pierce tissue and suck sap | wilting, curling, yellowing; **spread viruses** | aphids, **whiteflies**, mealybugs, cotton stainers, leafhoppers |
| **boring** | larvae tunnel inside stems, pods or grains | weakened stems, dead hearts, damaged grain | **stem borers** of rice and maize, grain weevils |

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Fall armyworm.** This caterpillar, native to the Americas, reached West Africa in 2016 and spread quickly, including into Sierra Leone. It feeds deep inside the whorl of maize plants, leaving ragged holes and sawdust-like droppings. Farmers are advised to scout fields weekly, hand-pick egg masses and young caterpillars, encourage natural enemies, and use approved pesticides only when needed.
:::

## Other pests

- **Nematodes** (eelworms): tiny worms in soil that attack roots, causing galls and stunting.
- **Birds**: weaver birds and others eat rice and millet grain in the field.
- **Rodents**: rats and cane rats (grasscutters) damage cassava, rice and maize in fields and stores.
- **Weeds** compete with crops for light, water and nutrients, and shelter pests.

## Pests of stored produce

| Pest | Attacks | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| **weevils** (maize and rice weevils) | stored grain | small holes in grains, powder |
| **grain moths** | stored grain | webbing, damaged grain |
| **bean bruchids** | cowpeas and beans | round holes in seeds |
| **rats and mice** | all stored food | eaten grain, droppings, contamination with urine (Lassa risk) |

## Crop diseases

| Disease | Crop | Cause | Signs | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **cassava mosaic** | cassava | **virus** | yellow-green mottled, twisted leaves; stunted plants; small roots | **whiteflies**; infected stem cuttings |
| **cassava bacterial blight** | cassava | bacterium | angular leaf spots, wilting, dieback | infected cuttings, rain splash |
| **rice blast** | rice | **fungus** | diamond-shaped spots on leaves; neck rot, empty grains | wind-blown spores |
| **rice yellow mottle** | rice | virus | yellowing, stunting, poor grain filling | insects; contact |
| **maize streak** | maize | virus | narrow yellow streaks along leaves | **leafhoppers** |
| **cocoa black pod** | cocoa | fungus (*Phytophthora*) | brown-black rotting pods | rain splash, wet conditions |
| **damping off** | seedlings | fungi | seedlings collapse at soil level | wet, crowded nurseries |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Spraying insecticide to cure a viral disease.** Insecticide cannot cure a plant infected with a virus. It may reduce the **vector** (whiteflies, leafhoppers), but the best control is **resistant varieties** and **clean planting material**, and removing infected plants.
:::

## Effects of pests and diseases

- **lower yields** and poorer quality produce
- **lower income** for farmers and higher food prices
- **food shortage** and malnutrition
- extra cost of control
- some contaminate food (rat urine; aflatoxin from moulds on damaged stored groundnuts and maize)

## Methods of control

![Integrated pest management in the centre, combining cultural, biological, chemical, mechanical and legislative control](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/pest-control-methods.svg)

### Cultural control (farm practices)

- **crop rotation**, so that pests and pathogens of one crop die out
- **resistant varieties** (e.g. mosaic-resistant cassava)
- **clean, healthy planting material** (disease-free cassava cuttings)
- **early or timely planting** to avoid the worst pest season
- **weeding**, correct spacing and **field sanitation** (removing and burning infected plants and crop remains)
- proper **drying** of grain before storage

### Biological control

Using a pest's **natural enemies**: predators, parasites or pathogens.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Explain the biological control of the cassava mealybug in Africa.**

The cassava mealybug, accidentally brought to Africa from South America, sucked sap from cassava and destroyed crops across the cassava belt in the 1970s and 1980s. Scientists found a tiny **parasitic wasp** from the mealybug's home area. The wasp lays its eggs inside mealybugs, and the larvae kill them. Released across Africa, the wasps brought the mealybug under control **without chemicals**, at low cost, and the control continues by itself because the wasps keep breeding.
:::

**Advantages:** no poisonous residues, cheap once established, self-sustaining. **Disadvantages:** slow; the agent might attack useful species if not tested carefully.

### Chemical control

Pesticides: **insecticides** (insects), **fungicides** (fungi), **herbicides** (weeds), **rodenticides** (rats), **nematicides**.

**Safe use:**

- read and follow the **label**; use the correct dose
- wear **protective clothing**: gloves, mask, boots, long sleeves
- do not spray in wind or before rain; keep away from water sources
- observe the **waiting period** before harvest
- store chemicals locked away from children and food; never reuse containers for food or water
- wash after spraying

**Disadvantages:** can poison people, livestock and wildlife; kill pollinators and natural enemies; pollute water; leave residues in food; pests become **resistant**.

### Mechanical and physical control

Hand-picking caterpillars and egg masses; traps for rats; scaring birds (scarecrows, children with slings, tins on strings); fencing; **drying grain well**; storage in **airtight (hermetic) bags or containers**, which kill storage insects by cutting off oxygen.

### Legislative control

**Quarantine laws** prevent infected plants, seeds and soil from being brought into the country or moved between areas.

## Integrated pest management (IPM)

**IPM** combines several methods to keep pests below the level that causes economic loss, using chemicals only as a **last resort**. It is cheaper, safer for people and the environment, and slows the development of resistance.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Distinguish between a pest and a pathogen."}
A **pest** is an organism (e.g. an insect, rat or bird) that damages crops and causes economic loss. A **pathogen** is a microorganism (fungus, bacterium or virus) that causes disease.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Give two signs of damage by a piercing and sucking insect."}
Any two: wilting, curling or yellowing leaves; stunted growth; spread of viral disease; sticky honeydew.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. State the cause, vector and one control method of cassava mosaic disease."}
Cause: a **virus**. Vector: **whiteflies** (also spread in infected cuttings). Control: plant **resistant varieties** and **disease-free cuttings**; uproot and destroy infected plants.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of chemical control."}
Advantages: fast; effective on large outbreaks. Disadvantages: poisons people and wildlife, kills natural enemies and pollinators, pollutes water, residues in food, pests become resistant (any two).
:::

:::reveal{title="5. How can a farmer protect stored maize from weevils without chemicals?"}
Dry the grain well, clean the store, sort out damaged grain, and store in airtight (hermetic) bags or containers.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Biting and chewing pests**: pests whose jaws bite off and chew plant tissue, such as grasshoppers and caterpillars.
- **Piercing and sucking pests**: pests whose mouthparts pierce plant tissue and suck sap, such as aphids.
- **Boring pests**: pests whose larvae tunnel inside stems, pods or grains, such as stem borers.
- **Storage pests**: pests of stored food, such as weevils, grain moths, bean bruchids and rats.
- **Nematodes** (eelworms): tiny worms in the soil that attack roots, causing galls and stunting.
- **Cassava mosaic**: a virus disease of cassava that causes mottled, twisted leaves and low yields.
- **Damping off**: a fungal disease that makes seedlings collapse at soil level.
:::

## Key points

- **Pests**: insects, nematodes, birds, rodents, weeds; **diseases**: caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses.
- Insects: **biting and chewing**, **piercing and sucking** (spread viruses), **boring**.
- Key local problems: fall armyworm, stem borers, cassava mosaic, rice blast, weevils, rats.
- Control: **cultural, biological, chemical, mechanical, legislative**.
- **IPM** combines them; pesticides are a last resort and must be used safely.
