# Support in plants and animals: the mammalian skeleton

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the main divisions of the human skeleton
- explain the functions of bones and cartilage
- distinguish the axial and appendicular skeletons
- describe the main types of joints
- explain how muscles, bones and joints work together

## Functions of the mammalian skeleton

The human skeleton is an endoskeleton made mainly of bone and cartilage. It performs several important functions:

- **Support:** provides a framework that maintains body shape and supports soft tissues.
- **Protection:** the skull protects the brain; the vertebral column protects the spinal cord; the rib cage protects the heart and lungs.
- **Movement:** bones act as levers, joints provide points of movement, and skeletal muscles pull on bones.
- **Blood-cell formation:** red bone marrow produces red blood cells, many white blood cells and platelets.
- **Mineral storage:** bones store calcium and phosphate, which can be released when needed.
- **Fat storage:** yellow marrow stores fat as an energy reserve.

## Main divisions of the human skeleton

The adult human skeleton is commonly described as having 206 bones, although the number can vary slightly because some bones may fuse or remain separate.

### Axial skeleton

The axial skeleton forms the central axis of the body. It includes:

- **Skull:** protects the brain and supports the structures of the face. The cranium encloses the brain; facial bones form the framework of the face.
- **Vertebral column:** made of vertebrae arranged into cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccygeal regions. It supports the head and trunk, protects the spinal cord and allows controlled flexibility.
- **Rib cage:** ribs and sternum protect organs in the chest. The ribs also move during breathing.

### Appendicular skeleton

The appendicular skeleton consists of the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton.

- **Pectoral girdle:** the clavicle and scapula connect the upper limbs to the trunk.
- **Upper limbs:** include the humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges.
- **Pelvic girdle:** the hip bones attach the lower limbs and help support body weight.
- **Lower limbs:** include the femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals and phalanges.

The femur is the longest bone in the human body. The patella protects the front of the knee joint and improves the leverage of the quadriceps muscle.

## Bone and cartilage

Bone is a living connective tissue. Its matrix contains collagen fibres, which provide some flexibility, and mineral salts, especially calcium phosphate, which provide hardness. Bone contains cells, blood vessels and nerves. It is continuously remodelled as old tissue is replaced.

Cartilage is a firm but more flexible connective tissue. It covers the ends of many bones at synovial joints, reducing friction and helping distribute loads. It is also found in structures such as the nose, external ear and rings of the trachea. Cartilage has no blood vessels within its matrix; nutrients diffuse through it from surrounding tissues.

## Joints

A joint is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints may be classified by the amount of movement they allow.

- **Immovable or fibrous joints:** allow little or no movement; examples include the sutures between skull bones.
- **Slightly movable joints:** permit limited movement; examples occur between adjacent vertebrae, where pads of cartilage help absorb shock.
- **Freely movable synovial joints:** allow a wide range of movement and are found at the shoulder, elbow, hip and knee.

A typical synovial joint has cartilage covering the ends of bones, a joint capsule, a synovial membrane and a cavity containing synovial fluid. The fluid lubricates the joint and reduces friction. Ligaments connect bone to bone and help stabilise the joint. Tendons connect muscle to bone.

## How movement occurs at a joint

Skeletal muscles usually work in antagonistic pairs because a muscle can pull but cannot push. At the elbow, the biceps and triceps act as an antagonistic pair. When the biceps contracts, it pulls the forearm upwards and the triceps relaxes. When the triceps contracts, it straightens the elbow while the biceps relaxes.

The muscle pulls on a tendon, which pulls on the bone. The bone moves around the joint, acting as a lever. The joint provides the pivot. Coordinated contraction and relaxation, together with the nervous system, produce controlled movement.

## WASSCE examination focus

For a labelled skeleton, learn the positions and names of major bones rather than memorising an isolated list. In joint questions, distinguish ligaments (bone to bone) from tendons (muscle to bone). In movement questions, identify the contracting muscle and the relaxing antagonist. Remember that cartilage reduces friction at joint surfaces, while synovial fluid lubricates the joint cavity.

## Quick check

1. State three functions of the skeleton.
2. Name the two main divisions of the human skeleton.
3. Which bone protects the brain?
4. Distinguish a ligament from a tendon.
5. Explain why the biceps and triceps are described as an antagonistic pair.

**Answers:** 1. Support, protection, movement, blood-cell formation or mineral storage (any three). 2. Axial and appendicular skeletons. 3. The skull, particularly the cranium. 4. A ligament joins bone to bone; a tendon joins muscle to bone. 5. They produce opposite movements at the elbow: one contracts while the other relaxes to bend or straighten the arm.
