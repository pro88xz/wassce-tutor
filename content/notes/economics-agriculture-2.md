A good harvest should make farmers richer, but in a bumper year the price of rice can collapse, and farmers may earn less than in a poor year. Farm prices and incomes swing more than almost any other. This lesson explains why, and what marketing systems and governments do about it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why the prices and incomes of farm products fluctuate
- describe measures to stabilise agricultural prices and incomes
- describe the channels and problems of agricultural marketing
- explain government policies to support agriculture
- explain the links between agriculture and industry

## Why farm prices are unstable

1. **Supply depends on the weather.** Good rains bring a bumper harvest; floods or drought cut output. Farmers cannot control supply as a factory can.
2. **Demand is inelastic.** People need food but do not eat much more when it is cheap; demand for staple foods changes little with price.
3. **Supply is inelastic in the short run.** Once a crop is planted, farmers cannot quickly grow more or less.
4. **Many small farmers** act independently, so supply responds late and all at once.
5. **Perishability.** Crops such as tomatoes and pepper must be sold quickly, whatever the price.
6. **World prices** of export crops (cocoa, coffee) change with world demand and supply.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why a good harvest can lower farmers' income.** Demand for rice is inelastic (PED about 0.4). A bumper harvest raises supply by 10%, and to sell it all the price must fall by about 25%.

Total revenue changes by roughly $(1.10 \times 0.75) - 1 = -0.175$: farmers' **total income falls by about 17.5%**, even though they sold more rice.
:::

**Effects of unstable prices:** farmers' incomes are uncertain, so they cannot plan or invest; some leave farming; governments earn less from export duties; and foreign-exchange earnings swing up and down.

## Stabilising prices and incomes

| Measure | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Buffer stock** | A board buys and stores the crop when prices are low and sells from store when prices are high, keeping prices within a band |
| **Marketing boards** | A state board buys export crops at fixed prices, sells abroad and, in theory, saves surpluses in good years to support prices in bad years |
| **Guaranteed minimum prices** | The government promises to buy at a minimum price, protecting farmers' income |
| **Storage and processing** | Drying, silos and processing let farmers sell over the whole year instead of all at harvest |
| **Crop diversification** | Growing several crops spreads the risk of one price collapsing |
| **Crop insurance** | Pays farmers compensation when weather destroys the harvest |
| **International commodity agreements** | Producing countries agree quotas or prices for crops such as cocoa or coffee |

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **Sierra Leone Produce Marketing Board (SLPMB)** once bought export crops such as cocoa, coffee and palm kernels from farmers at announced prices. Marketing boards across West Africa were criticised for paying farmers too little and for inefficiency, and many were reformed or closed as trade was liberalised.
:::

## Agricultural marketing

:::callout{type=definition}
**Agricultural marketing** covers all the activities between the farm and the final consumer: collecting, grading, storing, processing, transporting, financing and selling farm produce.
:::

**Channels:** farmer → local buyer (middleman) → wholesaler → retailer → consumer; or farmer → cooperative → processor or exporter; or farmer → consumer directly at the weekly market (lumo).

**Problems of agricultural marketing:**

1. **Exploitation by middlemen**, who buy cheaply at harvest when farmers need cash.
2. **Poor roads and transport**, which raise costs and cause losses.
3. **Lack of storage**, forcing quick sales at low prices.
4. **Lack of market information**, so farmers do not know prices elsewhere.
5. **No grading or standards**, so good produce is not rewarded.
6. **Lack of credit**, so farmers sell crops before harvest at low prices.

**Cooperatives** help by pooling members' produce, bargaining for better prices, buying inputs in bulk, providing storage and giving members credit.

## Government policies to support agriculture

1. **Credit**: agricultural development banks, community banks and microfinance.
2. **Extension services** to teach improved methods.
3. **Subsidies** on fertiliser, seed and machinery.
4. **Research** into better seeds and pest control (for example by SLARI).
5. **Irrigation and swamp development** for dry-season farming.
6. **Feeder roads and storage** facilities.
7. **Land reform** to make secure land available to farmers and investors.
8. **Tariffs or quotas** on cheap food imports to protect local producers (though these raise prices for consumers).

## Agriculture and industry

The two sectors depend on each other:

- Agriculture supplies **food** for industrial workers and **raw materials** for factories (palm oil mills, rice mills, breweries, soap makers).
- Industry supplies agriculture with **tools, fertiliser, machinery and processing**, and a **market** for farm produce.
- Agro-processing adds **value** to crops, creates rural jobs and reduces waste.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say a bumper harvest "always makes farmers rich". With inelastic demand, the price falls by a larger percentage than output rises, so total farm income can **fall**.
:::

## Key points

- Farm prices are unstable because **supply depends on the weather** and both **demand and short-run supply are inelastic**.
- A bumper harvest can **reduce** farmers' total income.
- Stabilisation: **buffer stocks, marketing boards, guaranteed prices, storage, diversification and insurance**.
- Marketing problems include **middlemen, poor roads and storage, and lack of information and credit**; cooperatives help.
- Agriculture and industry **support each other**.
