A young man in Kambia wants to grow groundnuts for the coming season but has no land. A neighbour has a field he will not use this year. They agree that the young man will farm it for one season and pay the owner a fixed amount after harvest. He is a **tenant farmer**, and the arrangement is a **tenancy**. Renting land in this way is one of the commonest ways for landless farmers to start farming.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define tenancy, tenant and landlord
- describe the main forms of tenancy: cash rent, rent in kind and sharecropping
- state the advantages and disadvantages of tenant farming
- explain how tenant farming can be improved

## What is tenancy?

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Tenancy**: a land tenure system in which a farmer is allowed to use another person's land for a period, usually a season or a year, in return for **rent**.
- **Tenant**: the farmer who uses the land and pays rent for it.
- **Landlord**: the owner of the land who allows the tenant to use it.
- **Rent**: the payment made for the use of land, in money or in produce.
:::

Tenancy is usually **short** and often agreed **by word of mouth**. That is what separates it from **leasehold**, which is a formal written agreement for many years.

## Forms of tenancy

| Form | How the rent is paid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash rent** | A fixed sum of money each season or year | NLe 500 for a plot for one rainy season |
| **Rent in kind** | A fixed quantity of produce | Two bags of rice after harvest, whatever the yield |
| **Sharecropping** | A **share** of the harvest (for example one-third) | One bag in every three goes to the landlord |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In **rent in kind** the amount of produce is **fixed** in advance (say, two bags), however good or bad the harvest. In **sharecropping** the landlord gets a **share** (a fraction) of the actual harvest, so the payment changes with the size of the crop.
:::

## Advantages of tenant farming

### To the tenant

1. A landless farmer gets land to farm **without buying** it.
2. Little money is needed at the start; rent is often paid **after harvest**.
3. The tenant can **move** to better land when the season ends.
4. Young farmers gain experience and savings to buy or lease land later.

### To the landlord and the community

1. Idle land earns an **income** in money or produce.
2. Land is kept clear and productive.
3. **Food production** and rural employment rise.

## Disadvantages of tenant farming

1. **Insecurity**: the tenant may be sent away after one season, so has no reason to improve the land.
2. **No long-term investment**: tenants avoid tree crops, fencing, irrigation and soil conservation.
3. **Soil exhaustion**: tenants may over-crop land they will soon leave.
4. **High or rising rents** reduce the tenant's income.
5. **Disputes**, especially where agreements are only oral.
6. A tenant cannot use rented land as **collateral** for a loan.

## Improving tenant farming

- Put agreements **in writing**, stating the plot, the rent, when it is paid and how long the tenancy lasts.
- Offer **longer tenancies** and the right to renew, so tenants can plan.
- **Compensate** tenants for lasting improvements, such as drains or fruit trees, when they leave.
- Set **fair rents**, through custom, farmer groups or local by-laws.
- Use chiefdom authorities or farmers' associations to **settle disputes** quickly.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Comparing the cost of tenancy.** A tenant grows rice and harvests **24 bags** in a good year and **12 bags** in a bad year.

- **Rent in kind** of 4 bags: he keeps 20 bags (good year) or 8 bags (bad year).
- **Sharecropping** at one-quarter: the landlord gets 6 bags (good year) or 3 bags (bad year), so he keeps 18 or 9.

In a bad year sharecropping is kinder to the tenant, because the landlord shares the loss; in a good year a fixed rent in kind leaves him more.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Across Sierra Leone, landless farmers, settlers and young people often farm land belonging to other families as tenants. In return they may pay a small sum, give part of the harvest, or simply present a gift to the landowner, such as rice or a goat, depending on local custom and the relationship between the two families.
:::

## Key points

- **Tenancy**: farming another person's land for a season or year in return for **rent**.
- Forms: **cash rent**, **rent in kind** (fixed produce) and **sharecropping** (a share of the harvest).
- Advantages: land for the landless, little start-up money, income for the landlord, more food production.
- Disadvantages: **insecurity**, no long-term investment, soil exhaustion, high rents, disputes, no collateral.
- Written, longer and fairer agreements, with compensation for improvements, make tenancy work better.

## WASSCE examination focus

WASSCE asks you to **explain tenancy or tenant farming**, to **state its advantages and disadvantages**, and to **distinguish it from leasehold and sharecropping**. A comparison table (length, how rent is paid, written or oral) is a quick way to show the differences.
:::
