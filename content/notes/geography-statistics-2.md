**Statistical maps** show how geographical data vary from place to place. The main types are **dot maps**, where each dot stands for a fixed number of people or things; **choropleth maps**, where areas are shaded according to their values; **isopleth (isoline) maps**, where lines join places of equal value, such as **isohyets** for rainfall and **isotherms** for temperature; and **flow maps**, where arrows of different widths show movement. Geographers also summarise data with simple **statistics**: the **mean**, **median**, **mode** and **range**, **percentages** and **population density**. This lesson explains how to draw and use each map and how to calculate the key statistics.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe dot, choropleth, isopleth, flow and proportional-symbol maps
- explain how to draw each type and its advantages and disadvantages
- name common isolines such as isohyets, isotherms and isobars
- calculate the mean, median, mode and range of geographical data
- calculate percentages, percentage change and population density

| Map | How it is drawn | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dot map** | each dot represents a fixed value (e.g. 1 dot = 1,000 people), placed where the things actually are | shows **distribution** and clustering clearly | counting dots is slow; choosing the dot value is hard |
| **Choropleth (shading) map** | each area is shaded by **class**: darker shades for higher values | easy to draw from area data; shows contrasts between areas | suggests sudden changes at boundaries; hides variation inside areas |
| **Isopleth (isoline) map** | lines join places of **equal value**, drawn between data points | shows gradual change; good for continuous data like rainfall | needs many data points; lines are estimated |
| **Flow map** | arrows or bands drawn along routes, with **width proportional** to the amount moved | shows direction and volume of movement | can become crowded |
| **Proportional symbols** | circles, squares or bars drawn at places, sized by value | shows totals at each place | symbols may overlap |

### Drawing a choropleth map

1. Find the **range** of the data and divide it into **4–6 classes** (for example, 0–49, 50–99, 100–149, 150 and over people per km²).
2. Choose **shades of one colour**, from light (low) to dark (high).
3. Shade each area according to its class.
4. Add a **key**, title, north arrow and scale.

## Statistical calculations

### Mean, median, mode and range

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Monthly rainfall (mm) at a station for the first seven months of a year was: 10, 5, 30, 70, 180, 340, 760. Find the mean, median and range.**

**Mean** $= \dfrac{10 + 5 + 30 + 70 + 180 + 340 + 760}{7} = \dfrac{1\,395}{7} \approx 199$ mm.

**Median:** arrange in order: 5, 10, 30, **70**, 180, 340, 760. The middle (4th) value is **70 mm**.

**Range** $= 760 - 5 = 755$ mm.

The mean is much higher than the median because the very wet months pull it up; the median gives a better idea of a "typical" month here.
:::

- **Mean:** the sum of the values divided by the number of values.
- **Median:** the middle value when the data are arranged in order (for an even number of values, the average of the two middle ones).
- **Mode:** the value that occurs most often.
- **Range:** the highest value minus the lowest.

### Percentages and percentage change

$$\text{Percentage} = \frac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 100\%$$

$$\text{Percentage change} = \frac{\text{new value} - \text{old value}}{\text{old value}} \times 100\%$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A town's population rose from 80,000 to 100,000. Find the percentage increase.**

$\dfrac{100\,000 - 80\,000}{80\,000} \times 100\% = \dfrac{20\,000}{80\,000} \times 100\% = 25\%$.
:::

### Population density

$$\text{Population density} = \frac{\text{total population}}{\text{land area (km}^2)}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A district has 600,000 people and an area of 4,000 km². Find its population density.**

$\dfrac{600\,000}{4\,000} = 150$ people per km².
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
A class could draw a **choropleth map of Sierra Leone's districts** shaded by population density from census figures, showing the very high density of the **Western Area** around Freetown and much lower densities in large rural districts such as **Koinadugu**. A **flow map** could then show the movement of people or goods along the main highways into Freetown.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not use **different colours** for the classes of a choropleth map. Use **shades of one colour**, getting darker as the values rise, so the reader can see "more" and "less" at a glance.
:::

## Key points

- **Dot maps** show distribution; **choropleth maps** shade areas by class; **isopleth maps** join equal values; **flow maps** show movement with proportional widths.
- Isolines: **isohyet** (rain), **isotherm** (temperature), **isobar** (pressure), **contour** (height).
- **Mean** = sum ÷ number; **median** = middle value; **mode** = most common; **range** = highest − lowest.
- **Percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100%; **density** = population ÷ area.

## Quick check

1. What does each dot on a dot map represent?
2. What is an isohyet?
3. Give one disadvantage of a choropleth map.
4. Find the median of 12, 7, 20, 15, 9.
5. A district of 2,500 km² has 350,000 people. Find its population density.

**Answers:**

1. A fixed number of people or things, such as 1,000 people.
2. A line on a map joining places with equal rainfall.
3. Any one: it suggests sudden changes at boundaries; it hides variation within each area.
4. In order: 7, 9, **12**, 15, 20, so the median is 12.
5. $350\,000 \div 2\,500 = 140$ people per km².
