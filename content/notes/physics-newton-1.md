Why do passengers in a poda-poda lurch forward when the driver brakes hard? Why does a gun kick back? Why is a loaded lorry harder to stop than an empty one? Newton's three laws answer all of these, and almost every mechanics question rests on them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state Newton's three laws of motion and explain inertia
- define momentum and relate force to the rate of change of momentum
- derive and use $F = ma$
- identify action–reaction pairs correctly

## Newton's first law and inertia

:::callout{type=definition}
**Newton's first law:** a body continues in its state of **rest** or of **uniform motion in a straight line** unless it is acted upon by an **external resultant force**.
:::

:::callout{type=definition}
**Inertia** is the tendency of a body to remain at rest or, if moving, to continue moving in a straight line with uniform velocity. The **mass** of a body is a measure of its inertia.
:::

Everyday examples of inertia:

- Passengers jerk **forward** when a vehicle stops suddenly: their bodies keep moving.
- Passengers jerk **backward** when it starts suddenly: their bodies tend to stay at rest.
- Shaking a wet cloth: the cloth stops, the water keeps going.
- Pulling a tablecloth quickly from under plates: the plates stay where they are.
- **Seat belts** stop the passenger's forward motion gradually instead of the windscreen stopping it suddenly.

## Momentum

:::callout{type=definition}
The **linear momentum** of a body is the product of its **mass** and its **velocity**:

$$p = mv$$

It is a **vector** (same direction as the velocity), measured in **kg m s⁻¹** (or N s).
:::

A loaded lorry at 10 m s⁻¹ ($m = 20\,000$ kg) has momentum $200\,000$ kg m s⁻¹; a motorcycle at the same speed ($m = 150$ kg) has only $1500$ kg m s⁻¹. That is why the lorry is far harder to stop.

## Newton's second law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Newton's second law:** the **rate of change of momentum** of a body is **directly proportional** to the **resultant force** acting on it and takes place in the **direction of the force**.
:::

For a constant mass $m$ whose velocity changes from $u$ to $v$ in time $t$:

$$F \propto \dfrac{mv - mu}{t} = m\left(\dfrac{v - u}{t}\right) = ma$$

So $F = kma$. The newton is **defined** so that $k = 1$:

$$\boxed{F = ma}$$

:::callout{type=definition}
One **newton** is the force that gives a mass of **1 kg** an acceleration of **1 m s⁻²**.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A car of mass 1200 kg accelerates from 10 m s⁻¹ to 25 m s⁻¹ in 6 s. Find the resultant force on it.**

$$a = \dfrac{25 - 10}{6} = 2.5\ \text{m s}^{-2} \qquad F = ma = 1200 \times 2.5 = 3000\ \text{N}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A trolley of mass 4 kg is pulled along a rough floor by a horizontal force of 20 N. Friction is 8 N. Find its acceleration.**

The **resultant** force is what accelerates it: $20 - 8 = 12$ N.

$$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{12}{4} = 3\ \text{m s}^{-2}$$
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In $F = ma$, $F$ is the **resultant (net) force**, not just the applied force. Subtract friction and other opposing forces first.
:::

### Weight

Weight is the gravitational force on a mass: applying $F = ma$ with $a = g$,

$$W = mg$$

A 60 kg student weighs $60 \times 10 = 600$ N on Earth. On the Moon ($g \approx 1.6$ m s⁻²) the mass is still 60 kg but the weight is only about 96 N.

## Newton's third law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Newton's third law:** to every action there is an **equal and opposite reaction**. If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts an equal force on A in the opposite direction.
:::

The two forces of an action–reaction pair:

- are **equal in size** and **opposite in direction**
- act on **different bodies**
- are the **same type** of force (both contact forces, or both gravitational)

| Action | Reaction |
|---|---|
| A swimmer pushes water backwards | water pushes the swimmer forwards |
| A rocket pushes exhaust gases out backwards | gases push the rocket forwards |
| A gun pushes a bullet forwards | the bullet pushes the gun backwards (recoil) |
| Your foot pushes the ground backwards | the ground pushes you forwards (walking) |
| The Earth pulls a mango down | the mango pulls the Earth up |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A book resting on a table: its **weight** (Earth pulls book) and the table's **normal reaction** (table pushes book) are equal and opposite, but they are **not** an action–reaction pair, because both act on the **same body**. The reaction to the book's weight is the book pulling the Earth upwards.
:::

## Key points

- First law: no resultant force → no change in velocity. Mass measures **inertia**.
- Momentum $p = mv$ (vector, kg m s⁻¹).
- Second law: force ∝ rate of change of momentum, giving $F = ma$; 1 N = 1 kg × 1 m s⁻².
- Third law: action and reaction are equal, opposite, and act on **different** bodies.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **state** all three laws precisely (word-perfect statements earn full marks), to **explain** an everyday observation using inertia, to **show how the second law leads to $F = ma$**, and to calculate with $F = ma$ using the **resultant** force. Objective items often ask which pair of forces is an action–reaction pair.
:::

## Quick check

1. Which property of a body measures its inertia?
2. A 50 kg box is pushed with a 300 N force against 100 N of friction. Find its acceleration.
3. Why does a gun recoil when fired?

**Answers:** 1. Its mass. 2. $(300 - 100) \div 50 = 4$ m s⁻². 3. By Newton's third law, the gun pushes the bullet forward, so the bullet pushes the gun backward with an equal force.
