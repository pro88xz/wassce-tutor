# Sense organs: the ear, balance and other senses

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the main parts of the human ear
- explain how sound is converted into nerve impulses
- describe how the ear helps maintain balance
- identify the receptors responsible for smell, taste and touch
- explain common causes of sensory impairment and basic prevention

## The ear: hearing and balance

The ear has three main regions: the outer ear, middle ear and inner ear. It detects sound and helps maintain balance and posture.

### Outer ear

The **pinna** collects sound waves and directs them into the external auditory canal. Sound waves travel along the canal and cause the tympanic membrane (eardrum) to vibrate.

### Middle ear

The middle ear is an air-filled cavity containing three small bones called the ossicles: the malleus, incus and stapes. The malleus is attached to the eardrum; the stapes transmits vibrations to the oval window of the inner ear. The ossicles amplify and transmit vibrations. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat and helps equalise air pressure on both sides of the eardrum.

### Inner ear and hearing

The cochlea is a fluid-filled, coiled structure. Vibrations transmitted through the oval window create waves in cochlear fluid. These waves move structures within the cochlea and stimulate sensory hair cells in the organ of Corti. The hair cells convert mechanical movement into electrical signals. Impulses travel along the auditory branch of the vestibulocochlear nerve to the brain, where sound is interpreted.

**Pathway of sound:** pinna → auditory canal → eardrum → ossicles → oval window → cochlear fluid and hair cells → auditory nerve → brain.

The round window allows pressure changes in cochlear fluid to be released. Different regions of the cochlea respond best to different sound frequencies.

## Balance and equilibrium

The vestibular apparatus in the inner ear helps detect head position and movement.

- **Semicircular canals** are arranged in three planes and detect rotational or angular movements of the head. Movement of fluid within the canals bends sensory hair cells.
- **Utricle and saccule** contain sensory structures that detect linear acceleration and the position of the head relative to gravity. Tiny calcium carbonate particles (otoliths) move and bend hair cells as the head changes position.

Information from the vestibular system is combined with signals from the eyes, muscles and joints. The brain uses it to coordinate posture, balance and eye movements.

## Other human senses

### Smell (olfaction)

Olfactory receptors are located in the upper region of the nasal cavity. Airborne chemical molecules dissolve in mucus and stimulate receptor cells. Impulses travel to the olfactory bulb and then to brain regions involved in identifying smells. Smell contributes to taste perception and can warn of hazards such as smoke or spoiled food.

### Taste (gustation)

Taste receptor cells are found in taste buds, many of which are located on the tongue. Chemicals in food dissolve in saliva and stimulate these cells. The main taste qualities commonly recognised are sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Taste combines with smell, texture and temperature to create the overall experience of flavour.

### Touch, temperature and pain

The skin contains different sensory receptors. Mechanoreceptors detect touch, pressure and vibration; thermoreceptors detect changes in temperature; and nociceptors respond to potentially damaging stimuli and contribute to the sensation of pain. Receptor distribution varies across the body, so some regions are more sensitive to fine touch than others.

## Sensory impairment and care

Hearing loss may result from damage to hair cells, prolonged exposure to loud noise, infection, injury or age-related changes. Avoiding excessive noise and seeking medical attention for persistent ear pain or discharge can help protect hearing. Never insert sharp objects into the ear canal.

Vision may be affected by refractive errors, cataract, infection, injury or diseases affecting the retina and optic nerve. Good lighting for close work, protective eyewear where necessary and professional eye examinations support eye health. Do not look directly at the sun or intense light sources.

Loss of smell or taste can occur with infections, injury or other medical conditions. Persistent or sudden sensory changes should be assessed by a qualified health professional.

## WASSCE examination focus

For hearing, learn the sequence from the pinna to the auditory nerve. The ossicles transmit vibrations; the cochlear hair cells convert mechanical stimulation into nerve signals. For balance, distinguish the semicircular canals (rotational movement) from the utricle and saccule (linear acceleration and head position). Do not state that the pinna itself converts sound into nerve impulses.
