Gases are measured in volumes and solutions in concentrations, not in grams. This lesson extends the mole to both: the **molar volume** of gases and the **molar concentration** of solutions. With these, you can prepare a standard solution, dilute an acid accurately and work out volumes of gas from masses, skills every titration and practical question depends on.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the molar volume of a gas at s.t.p. and at room conditions
- convert between gas volume, moles and mass
- define concentration in mol/dm³ and g/dm³ and convert between them
- calculate the moles of solute in a given volume of solution
- describe how to prepare a standard solution
- calculate concentrations after dilution

## Molar volume of gases

From Avogadro's law, one mole of **any** gas occupies the same volume at a given temperature and pressure.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Molar volume:**

- **22.4 dm³** at s.t.p. (0 °C, 1 atm)
- about **24 dm³** at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p., about 25 °C, 1 atm)
:::

$$n = \dfrac{V}{V_m} \qquad \text{moles} = \dfrac{\text{volume of gas (dm}^3)}{22.4 \text{ dm}^3\text{ at s.t.p.}}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Gas volumes and moles (at s.t.p.).**

1. How many moles are in 5.6 dm³ of carbon(IV) oxide? $n = 5.6 \div 22.4 = 0.25$ mol.
2. What is the mass of that gas? $m = 0.25 \times 44 = 11$ g.
3. What volume does 3.2 g of oxygen gas occupy? $n = 3.2 \div 32 = 0.1$ mol; $V = 0.1 \times 22.4 = 2.24$ dm³.
4. 1.12 dm³ of a gas weighs 2.2 g at s.t.p. Find its molar mass. $n = 1.12 \div 22.4 = 0.05$ mol; $M = 2.2 \div 0.05 = 44$ g/mol (it could be CO₂).
:::

Remember to convert **cm³ to dm³** (÷ 1000) first: 224 cm³ = 0.224 dm³ = 0.01 mol at s.t.p.

## Concentration of solutions

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Molar concentration** (molarity): the number of **moles of solute in 1 dm³** of solution, in **mol/dm³** (mol dm⁻³).
- **Mass concentration**: the **mass of solute in 1 dm³** of solution, in **g/dm³**.

$$C = \dfrac{n}{V} \qquad \text{mass concentration} = \text{molar concentration} \times M$$
:::

Here V is the volume of **solution** in **dm³**. The **moles of solute** in a volume of solution are

$$n = C \times V \qquad \text{or} \qquad n = \dfrac{C \times V(\text{cm}^3)}{1000}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**4.0 g of sodium hydroxide (M = 40) is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. Find the concentration.**

- Moles: $n = 4.0 \div 40 = 0.10$ mol.
- Volume: 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³.
- Molar concentration: $C = 0.10 \div 0.250 = 0.40$ mol/dm³.
- Mass concentration: $0.40 \times 40 = 16$ g/dm³ (check: 4.0 g in 0.25 dm³ is 16 g per dm³).
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How many moles of HCl are in 25.0 cm³ of 0.10 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid?**

$$n = \dfrac{0.10 \times 25.0}{1000} = 0.0025 \text{ mol} = 2.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ mol}$$
:::

## Preparing a standard solution

A **standard solution** is a solution of **accurately known concentration**. It is prepared from a pure, stable solid (a **primary standard**) such as anhydrous sodium carbonate.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Prepare 250 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ sodium carbonate solution (Na₂CO₃, M = 106).**

1. Moles needed: $0.100 \times 0.250 = 0.0250$ mol.
2. Mass needed: $0.0250 \times 106 = 2.65$ g.
3. **Weigh** 2.65 g accurately on a weighing bottle.
4. **Dissolve** it in a little distilled water in a beaker.
5. **Transfer** the solution to a **250 cm³ volumetric flask** using a funnel; **rinse** the beaker and funnel into the flask.
6. **Add distilled water** up to the mark, with the bottom of the meniscus on the line.
7. **Stopper and shake** (invert several times) to mix.
:::

Sodium hydroxide is **not** a primary standard: it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so its mass cannot be weighed accurately.

## Dilution

Adding water does not change the number of moles of solute, only the volume:

$$C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**25.0 cm³ of 2.0 mol/dm³ sulphuric acid is diluted to 500 cm³. What is the new concentration?**

$$C_2 = \dfrac{C_1V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 25.0}{500} = 0.10 \text{ mol/dm}^3$$

**What volume of 2.0 mol/dm³ acid is needed to make 250 cm³ of 0.40 mol/dm³ acid?**

$$V_1 = \dfrac{C_2V_2}{C_1} = \dfrac{0.40 \times 250}{2.0} = 50 \text{ cm}^3$$
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Oral rehydration solution (ORS), used in clinics across Sierra Leone to treat diarrhoea, must be made at the **right concentration**. Too little salt and sugar and it does not work; too much and it can harm a child. The sachet is designed to be dissolved in exactly **1 litre (1 dm³)** of clean water, which is a real-life standard solution.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Concentration in mol/dm³ needs the volume in **dm³**. Divide cm³ by 1000 first, or the answer will be 1000 times too small.
:::

## Key points

- Molar volume: **22.4 dm³ at s.t.p.**, about **24 dm³ at r.t.p.**; $n = V \div 22.4$.
- **Concentration** $C = n \div V$ (V in dm³); g/dm³ = mol/dm³ × M.
- Moles in a solution: $n = C \times V$, or $C \times V(\text{cm}^3) \div 1000$.
- A **standard solution** is made by weighing a primary standard, dissolving and making up to the mark in a **volumetric flask**.
- **Dilution:** $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$.

## Quick check

1. What volume does 0.5 mol of nitrogen occupy at s.t.p.?
2. 2.65 g of Na₂CO₃ is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Find its concentration in mol/dm³.
3. How many moles of NaOH are in 20.0 cm³ of 0.50 mol/dm³ solution?
4. 50 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ HCl is diluted to 250 cm³. Find the new concentration.

**Answers:**

1. $0.5 \times 22.4 = 11.2$ dm³.
2. $n = 2.65 \div 106 = 0.025$ mol; $C = 0.025 \div 0.500 = 0.050$ mol/dm³.
3. $0.50 \times 20.0 \div 1000 = 0.010$ mol.
4. $1.0 \times 50 \div 250 = 0.20$ mol/dm³.
