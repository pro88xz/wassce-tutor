The radio playing in a taxi, the light from the Sun, the ripples when a stone drops into a pond and the voice of your teacher all arrive as waves. A wave carries energy from one place to another without carrying the material along with it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a wave and distinguish mechanical from electromagnetic waves
- distinguish transverse from longitudinal waves with examples
- define amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period and wave speed
- use $v = f\lambda$ and $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$

## What is a wave?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **wave** is a disturbance that travels through a medium (or through space), **transferring energy** from one point to another **without** transferring the medium itself.
:::

When a wave passes along a rope, each part of the rope only moves up and down about its rest position; the rope does not travel to the other end. When a ripple crosses a pond, a floating leaf bobs up and down but stays in roughly the same place.

### Mechanical and electromagnetic waves

| Mechanical waves | Electromagnetic waves |
|---|---|
| need a **material medium** (solid, liquid or gas) | can travel through a **vacuum** |
| e.g. sound, water waves, waves on a rope or spring, seismic waves | e.g. light, radio waves, X-rays, microwaves |
| speed depends on the medium | all travel at $3 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum |

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

![A transverse wave drawn as a sine curve, with a crest, a trough, the amplitude and one wavelength marked](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/transverse-wave.svg)

*Figure 1. A transverse wave. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
- In a **transverse wave**, the particles of the medium vibrate **perpendicular** (at right angles) to the direction in which the wave travels. Examples: waves on a rope, water ripples, light and all electromagnetic waves.
- In a **longitudinal wave**, the particles vibrate **parallel** to (along) the direction of travel. Examples: sound waves, waves along a slinky spring pushed and pulled at one end.
:::

![A longitudinal wave shown as vertical lines bunched together at compressions and spread apart at rarefactions, with one wavelength marked between two compressions](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/longitudinal-wave.svg)

*Figure 2. A longitudinal wave: compressions (C) and rarefactions (R). WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

A transverse wave has **crests** and **troughs**; a longitudinal wave has **compressions** (particles crowded together, higher pressure) and **rarefactions** (particles spread out, lower pressure).

## Terms used to describe waves

| Term | Symbol | Definition | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amplitude** | $a$ | the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest (equilibrium) position | m |
| **Wavelength** | λ | the distance between two successive particles **in phase**, e.g. crest to crest or compression to compression | m |
| **Period** | $T$ | the time for one complete oscillation (time for one wavelength to pass a point) | s |
| **Frequency** | $f$ | the number of complete oscillations (waves) per second | hertz (Hz) |
| **Wave speed** | $v$ | the distance travelled by the wave per second | m s⁻¹ |

Particles are **in phase** when they are at the same point in their motion at the same time (for example, two neighbouring crests).

## The wave equation

In one period $T$ the wave moves forward one wavelength λ, so

$$v = \dfrac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda \qquad\text{since}\qquad f = \dfrac{1}{T}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A radio station broadcasts on a frequency of 98.0 MHz. Find the wavelength of its waves. (speed of radio waves = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)**

$$\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{98.0 \times 10^{6}} = 3.06\ \text{m}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Water waves have a wavelength of 0.4 m. A floating bottle bobs up and down 15 times in 10 s. Find (a) the frequency, (b) the period, (c) the speed of the waves.**

(a) $f = \dfrac{15}{10} = 1.5$ Hz. (b) $T = \dfrac{1}{1.5} = 0.67$ s. (c) $v = f\lambda = 1.5 \times 0.4 = 0.6$ m s⁻¹.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Changing the medium changes the **speed** and the **wavelength** of a wave, but **not its frequency**: the frequency is set by the source. Light entering glass slows down and its wavelength shortens; its frequency (and colour) stays the same.
:::

## Graphs of waves

There are two kinds of graph, and they look alike — read the horizontal axis carefully.

| Graph | Horizontal axis | What you read off |
|---|---|---|
| Displacement–**distance** | distance along the wave | amplitude and **wavelength** |
| Displacement–**time** | time, for one particle | amplitude and **period** (then $f = 1/T$) |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Okada riders and market traders across Sierra Leone keep radios tuned to stations on FM frequencies near 100 MHz, whose waves are about 3 m long. Mobile phones use microwaves of about 900 MHz to 2 GHz, with wavelengths of only 15–33 cm.
:::

## Key points

- A wave transfers energy, not matter.
- Mechanical waves need a medium; electromagnetic waves do not.
- Transverse: vibration ⟂ travel (light, ripples). Longitudinal: vibration ∥ travel (sound).
- $v = f\lambda$, $f = 1/T$; frequency is set by the source and does not change between media.

## Quick check

1. Give one example of a longitudinal wave.
2. A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 6 m. Find its speed.
3. What happens to the frequency of a wave when it passes from air into water?

**Answers:** 1. Sound waves. 2. $50 \times 6 = 300$ m s⁻¹. 3. It stays the same.
