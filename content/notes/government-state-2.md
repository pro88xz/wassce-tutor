Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution opens its account of the Republic by declaring that **sovereignty belongs to the people of Sierra Leone**, from whom the government derives all its powers. Yet the same state signs treaties, obeys court rulings from ECOWAS and listens to public opinion. This lesson explains what sovereignty is, its types, where it lies, and why in practice it is never unlimited.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define sovereignty and state its characteristics
- distinguish legal and political, de jure and de facto, and internal and external sovereignty
- explain where sovereignty is located in different systems
- explain the limitations on sovereignty

## Meaning and characteristics

:::callout{type=definition}
**Sovereignty** is the supreme, absolute and unlimited power of the state to make and enforce laws within its territory, without interference from any other state.
:::

The French thinker **Jean Bodin** (1576) first set out the idea of sovereignty as supreme power over citizens. Its traditional characteristics are:

1. **Absoluteness (supremacy):** no authority within the state is higher.
2. **Permanence:** it lasts as long as the state exists, even when governments change.
3. **Universality (comprehensiveness):** it applies to every person and body within the territory.
4. **Indivisibility:** it cannot be split between two rulers of the same state.
5. **Inalienability:** the state cannot give it away and still remain a state.

## Types of sovereignty

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal sovereignty** | Supreme power over all people and bodies within the state | Parliament's laws bind every Sierra Leonean |
| **External sovereignty** | Independence from control by other states; equal status in international relations | Sierra Leone's seat at the UN General Assembly |
| **Legal sovereignty** | Power of the body that the law recognises as having the final say in making law | Parliament (with the President's assent) |
| **Political sovereignty** | Power of the body whose will is ultimately obeyed: the electorate | Voters who choose and remove governments |
| **De jure sovereignty** | Sovereignty that is lawful but may not be in actual control | An elected government driven from the capital by rebels |
| **De facto sovereignty** | Actual control, whether or not lawful | A military junta that seizes power |
| **Popular sovereignty** | The idea that final authority belongs to the people | Section 5 of Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution |

## Where sovereignty lies

- **In a monarchy:** traditionally in the king or queen.
- **In Britain:** legal sovereignty lies in Parliament ("parliamentary supremacy"): Parliament can make or unmake any law.
- **In a federation:** sovereignty is shared between the federal and state governments under the constitution.
- **In a state with a written constitution:** the constitution is supreme, and the people are the ultimate source of authority (popular sovereignty).
- **Under military rule:** in the ruling council, which governs by decree.

## Limitations on sovereignty

![Sovereignty with its internal and external sides, and six limits: the constitution, international law, public opinion, pressure groups, customs and religion, and membership of international organisations](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/sovereignty-limits.svg)

*Figure 1. Sovereignty is supreme in theory but limited in practice. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

1. **The constitution:** a written constitution limits what government may do and protects fundamental rights.
2. **International law and treaties:** states agree to rules on trade, human rights, the sea and war.
3. **Membership of international organisations:** the UN, the AU and ECOWAS impose obligations; for example, the ECOWAS Court of Justice can rule against member states.
4. **Public opinion:** governments avoid policies that provoke strong popular opposition.
5. **Pressure groups:** trade unions, teachers, students and business groups influence policy.
6. **Customs, traditions and religion:** laws that conflict with deeply held beliefs are hard to enforce.
7. **Economic dependence:** states that rely on foreign aid or loans must often accept the conditions of donors and lenders.
8. **Federalism:** sovereignty is divided between levels of government.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**De jure versus de facto.** In May 1997 soldiers of the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC) seized Freetown and President Ahmad Tejan Kabbah fled to Guinea.

- The AFRC had **de facto** sovereignty: it controlled the capital.
- Kabbah's government kept **de jure** sovereignty: it was lawfully elected and was still recognised by ECOWAS, the UN and other states.

In 1998 ECOWAS forces (ECOMOG) removed the junta and Kabbah returned to office.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
As a member of **ECOWAS**, Sierra Leone has agreed to protocols on the free movement of people and goods and on democracy and good governance. Accepting these rules is an exercise of sovereignty (the state chose to join), but it also limits sovereignty in practice, because Sierra Leone must respect decisions made jointly with other states.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say sovereignty means a government can do anything it likes. In theory sovereignty is absolute, but in practice every state's sovereignty is limited by its constitution, international obligations, public opinion and economic realities.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Internal sovereignty**: supreme power over all people and bodies within the state.
- **External sovereignty**: independence from control by other states, with equal status in international relations.
- **De jure sovereignty**: sovereignty that is lawful but may not be in actual control.
- **De facto sovereignty**: actual control of the state, whether or not it is lawful.
- **Popular sovereignty**: the idea that final authority belongs to the people.
:::

## Key points

- **Sovereignty** is the supreme power of the state, first defined by **Jean Bodin**.
- Characteristics: **absoluteness, permanence, universality, indivisibility, inalienability**.
- Types: **internal and external, legal and political, de jure and de facto, popular**.
- In Sierra Leone, **sovereignty belongs to the people**, and the government derives its powers from them through the 1991 Constitution.
- Limits: **constitution, international law, international bodies, public opinion, pressure groups, customs, economic dependence, federalism**.

## Quick check

1. Who first set out the theory of sovereignty?
2. Distinguish legal sovereignty from political sovereignty.
3. What is de facto sovereignty?
4. State three limitations on the sovereignty of a state.

**Answers:**

1. Jean Bodin.
2. Legal sovereignty lies with the body that the law recognises as having the final say in making law (such as Parliament); political sovereignty lies with the body whose will is ultimately obeyed (the electorate).
3. Actual control over a state, whether or not it is lawful, such as control held by a military junta.
4. Any three: the constitution, international law and treaties, membership of international organisations, public opinion, pressure groups, customs and religion, economic dependence, federalism.
