The **road to independence in West Africa** after 1945 ran through a series of **constitutions, elections and protests** that transferred power step by step from colonial governors to elected African governments. **Ghana** (the Gold Coast) led the way, becoming the **first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to win independence** on **6 March 1957** under **Kwame Nkrumah**. **Guinea** followed in **1958** after voting **"No"** in de Gaulle's referendum; **Nigeria** and most **French colonies** became independent in **1960**, **Sierra Leone** on **27 April 1961**, and **The Gambia** in **1965**. This lesson explains **how Ghana, Nigeria and French West Africa won independence**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the stages by which the Gold Coast became independent Ghana
- explain the role of Kwame Nkrumah and the CPP
- describe the constitutional stages of Nigeria's independence
- explain how French West African colonies became independent
- compare the routes to independence in British and French West Africa

## Ghana: the first to independence

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1946** | **Burns Constitution**: first **African majority** in the Legislative Council (mostly not directly elected) |
| **1947** | **United Gold Coast Convention (UGCC)** formed by J. B. Danquah and others; Nkrumah becomes its secretary |
| **February 1948** | **Accra riots**: police fire on ex-servicemen marching to the Governor; three killed; looting and unrest follow |
| **1948** | the **Watson Commission** blames the constitution and recommends reform |
| **June 1949** | Nkrumah breaks with the UGCC and forms the **Convention People's Party (CPP)**, demanding **"self-government now"** |
| **January 1950** | **Positive Action** (strikes and boycotts); Nkrumah **imprisoned** |
| **February 1951** | CPP wins the first general election; Nkrumah released and made **Leader of Government Business** (Prime Minister from **1952**) |
| **1954 and 1956** | further elections won by the CPP |
| **6 March 1957** | independence as **Ghana**, the name of the ancient empire |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why the Gold Coast was first**

1. **Nkrumah's leadership** and the **CPP's mass organisation** among workers, farmers, youth and market women.
2. A **large educated elite** and a strong tradition of politics since the NCBWA.
3. **Wealth from cocoa**, which made the country able to support itself.
4. The **1948 riots** shocked Britain into reform.
5. The country was **relatively united**, with fewer regional divisions than Nigeria.
6. Britain's Labour government accepted **gradual self-government**.
:::

## Nigeria: constitutional steps to 1960

| Constitution | Year | Main features |
|---|---|---|
| **Clifford** | **1922** | **elective principle**: four elected Africans (three for Lagos, one for Calabar) |
| **Richards** | **1946** | **three regions** (North, West, East) with regional houses; African majority, but chosen mainly by Native Authorities |
| **Macpherson** | **1951** | regional governments and a central Council of Ministers; elections; strengthened regional parties |
| **Lyttelton** | **1954** | **federal** constitution; regions given wide powers |
| **Self-government** | **1957 and 1959** | **Western and Eastern regions** self-governing in 1957, **Northern** in 1959 |
| **Independence** | **1 October 1960** | Nigeria independent; **Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa** Prime Minister; republic in 1963 |

**Main parties and leaders:**

- **NCNC** (1944): **Nnamdi Azikiwe** and **Herbert Macaulay**, strongest in the East.
- **Action Group** (1951): **Obafemi Awolowo**, strongest in the West.
- **Northern People's Congress (NPC)** (1951): **Ahmadu Bello**, Sardauna of Sokoto, and **Tafawa Balewa**, strongest in the North.

Nigeria's road was slower because the regions and parties were **divided**: the North feared domination by the more educated South and was unwilling to accept independence in **1956**, as the Action Group proposed in a motion of **1953**.

## French West Africa

![Map of West Africa with each country's year of independence: Ghana 1957, Guinea 1958, Nigeria and most French colonies 1960, Sierra Leone 1961, The Gambia 1965, Guinea-Bissau 1974 and Cabo Verde 1975; Liberia independent since 1847](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/west-africa-independence.svg)

*Figure 1. Independence dates of West African countries. WASSCE Tutor map.*

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1944** | **Brazzaville Conference**: France promises reforms but **rules out independence** |
| **1946** | **French Union**: Africans become citizens; forced labour and the indigénat abolished; African deputies sit in Paris; the **RDA** (Rassemblement Démocratique Africain) founded under **Félix Houphouët-Boigny** |
| **1956** | **Loi-cadre** (framework law): **universal suffrage** and elected territorial assemblies in each colony |
| **1958** | **de Gaulle's referendum**: colonies choose between autonomy in the **French Community** or immediate independence; only **Guinea**, led by **Ahmed Sékou Touré**, votes **"No"** and becomes independent on **2 October 1958** |
| **1960** | the other colonies win independence: **Senegal, Mali, Mauritania, Côte d'Ivoire, Upper Volta (Burkina Faso), Niger, Dahomey (Benin)**, and the trust territory of **Togo** |

France punished Guinea's choice by **withdrawing officials, equipment and aid** at once, but Sékou Touré's stand inspired nationalists across Africa.

## British and French routes compared

| Point | British West Africa | French West Africa |
|---|---|---|
| **Method** | colony by colony through **new constitutions** and elections | through **reforms in Paris** that applied to all colonies at once |
| **Timing** | spread out: **1957–1965** | mostly together in **1960** |
| **Links after independence** | **Commonwealth** | close ties through the French Community, the **CFA franc**, aid and defence agreements |
| **Armed struggle** | none in West Africa | none in West Africa (but war in Algeria) |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's leaders watched Ghana closely. The **Sierra Leone People's Party (SLPP)**, formed in **1951**, followed the same British pattern of **constitutional advance** through the **Stevenson Constitution (1951)** and elections, leading to independence in **1961**. Sierra Leone joined the **Commonwealth** and the **United Nations** in the same year.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not write that Ghana was the first African country to become independent. **Liberia, Ethiopia, Egypt, Libya, Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia** were independent earlier. Ghana is rightly described as the first **colony in sub-Saharan Africa** to win independence from colonial rule.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Kwame Nkrumah**: the founder of the Convention People's Party (1949), who led the Gold Coast to independence as Ghana on 6 March 1957.
- **Positive Action**: the campaign of strikes and boycotts launched by Nkrumah's CPP in January 1950 to demand self-government.
- **Loi-cadre** (1956): the French "framework law" that gave universal suffrage and elected territorial assemblies to each French West African colony.
:::

## Key points

- **Ghana (6 March 1957)**: Burns Constitution 1946 → UGCC 1947 → **Accra riots 1948** → **CPP 1949** → Positive Action 1950 → elections 1951 → independence.
- **Nigeria (1 October 1960)**: Clifford 1922 → Richards 1946 → Macpherson 1951 → Lyttelton 1954 → regional self-government 1957–59.
- **French West Africa**: Brazzaville 1944 → French Union 1946 → **Loi-cadre 1956** → **1958 referendum** (Guinea says No) → independence **1960**.
- **Sierra Leone 1961**, **The Gambia 1965**, **Guinea-Bissau 1973–74** after armed struggle.

2. What happened in Accra in February 1948?
3. What did "Positive Action" mean?
4. Name Nigeria's four colonial constitutions from 1922 to 1954.
5. Why did Guinea become independent in 1958?
6. Give one difference between the British and French routes to independence.

**Answers:**

1. On 6 March 1957; Kwame Nkrumah.
2. Police fired on ex-servicemen marching to the Governor, killing three, and riots followed.
3. Nkrumah's campaign of strikes, boycotts and non-cooperation in 1950 to win self-government.
4. Clifford (1922), Richards (1946), Macpherson (1951) and Lyttelton (1954).
5. It voted "No" to de Gaulle's 1958 referendum on joining the French Community, choosing immediate independence.
6. Any one: British colonies advanced one by one through new constitutions, while French colonies advanced together through laws made in Paris; British colonies became independent over 1957–65 while most French colonies did so in 1960; British colonies joined the Commonwealth while French colonies kept close ties with France.
