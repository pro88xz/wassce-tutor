:::callout{type=definition}
The **government budget** is the annual estimate of the revenue the government expects to collect and the expenditure it plans to make.
:::

Once Parliament passes it as the **Appropriation Act**, it gives the legal **authority** to spend. All public revenue is paid into the **Consolidated Revenue Fund**, and money can only be withdrawn from it as authorised by law. Controls such as **warrants**, the **vote book**, **public procurement rules** and the **Auditor-General's audit** make sure public money is spent only for approved purposes. This lesson explains the budget, the Consolidated Revenue Fund and the controls over public spending.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the government budget and the stages of the budget process
- distinguish recurrent from capital (development) expenditure
- explain the Consolidated Revenue Fund
- describe controls over public expenditure, including warrants and the vote book
- explain the role of the Auditor-General and the Public Accounts Committee

## The budget

The **budget** is the government's financial plan for a year. Stages:

1. **Preparation:** ministries, departments and agencies (MDAs) submit estimates; the **Ministry of Finance** compiles them.
2. **Approval:** the Minister of Finance presents the budget to **Parliament**, which debates and passes the **Appropriation Act**.
3. **Execution:** funds are released to MDAs and spent.
4. **Accounting and reporting:** the **Accountant-General** records spending and prepares the public accounts.
5. **Audit:** the **Auditor-General** audits the accounts and reports to Parliament.

## Types of expenditure

| Recurrent expenditure | Capital (development) expenditure |
|---|---|
| spent **regularly** to run government | spent on **long-lasting** assets |
| salaries, office running costs, fuel, interest on debt | roads, bridges, schools, hospitals, power projects |
| benefits used up within the year | benefits last for many years |
| financed mainly by **taxes** | often financed by **loans and grants** as well |

A **budget surplus** occurs when revenue exceeds expenditure; a **budget deficit** when expenditure exceeds revenue.

## The Consolidated Revenue Fund

All revenues and other money raised or received by the government (with some exceptions allowed by law) are paid into the **Consolidated Revenue Fund**, established under the **1991 Constitution of Sierra Leone**. Money can be withdrawn only:

- to meet expenditure **charged on the Fund** by the Constitution or by law (such as certain salaries and public debt charges), or
- as **authorised by an Appropriation Act** passed by Parliament.

## Controls over public expenditure

| Control | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Appropriation Act** | sets the maximum each MDA may spend for each purpose (each **vote**) |
| **Warrants** | written authority from the Ministry of Finance releasing funds to be spent |
| **Vote book** | kept by each MDA to record commitments and payments against each vote, so spending never exceeds the amount approved |
| **Public procurement rules** | require competitive and transparent buying of goods and services |
| **Internal audit** | checks within each MDA |
| **External audit** | the **Auditor-General** audits the accounts of all MDAs |
| **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** | a parliamentary committee that examines the Auditor-General's report and questions officials |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Each year the **Audit Service Sierra Leone** publishes the Auditor-General's report on the government's accounts, and the **Public Accounts Committee** of Parliament holds hearings at which officials must explain any unsupported payments or missing records. These reports are an important check on how public money is used.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **warrant** is not a cheque. It is the **authority** to spend from the funds Parliament has approved; the actual payment is then made through the government's accounts.
:::

## Key points

- The **budget** is the government's annual plan; Parliament passes it as the **Appropriation Act**.
- **Recurrent** expenditure runs government; **capital** expenditure creates long-lasting assets.
- All public revenue goes into the **Consolidated Revenue Fund**; withdrawals need legal authority.
- Controls: **Appropriation Act, warrants, vote book, procurement rules, internal and external audit, PAC**.

## Quick check

1. What is the Appropriation Act?
2. Give two examples each of recurrent and capital expenditure.
3. What is the Consolidated Revenue Fund?
4. What is the purpose of a vote book?
5. To whom does the Auditor-General report?

**Answers:**

1. The law passed by Parliament approving the government's budget and authorising spending.
2. Recurrent: any two of salaries, running costs, fuel, interest on debt. Capital: any two of roads, bridges, schools, hospitals, power projects.
3. The fund into which all government revenue is paid, and from which money can be withdrawn only as authorised by law.
4. To record commitments and payments against each vote so that spending does not exceed the amount approved.
5. To Parliament.
