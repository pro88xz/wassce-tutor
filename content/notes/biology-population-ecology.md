A **population** is all the members of one species living in an area: the rats on a rice farm, the oil palms in a plantation, the people of Freetown. Populations grow, shrink, crowd together or spread out, and the reasons can be measured. Farmers need this knowledge to control pests; fisheries officers to set catch limits; health workers to plan for a growing number of children. This lesson covers the characteristics of populations, how to estimate their size, the growth curve, the factors that control population size, and human population growth in Sierra Leone.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the characteristics of a population
- estimate population size with quadrats and by capture–recapture
- describe and explain the S-shaped growth curve
- explain the factors that increase and decrease population size
- distinguish density-dependent and density-independent factors
- interpret age–sex pyramids and discuss human population growth

## Characteristics of a population

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **size** | the total number of individuals |
| **density** | the number per unit area or volume (e.g. 3 plants per m²) |
| **distribution** | how individuals are spread: clumped, even or random |
| **age structure** | the proportions of young, adult and old individuals |
| **birth rate** (natality) | the number of births per 1000 individuals per year |
| **death rate** (mortality) | the number of deaths per 1000 individuals per year |

## Estimating population size

**Plants and slow animals:** use **quadrats** placed at random (see the introduction to ecology lesson): mean number per m² × total area.

**Mobile animals** (fish, rats, grasshoppers, snails): use **capture–recapture**.

:::callout{type=definition}
1. Capture a sample, count it ($n_1$), **mark** each animal harmlessly, and release them.
2. Wait for them to mix back into the population.
3. Capture a second sample ($n_2$) and count how many are **marked** ($m$).

$$\text{estimated population } N = \frac{n_1 \times n_2}{m}$$

(This is the **Lincoln index**.)
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**In a school pond, 40 snails were caught, marked with a dot of paint and released. A week later 50 snails were caught, of which 8 were marked. Estimate the snail population.**

$$N = \frac{40 \times 50}{8} = \frac{2000}{8} = 250 \text{ snails}$$
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Forgetting the assumptions.** The method works only if the marks do not come off or harm the animals (making them easier for predators to see), the marked animals mix fully, and there are no births, deaths or migration between the two samples. Mention these if asked for limitations.
:::

## Population growth

![An S-shaped growth curve: a slow lag phase, a steep exponential phase, then a stationary phase where the population levels off at the carrying capacity](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/population-growth.svg)

When a few organisms (yeast in sugar solution, rats on a newly cleared farm) enter a suitable habitat, the population follows an **S-shaped (sigmoid) curve**:

| Phase | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **lag** | slow growth | few individuals to breed; adjusting to the new habitat |
| **exponential (log)** | rapid growth, numbers doubling regularly | plenty of food and space; birth rate far above death rate |
| **stationary** | levels off | food, space or other resources run short; birth rate = death rate |
| **decline** (sometimes) | falls | resources used up, waste builds up, or disease spreads |

The maximum population the environment can support for a long time is its **carrying capacity**.

## Factors that change population size

A population **increases** by **births** and **immigration** (moving in), and **decreases** by **deaths** and **emigration** (moving out).

:::callout{type=definition}
**Density-dependent factors** have a greater effect when the population is **crowded**:

- **competition** for food, water, space and mates
- **predation** (predators find crowded prey more easily)
- **disease and parasites** (they spread faster in crowds)
- build-up of **toxic wastes**

**Density-independent factors** affect the population whatever its density:

- floods, drought, bush fires, storms
- extreme temperatures
- pollution, such as an oil spill
:::

### Predator and prey

When prey (such as rats) increase, their predators (owls, snakes) have more food and increase too, after a delay. More predators eat more prey, so the prey decreases, and then the predators decrease from lack of food. The two populations rise and fall in linked **cycles**, with the predator peak just after the prey peak.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Rats after the rice harvest.** In many farming areas, rat numbers rise after harvest, when spilled grain gives them plenty of food and they move into stores and homes. Farmers who clear grass around stores, keep grain in sealed containers and protect owls and snakes (which eat rats) are using population ecology: they reduce food and shelter (density-dependent factors) and support the predators.
:::

## Human population

### Age–sex pyramids

An **age–sex pyramid** shows the percentage of males and females in each age group.

| Shape | Meaning | Typical of |
|---|---|---|
| **wide base, narrow top** | many children, high birth rate, short life expectancy: a growing population | Sierra Leone and many developing countries |
| **straight sides** | birth and death rates similar: a stable population | some developed countries |
| **narrow base** | few births, many old people: a shrinking population | Japan, parts of Europe |

### Growth in Sierra Leone

Sierra Leone's 2015 census counted about **7.1 million** people, and the population has grown since. A large share of the population is **under 15**. This means a growing need for schools, teachers, health care, jobs, housing, water and food, and more pressure on forests and farmland.

:::callout{type=tip}
When asked about the **effects of rapid population growth**, think in pairs: **resources** (food, water, land, fuel wood) and **services** (schools, hospitals, housing, jobs, transport), plus the **environment** (deforestation, pollution, waste).
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Define population density."}
The number of individuals of a species per unit area (or volume) of habitat.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. 60 grasshoppers were caught and marked. Later, 45 were caught, of which 9 were marked. Estimate the population."}
$N = \dfrac{60 \times 45}{9} = 300$.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Explain the stationary phase of a growth curve."}
Resources such as food and space become limiting; competition, predation and disease increase, so the **death rate rises to equal the birth rate** and the population stops growing.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Give two density-dependent and two density-independent factors."}
Density-dependent: competition, predation, disease, toxic waste (any two). Density-independent: floods, drought, fire, extreme temperature (any two).
:::

:::reveal{title="5. What does a wide-based age pyramid tell you about a country?"}
It has a **high birth rate** and many young dependants; the population is **growing** quickly, and life expectancy is usually lower.
:::

## Key points

- Population characteristics: **size, density, distribution, age structure, birth and death rates**.
- Estimate size with **quadrats** (plants) or **capture–recapture** $N = \dfrac{n_1 n_2}{m}$ (mobile animals).
- **S-shaped curve**: lag, exponential, stationary; the level is the **carrying capacity**.
- Births and immigration increase a population; deaths and emigration decrease it.
- **Density-dependent**: competition, predation, disease. **Density-independent**: floods, fire, drought.
- A **wide-based** age pyramid shows a young, fast-growing population, as in Sierra Leone.
