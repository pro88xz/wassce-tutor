A speech is writing meant to be **heard**. That changes everything: the audience cannot reread a sentence they missed, so a good speech greets them properly, tells them clearly what it is about, makes its points one by one with signposts, speaks to them directly, and ends with words they will remember. WASSCE sets speeches and debates regularly, and both have a format that examiners check. This lesson covers the format, the techniques that make a speech persuasive, and a complete model speech.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- write a correct vocative (the opening address to the audience)
- introduce yourself and your topic as a speaker does
- organise a speech with clear signposts
- use spoken techniques: direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition, the rule of three
- close a speech memorably
- adapt the format for a debate

## The format of a speech

:::callout{type=definition}
A speech has five parts:

1. a **title** (if the question asks for one, or for a published speech)
2. the **vocative**: greeting the audience, most important first
3. the **introduction**: who you are and what you will speak about
4. the **body**: your points, one per paragraph, with signposts
5. the **conclusion** and the closing words: *Thank you for listening.*
:::

### The vocative

Greet the audience **in order of importance**, on separate lines or in one line separated by commas:

> *The Chairman, the Principal, members of staff, distinguished guests, parents, fellow students, ladies and gentlemen,*

| Occasion | Vocative |
|---|---|
| School speech day | *The Chairman, the Principal, members of staff, parents, fellow students,* |
| Community meeting | *The Paramount Chief, elders, the Councillor, ladies and gentlemen,* |
| Debate | *The Chairman, panel of judges, the time-keeper, co-debaters, ladies and gentlemen,* |
| Health talk to students | *The Principal, teachers, fellow students,* |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Starting with "Dear audience,"** or putting the students before the Principal. A speech is not a letter, and the vocative follows the order of importance in the hall.
:::

### The introduction

Introduce yourself and your topic in the first paragraph:

> *I am Fatmata Koroma, the Senior Girls' Prefect, and I stand before you this morning to speak on a matter that affects every one of us in this hall: the dangers of social media to students.*

### The conclusion

Sum up in a fresh way, call the audience to action, and end with **Thank you for listening** (or *for your kind attention*).

## Techniques that make a speech work

| Technique | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct address** | *You have all seen it happen…* | involves every listener |
| **Inclusive "we"** | *We cannot wait for others to act.* | builds a sense of community |
| **Rhetorical question** | *How many of us slept before midnight last night?* | makes the audience think |
| **Rule of three** | *It steals our time, our sleep and our grades.* | a rhythm that is easy to remember |
| **Repetition** | *Not tomorrow. Not next term. Today.* | emphasis |
| **Signposts** | *My first point… Secondly… Finally…* | helps listeners follow |
| **A short story** | *Last term, a Form Five boy…* | makes the point real |

:::callout{type=tip}
Read your opening and closing lines **aloud in your head**. If they would sound flat in a crowded hall, sharpen them. A speech is judged on how it would sound.
:::

## A complete model speech

**Question:** *You are the Senior Prefect of your school. Write a speech to be delivered at the school's speech day on the topic "Social media: friend or foe to the Sierra Leonean student?"*

::::model{title="Social media: friend or foe?" label="Model speech"}
**SOCIAL MEDIA: FRIEND OR FOE TO THE SIERRA LEONEAN STUDENT?**

The Chairman, the Principal, members of staff, distinguished guests, parents, fellow students, ladies and gentlemen,

I am Fatmata Koroma, the Senior Prefect, and I am honoured to stand before you on this speech day to speak on a question that touches every one of us in this hall: is social media a friend or a foe to the Sierra Leonean student?

Let me begin with a confession. Last night I promised myself I would sleep at ten. At ten, I opened WhatsApp "for five minutes". When I looked up, it was half past twelve. How many of you have made the same promise, and broken it in the same way? I see some of you smiling. You know exactly what I mean.

Social media can be a true friend. Through it, a student in Kailahun can watch a chemistry lesson recorded in Freetown, join a study group that meets at midnight, or ask a question and receive an answer within minutes. It connects us with brothers and sisters abroad, and it has given many young Sierra Leoneans a voice and even a business. Used well, it is a library, a classroom and a market in our pockets.

But a friend who steals from you is no friend at all, and social media steals three things from students. It steals our time: the hour we planned for revision disappears into videos and arguments about football. It steals our sleep: we arrive at school with heavy eyes and empty heads. And, if we are not careful, it steals our peace: rumours, insults and cruel comments spread faster than any teacher can stop them, and some of our classmates suffer in silence because of what is posted about them.

So what are we to do? We cannot throw our phones into the sea, and we should not. Instead, I propose three simple rules. First, study before you scroll: finish your homework before you open any app. Secondly, give your phone a bedtime: switch it off at ten, and keep it out of your bedroom. Thirdly, think before you post: if you would not say it to someone's face, do not type it.

Parents, we ask you to help us keep these rules at home. Teachers, we ask you to show us the good websites and channels that can support our learning. And fellow students, we ask ourselves to remember why we are here.

Social media is a tool, and like a cutlass it can clear a farm or cut the hand that holds it. Whether it becomes our friend or our foe is not decided by the phone. It is decided by us, every evening, one choice at a time.

Thank you for listening.
::::

### How it earns the marks

- **Format**: title, a full vocative in order of importance, self-introduction, *Thank you for listening*.
- **Content**: both sides of *friend or foe*, developed, then practical solutions, exactly what the question asks.
- **Spoken techniques**: a personal story (the 10 p.m. promise), rhetorical questions, the rule of three (*time, sleep, peace*), direct address to parents, teachers and students, a memorable image (the cutlass) at the end.
- **Length**: about 460 words, comfortably over the minimum.

## Debates: a speech that takes a side

A debate speech has the same format, with three differences:

1. The vocative greets the **panel of judges**, the **time-keeper** and the **co-debaters**.
2. You **state your side** clearly at the start: *"I stand here to support the motion that…"* or *"…to oppose the motion that…"*.
3. You **answer the other side** (rebuttal): *"My opponent may tell you that… but…"*.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Opening of a debate speech:**

*The Chairman, panel of judges, the time-keeper, co-debaters, ladies and gentlemen,*

*I am Ibrahim Sesay of Form Five Science, and I stand before you to **oppose** the motion that "Boarding schools are better than day schools". Before I give my reasons, let me make one thing clear: the question is not which school has bigger buildings, but which one produces better students.*
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Write the vocative for a speech at a community meeting on keeping the town clean."}
*The Paramount Chief, the Councillor, elders of the community, religious leaders, ladies and gentlemen, my fellow young people,*
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Rewrite as a rule of three: 'Drug abuse is bad for health. It is also bad for education.'"}
*Drug abuse destroys our health, ruins our education and robs our families of their hope.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Write a rebuttal sentence against: 'Students need phones in school for research.'"}
*My opponent will tell you that students need phones for research. But how much research happens on WhatsApp at break time? Our school library and our teachers can give us everything a phone can, without the distractions.*
:::

## Key points

- Format: **title**, **vocative** (most important first), **self-introduction**, body, **Thank you for listening**.
- Use **spoken techniques**: direct address, *we*, rhetorical questions, the rule of three, repetition, signposts, a short story.
- In a **debate**, greet the judges and time-keeper, **state your side**, and **answer your opponents**.
- End with a line the audience will remember.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Examiners check the **vocative**, the **self-introduction** and the **closing words** under Organisation, and reward speeches that **sound** like speeches under Expression. A speech written exactly like an essay, with no audience in it, loses marks even if the ideas are good.
:::
