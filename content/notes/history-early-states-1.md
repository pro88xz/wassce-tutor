**Ancient Ghana** (called **Wagadu** by its founders, the **Soninke**) was the first great empire of the Western Sudan, flourishing from about the **4th century** to the **11th century AD** in the Sahel of present-day **south-eastern Mauritania and western Mali**, far north of modern Ghana. Its wealth came from controlling the **trans-Saharan trade**, especially **gold** from the south and **salt** from the north. Its capital, **Kumbi Saleh**, was described by the Arab geographer **al-Bakri** in **1068**. Ghana declined in the late eleventh century after **Almoravid** attacks, drought and the loss of its trade. This lesson explains the rise, organisation, economy and fall of ancient Ghana.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- locate ancient Ghana and explain how it differed from modern Ghana
- explain the factors that led to the rise of ancient Ghana
- describe the government and administration of ancient Ghana
- describe its economy and the role of the gold–salt trade
- explain the causes of its decline

Ancient Ghana lay between the **upper Senegal and upper Niger rivers**, hundreds of kilometres north-west of today's Republic of Ghana, which took the name at independence in **1957** to honour this great African empire.

## Factors in the rise of Ghana

1. **Control of trade:** Ghana stood between the **gold fields** of Bambuk and Bure to the south and the **salt** of the Sahara to the north, so it could tax both.
2. **Iron weapons:** the Soninke used **iron** spears and swords, giving them an advantage over neighbours.
3. **Strong kings and good organisation**, with a powerful **army** said to number many thousands.
4. **Fertile land** along the rivers supported farming and a growing population.
5. **The camel**, used widely in the Sahara from the early centuries AD, made regular caravan trade possible.

## Government and administration

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **The king** | the title *Ghana* meant "war chief"; the king was the supreme ruler, judge and religious head, believed to have divine powers |
| **Succession** | **matrilineal**: the king was succeeded by his **sister's son** |
| **Provinces** | conquered areas were ruled by **vassal kings** who paid tribute; the heartland was ruled directly |
| **Officials** | ministers and advisers, including **Muslim scribes** who kept records and handled foreign trade |
| **Justice** | the king heard cases and appeals; trial by ordeal was used |
| **Army** | a large army of foot soldiers and archers, used to protect trade routes and collect tribute |

## Economy

- **Taxes on trade:** according to al-Bakri, the king charged a duty on every load of **salt** entering and leaving the kingdom.
- **Gold:** gold nuggets belonged to the **king**, while gold dust could be traded, which kept the price of gold high.
- **Farming, cattle rearing, crafts** (iron working, weaving, pottery).
- **Tribute** from vassal states.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Al-Bakri's description of Kumbi Saleh (1068)**

Al-Bakri, writing in Córdoba from the reports of traders, described Kumbi Saleh as **two towns**: one where the **Muslim merchants** lived, with **twelve mosques**, and the king's town, about six miles away, with the **palace**, the sacred groves of the traditional religion, and the houses of the court. This shows that the king followed the **traditional religion** but welcomed **Muslim traders and scribes**, who were essential to the trans-Saharan trade. Archaeologists excavating Kumbi Saleh have found stone houses and a mosque that support his account.
:::

## Decline of Ghana

1. **Almoravid attacks:** the **Almoravids**, a Berber Islamic reform movement from the Sahara, attacked Ghana; Arab sources date the capture of Kumbi Saleh to **1076**, though historians debate how complete the conquest was.
2. **Drought and desertification** reduced farmland and pasture in the Sahel.
3. **Shift of trade routes** eastwards, away from Ghana's towns.
4. **Revolts of vassal states** once the centre was weak.
5. **Rise of rivals**, especially the **Sosso** under **Sumanguru Kante**, who seized the remnants of Ghana in the early thirteenth century, before Sosso itself was defeated by **Mali**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Ancient Ghana was **not** in the area of today's Republic of Ghana. Modern Ghana borrowed the name in 1957. Ancient Ghana lay in the Sahel of present-day Mauritania and Mali.
:::

## Key points

- Ancient **Ghana (Wagadu)** of the **Soninke**, about the 4th to 11th centuries, in present-day **Mauritania and Mali**.
- Rise: **trade between gold and salt areas, iron weapons, strong kings, fertile land, the camel**.
- Government: divine **king**, **matrilineal** succession, **vassal kings**, Muslim **scribes**, large **army**.
- Economy: **taxes on salt and trade**, royal control of **gold nuggets**, farming, tribute.
- Decline: **Almoravids (1076), drought, shift of trade, revolts, rise of Sosso**.

## Quick check

1. Which people founded ancient Ghana?
2. What was the capital of ancient Ghana?
3. Why did the king keep all gold nuggets?
4. What type of succession did Ghana practise?
5. Give three reasons for the decline of Ghana.

**Answers:**

1. The Soninke.
2. Kumbi Saleh.
3. To keep gold scarce and its price high.
4. Matrilineal succession: the king's sister's son succeeded.
5. Any three: Almoravid attacks; drought and desertification; shift of trade routes; revolts of vassals; rise of the Sosso.
