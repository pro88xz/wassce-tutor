Every afternoon on the Freetown peninsula a cool breeze blows in from the sea; late at night it reverses. A good flask keeps tea hot all morning. The Earth stays warm enough for life. All three are heat transfer at work.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain land and sea breezes using convection and specific heat capacity
- explain how each part of a vacuum flask reduces heat transfer
- explain the greenhouse effect and its link to global warming
- apply heat transfer ideas to houses, clothing and cooking

## Land and sea breezes

**Sea breeze (daytime):** the **land heats up faster** than the sea (land has a lower specific heat capacity). Air above the land is warmed, expands, becomes less dense and **rises**. Cooler, denser air from over the sea moves in to replace it: a **sea breeze blows from the sea to the land**.

**Land breeze (night):** the **land cools faster** than the sea. Now the air above the **sea** is warmer and rises, and cooler air from the land flows out to replace it: a **land breeze blows from the land to the sea**.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Fishermen at Goderich and Tombo have long used these breezes: setting out in the early morning with the land breeze behind them, and returning in the afternoon helped by the sea breeze.
:::

## The vacuum (Thermos) flask

![A vacuum flask: double glass walls with a vacuum between them, silvered surfaces, an insulating stopper and insulating supports inside a protective case](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/vacuum-flask.svg)

*Figure 1. Each part of a vacuum flask blocks a method of heat transfer. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Part | Reduces heat transfer by |
|---|---|
| **Vacuum** between the double walls | **conduction** and **convection** (both need a medium) |
| **Silvered** inner surfaces | **radiation** (shiny surfaces are poor emitters and good reflectors) |
| **Stopper** of cork or plastic (bad conductor) | **conduction** and **convection** through the top; stops evaporation |
| **Insulating supports** (cork or felt pads) | **conduction** to the outer case |

The flask keeps **hot things hot** and **cold things cold**: it reduces heat transfer in either direction. Some heat is still lost, mostly by conduction through the glass at the neck and through the stopper.

## The greenhouse effect

1. **Short-wavelength** radiation from the very hot Sun passes easily through glass (or through the atmosphere).
2. It is absorbed by the plants and soil, which warm up.
3. They re-emit radiation, but because they are much cooler, it is **long-wavelength infrared**.
4. Glass (or carbon dioxide and water vapour in the atmosphere) **does not let long-wavelength infrared pass** easily, so the energy is **trapped** and the temperature rises.

In the atmosphere, this natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be. **Burning fuels and cutting down forests** add carbon dioxide, **enhancing** the effect and causing **global warming**: rising temperatures, rising sea levels and more extreme weather.

:::callout{type=local-example}
A car parked in the sun in Freetown with its windows shut becomes much hotter inside than the air outside — a small greenhouse. Coastal communities such as those on the Freetown peninsula and Sherbro Island are among those most at risk from rising sea levels.
:::

## Heat transfer in daily life

| Situation | Physics |
|---|---|
| Woollen blankets and cotton clothes keep you warm | trapped air is a bad conductor |
| Thatched or ceiling-lined houses are cooler in the dry season | insulating layers reduce conduction from the hot roof |
| Corrugated iron roofs make rooms very hot | metal conducts and radiates the Sun's heat into the room |
| Ventilation openings high in walls | warm air rises and escapes by convection |
| Cooking pots with copper or aluminium bottoms | good conductors spread heat evenly |
| Clay pots keep water cool | evaporation through the pores absorbs latent heat |
| Igloos (in cold countries) keep people warm | snow traps air, a poor conductor |

## Key points

- Sea breeze by day (land warms faster); land breeze by night (land cools faster).
- Vacuum flask: vacuum stops conduction and convection; silvering reduces radiation; stopper and supports reduce conduction.
- Greenhouse effect: short-wave radiation enters, long-wave infrared is trapped; extra CO₂ causes global warming.
- Trapped air is a good insulator in clothing, blankets and roofs.

## Quick check

1. In which direction does a sea breeze blow, and when?
2. Which part of a vacuum flask reduces heat loss by radiation?
3. Why do woollen clothes keep you warm?

**Answers:** 1. From the sea to the land, during the day. 2. The silvered surfaces of the walls. 3. Wool traps air, which is a poor conductor of heat.
