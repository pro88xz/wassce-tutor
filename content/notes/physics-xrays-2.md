X-rays let a doctor see a broken bone without cutting the skin and let airport staff see inside a suitcase. They are produced when fast electrons smash into metal — and they must be handled with care.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the production of X-rays in an X-ray tube
- distinguish hard and soft X-rays and how each is controlled
- state the properties and uses of X-rays
- describe the dangers of X-rays and the precautions taken

## The X-ray tube

![An X-ray tube: electrons from a heated cathode are accelerated by a very high p.d. across an evacuated tube and strike a tungsten target set in a copper anode, which emits X-rays](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/xray-tube.svg)

*Figure 1. An X-ray tube. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

1. A **heated filament (cathode)** emits electrons by **thermionic emission**.
2. A **very high p.d.** (tens of thousands of volts) accelerates them across an **evacuated** tube.
3. They strike a **tungsten target** set in a **copper anode**, and **X-rays** are produced as they decelerate suddenly.
4. Only about **1%** of the electrons' energy becomes X-rays; the rest becomes **heat**. The target is made of **tungsten** (very high melting point), and the **copper** anode with **cooling fins** (or oil cooling, or a rotating anode) conducts the heat away.
5. The tube is surrounded by **lead shielding** with a window for the useful beam.

## Hard and soft X-rays

| Quantity controlled | By | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality** (penetrating power) | the **accelerating p.d.** | higher p.d. → faster electrons → higher-frequency, more penetrating **hard** X-rays; lower p.d. → **soft** X-rays |
| **Intensity** (number of X-rays) | the **filament current** (cathode temperature) | hotter filament → more electrons → more X-rays |

The maximum frequency of the X-rays produced is set by the accelerating p.d.: an electron that loses all its energy in one collision gives a photon with

$$hf_{max} = eV \Rightarrow \lambda_{min} = \dfrac{hc}{eV}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**An X-ray tube operates at 50 kV. Find the minimum wavelength of the X-rays. (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)**

$$\lambda_{min} = \dfrac{hc}{eV} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 50\,000} = 2.5 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}$$
:::

## Properties of X-rays

1. They are **electromagnetic waves** of very short wavelength (about $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-12}$ m).
2. They travel in **straight lines** at the speed of light.
3. They are **not deflected** by electric or magnetic fields (no charge).
4. They **penetrate** matter; the penetration is less for **dense** materials (bone, lead) than for soft tissue.
5. They affect **photographic film** and cause **fluorescence**.
6. They **ionise** gases and can damage living cells.
7. They are **diffracted** by crystals (their wavelength is similar to the spacing of atoms).

## Uses of X-rays

| Field | Use |
|---|---|
| **Medicine** | radiographs of bones, teeth and chest (soft X-rays); **CT scans**; radiotherapy to destroy cancer cells (hard X-rays) |
| **Industry** | detecting cracks and flaws in metal castings and welds (hard X-rays) |
| **Security** | scanning luggage and cargo at airports and seaports |
| **Science** | **X-ray crystallography**: finding the arrangement of atoms in crystals (DNA's structure was found this way) |
| **Art and history** | examining paintings and relics without damage |

:::callout{type=local-example}
At Lungi International Airport and the Queen Elizabeth II Quay in Freetown, luggage and containers pass through X-ray scanners. In hospitals, a chest X-ray helps diagnose tuberculosis and pneumonia.
:::

## Dangers and precautions

X-rays **ionise** atoms in living cells, which can cause **burns**, **damage to DNA**, **cancer** and genetic defects. Precautions:

1. Expose patients for the **shortest time** and to the lowest dose that gives a clear image.
2. Radiographers stand **behind lead screens** or leave the room during exposure.
3. Patients wear **lead aprons** over parts not being examined (especially reproductive organs).
4. Workers wear **film badges** (dosimeters) to monitor their total exposure.
5. X-ray rooms have **lead-lined** walls and doors; X-rays are avoided during pregnancy where possible.

## Key points

- X-rays are produced when fast electrons (accelerated by a very high p.d.) strike a tungsten target.
- Higher p.d. → harder (more penetrating) X-rays; higher filament current → more intense X-rays.
- $\lambda_{min} = hc/eV$.
- X-rays are penetrating, ionising electromagnetic waves; used in medicine, industry, security and crystallography.
- Shielding with lead, short exposures and monitoring protect people.

## Quick check

1. Why is the target of an X-ray tube made of tungsten?
2. How can the penetrating power of X-rays be increased?
3. Give one precaution for people working with X-rays.

**Answers:** 1. It has a very high melting point and can withstand the heat produced. 2. By increasing the accelerating p.d. across the tube. 3. For example: work behind lead screens, or wear a film badge to monitor exposure.
