# Weathering: physical, chemical and biological processes

## Learning objectives
You should be able to define weathering, distinguish its main types, explain the factors controlling its rate and describe its importance to landscapes and soils.

## Meaning of weathering
Weathering is the breakdown or alteration of rock at or near the Earth's surface without the material being transported away. It differs from erosion, which involves the removal and transport of weathered material by water, wind, ice, waves or gravity.

Weathering weakens rock, contributes to soil formation and prepares material for erosion. Its rate depends on climate, rock type, joints and fractures, relief, vegetation and the length of time the rock is exposed.

## Physical or mechanical weathering
Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemical composition.

- **Thermal expansion and contraction:** repeated heating and cooling can cause minerals to expand and contract at different rates, encouraging cracking.
- **Freeze–thaw action:** water enters cracks, freezes and expands; repeated cycles may widen the cracks. This is most effective where temperatures repeatedly cross the freezing point.
- **Pressure release or exfoliation:** when overlying material is removed, pressure is reduced and outer rock layers may expand and peel away.
- **Salt crystallisation:** salty water enters pores and cracks; evaporation leaves crystals that exert pressure on the rock.
- **Wetting and drying:** repeated moisture changes can cause some rocks and minerals, especially clays, to swell and shrink.

## Chemical weathering
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock through reactions with water, oxygen, carbon dioxide and acids.

- **Solution:** soluble minerals dissolve in water.
- **Carbonation:** carbon dioxide dissolves in rainwater to form weak carbonic acid, which reacts with carbonate rocks such as limestone.
- **Oxidation:** oxygen reacts with iron-bearing minerals, producing oxides that may weaken the rock.
- **Hydrolysis:** water reacts with silicate minerals, converting some into clay minerals and releasing dissolved substances.
- **Hydration:** minerals take up water into their structure and may expand.

Chemical weathering is generally faster in warm, moist conditions because water is available and chemical reactions proceed more rapidly.

## Biological weathering
Living organisms contribute to both physical and chemical breakdown. Plant roots grow into joints and widen cracks. Burrowing animals expose fresh material and disturb soil. Lichens and microorganisms may produce organic acids that react with minerals. Human activities such as excavation and construction can expose rock and accelerate breakdown.

## Factors controlling weathering
Climate is important: temperature range and rainfall influence the dominant processes. Rock composition and structure determine resistance; jointed rock exposes more surface area. Steep slopes may remove weathered material quickly, while vegetation can protect surfaces but roots can also widen cracks.

## WASSCE examination focus
Define weathering and distinguish it from erosion. For each type, name the process, explain how it works and identify the conditions under which it is effective. Do not describe the transport of rock fragments as weathering.

## Quick check
1. Define weathering.
2. Distinguish physical from chemical weathering.
3. Name two chemical weathering processes.
4. Why is chemical weathering often intense in warm, wet climates?

**Answers:** 1. Breakdown or alteration of rock in place. 2. Physical changes size without changing composition; chemical changes mineral composition. 3. Carbonation, oxidation, hydrolysis, hydration or solution. 4. Water is abundant and warmth generally increases reaction rates.
