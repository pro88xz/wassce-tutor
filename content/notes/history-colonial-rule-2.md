The **French policy of assimilation** aimed to turn colonised Africans into **French citizens** in language, culture, religion and law, so that the colonies became part of France. Because it proved costly and was resisted, France changed to a **policy of association** in the early twentieth century, but in practice ruled **directly** through French officials, with chiefs as **appointed agents**. French West Africa (**AOF**), formed in **1895** and governed from **Dakar** from 1902, was run through the **Governor-General, Lieutenant-Governors and commandants de cercle**, and most Africans were **subjects** under the harsh **indigénat** code. This lesson explains **assimilation, association and French direct rule**, and compares them with British **indirect rule**.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

## The policy of assimilation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Assimilation** was the French policy of making colonised peoples **French** in **culture, language, law and political rights**, on the belief that French civilisation was superior and that Africans who adopted it should become **French citizens** with the same rights as people in France.
:::

**Features:**

1. African **citizens** had the same rights as French people, including voting and being elected.
2. **French language, education and law** replaced local ones.
3. The colonies were treated as **overseas parts of France**.
4. Colonies sent **deputies** (representatives) to the French parliament.
5. African rulers and institutions were **ignored or abolished**.

**Where it was applied:** fully only in the **Four Communes of Senegal**: **Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque and Dakar**. Their people were French citizens. In **1914** **Blaise Diagne** of Gorée became the **first Black African deputy** in the French National Assembly.

**Becoming a citizen:** outside the Four Communes, an African could become an **évolué** ("evolved" person) and apply for citizenship if he could **speak and write French**, had a **French education** or had served France (for example in the army), had **one wife**, and gave up customary law. Very few did.

## From assimilation to association

France turned away from full assimilation in the early twentieth century because:

1. It was **too costly** to educate and give rights to millions of Africans.
2. French people feared Africans would **outnumber** them in parliament.
3. **Muslims** and traditional societies **resisted** giving up their religion and customs.
4. Racist ideas held that Africans could not become "French".
5. Colonies existed mainly to supply **raw materials and markets**, not to create citizens.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Association** was the later French policy of ruling colonised peoples **through French administration** while **respecting some local customs**, and treating them as partners in developing the colony for France's benefit, without giving them citizenship.
:::

## How French West Africa was governed

![British indirect rule and French direct rule compared: Governor, Resident or District Commissioner, Native Authority of traditional chiefs, district and village heads, the people; and Governor-General at Dakar, Lieutenant-Governor, commandant de cercle, canton and village chiefs, the people](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/colonial-rule-compared.svg)

*Figure 1. Chains of authority under British indirect rule and French direct rule. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Level | Official | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Federation (AOF)** | **Governor-General** at Dakar | head of all French West Africa; answerable to the Minister of Colonies in Paris |
| **Colony** | **Lieutenant-Governor** | ran each colony, such as Guinea or Dahomey |
| **Cercle (district)** | **Commandant de cercle** | French officer with wide powers over tax, labour and justice |
| **Canton** | **chef de canton** | appointed African chief, often not of royal family |
| **Village** | **chef de village** | collected taxes and supplied labour |

Chiefs were **civil servants**: they could be appointed, moved or dismissed, and often had **no traditional claim** to rule.

## Citizens, subjects and the indigénat

| Citizens | Subjects (sujets) |
|---|---|
| people of the **Four Communes** and the few **évolués** | the great majority of Africans |
| French law and courts | **customary law** and the **indigénat** |
| could vote and be elected | no political rights |
| exempt from forced labour | **forced labour** (corvée) and military conscription |

:::callout{type=definition}
The **indigénat** was a code that allowed French officials to **punish subjects without trial**, with fines or imprisonment, for offences such as disrespect or failure to pay tax.
:::

Subjects also had to perform **forced labour** on roads, railways and plantations. Both were abolished only in **1946**.

## French and British systems compared

| Point | British indirect rule | French direct rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional rulers** | kept and used as **Native Authorities** | reduced to **appointed agents** of the administration |
| **Aim** | separate development "along their own lines" | assimilation, later association with France |
| **Culture** | local languages and customs respected | **French language and culture** promoted |
| **Centralisation** | each colony largely separate | **federation** (AOF) centralised at Dakar |
| **Representation** | Legislative Councils in each colony | deputies in the **French parliament** (from the Four Communes) |
| **Educated elite** | often excluded from government | évolués could become citizens and officials |
| **Forced labour** | used, but less systematically | widespread (**corvée**) until 1946 |

## Other colonial powers

- **Portugal** (Guinea-Bissau, Angola, Mozambique) also had an **assimilado** policy, but very few Africans qualified, and forced labour was harsh.
- **Belgium** in the Congo followed **paternalism**: it provided primary education, health care and jobs but **no political rights** or higher education.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's neighbour **Guinea** was a French colony, so families living across the border experienced two systems. In Guinea, chiefs were appointed agents who collected tax and labour for French commandants; in the Sierra Leone Protectorate, **paramount chiefs** kept more traditional authority under **District Commissioners**. These differences shaped the **French** and **English** languages and the different school and legal systems the two countries have today.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Assimilation was **not** applied to all French colonies. It worked fully only in the **Four Communes of Senegal**. Elsewhere, most Africans remained **subjects** without citizenship. Do not write that "all Africans in French colonies became French citizens".
:::

## Key points

- **Assimilation:** make Africans **French citizens** in culture, language and law; fully applied only in the **Four Communes** (Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque, Dakar).
- **Association** replaced it: French rule respecting some customs, without citizenship.
- **AOF (1895)**, capital **Dakar** (from 1902): Governor-General → Lieutenant-Governors → **commandants de cercle** → appointed chiefs.
- Most Africans were **subjects** under the **indigénat** and **forced labour**, abolished in **1946**.
- French chiefs were **agents**; British chiefs kept more **traditional authority**.
