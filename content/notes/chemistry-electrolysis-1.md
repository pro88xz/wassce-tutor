Aluminium for cooking pots, chlorine for treating water, the shiny chrome on a motorbike and the pure copper in electric wire all depend on **electrolysis**: using electricity to drive a chemical change. This lesson explains how electrolysis works and how to predict the products at each electrode.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define electrolysis, electrolyte, non-electrolyte, anode and cathode
- distinguish strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes and non-electrolytes
- explain the movement of ions and the reactions at each electrode
- explain the factors that decide which ion is discharged
- predict and explain the products of electrolysis of common electrolytes

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Electrolysis**: the **decomposition** of an electrolyte by passing a **direct current** through it.
- **Electrolyte**: a compound that **conducts electricity when molten or in aqueous solution** and is decomposed by it. Its **ions** carry the current.
- **Non-electrolyte**: a substance that does not conduct in any state (sugar, ethanol, kerosene).
- **Anode**: the electrode joined to the **positive** terminal. **Anions** move to it.
- **Cathode**: the electrode joined to the **negative** terminal. **Cations** move to it.
:::

| Type | Ionisation | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong electrolyte** | Completely ionised | Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄), alkalis (NaOH), most salts (NaCl, CuSO₄) |
| **Weak electrolyte** | Partly ionised; conducts poorly | Ethanoic acid, aqueous ammonia, water |
| **Non-electrolyte** | No ions | Sugar solution, ethanol, kerosene |

**Metallic conduction** is a flow of **electrons**; the metal is not changed. **Electrolytic conduction** is a flow of **ions**, and chemical changes happen at the electrodes.

## How electrolysis works

![An electrolytic cell: cations move to the cathode and gain electrons (reduction); anions move to the anode and lose electrons (oxidation); electrons flow in the wire from anode to cathode](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/electrolysis-cell.svg)

*Figure 1. Ions move to the oppositely charged electrodes. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **At the cathode:** cations **gain electrons**, which is **reduction**: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
- **At the anode:** anions **lose electrons**, which is **oxidation**: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

**Inert electrodes** (graphite or platinum) only carry the current. **Active electrodes** (such as copper) can themselves take part in the reaction.

## Which ion is discharged? (preferential discharge)

In aqueous solutions there are **several ions** at each electrode, including H⁺ and OH⁻ from water. Which one is discharged depends on three factors.

**1. Position in the electrochemical series.** Ions lower in the series are discharged more easily.

| Cations (at the cathode) | Anions (at the anode) |
|---|---|
| K⁺, Ca²⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺, **H⁺**, Cu²⁺, Ag⁺ | SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, **OH⁻**, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ |
| ← harder to discharge … easier to discharge → | ← harder to discharge … easier to discharge → |

So, in dilute solutions, metals above hydrogen (Na, K, Ca, Mg) are **not** discharged; **hydrogen** is given off instead. Sulphate and nitrate are not discharged; **OH⁻ gives oxygen** instead.

**2. Concentration.** A much more **concentrated** ion can be discharged in preference. In **concentrated** sodium chloride solution, **Cl⁻** is discharged (giving chlorine) rather than OH⁻.

**3. Nature of the electrode.** With **copper electrodes**, the copper **anode itself dissolves** instead of any anion being discharged.

## Products of common electrolyses

| Electrolyte (electrodes) | At the cathode | At the anode | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Molten lead(II) bromide** (graphite) | Lead: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | Bromine: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ | — |
| **Acidified water** (platinum) | Hydrogen: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | Oxygen: 4OH⁻ → 2H₂O + O₂ + 4e⁻ | Acid becomes more concentrated |
| **Dilute NaCl** (graphite) | Hydrogen | Oxygen | Effectively water is electrolysed |
| **Concentrated NaCl (brine)** (graphite) | Hydrogen | **Chlorine** | **Sodium hydroxide** solution |
| **CuSO₄** (graphite or platinum) | **Copper** deposited | Oxygen | Solution becomes **acidic** (H₂SO₄); blue colour fades |
| **CuSO₄** (copper electrodes) | **Copper** deposited | **Copper anode dissolves**: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ | Colour unchanged: concentration stays the same |

![A Hofmann voltameter: hydrogen collects above the cathode and oxygen above the anode, in a 2 : 1 ratio by volume](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/hofmann-voltameter.svg)

*Figure 2. Electrolysis of acidified water gives hydrogen and oxygen in a 2 : 1 ratio. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why do dilute and concentrated sodium chloride give different gases at the anode?**

Both solutions contain Cl⁻ and OH⁻ ions at the anode.

- In **dilute** solution, OH⁻ is lower in the series and is discharged: **oxygen** forms.
- In **concentrated** solution, Cl⁻ ions greatly outnumber OH⁻, so **Cl⁻** is discharged: **chlorine** forms.

At the cathode, H⁺ (from water) is discharged in both, not Na⁺, because sodium is far above hydrogen in the series. Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions are left behind as sodium hydroxide.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many motorbike and car parts sold in Freetown are **chrome- or nickel-plated** to stop rust and give a shine, using electrolysis. Even the zinc coating on galvanised roofing sheets can be applied by electrolysis (electrogalvanising) as well as by hot-dipping.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Cations go to the cathode** (negative) and **anions go to the anode** (positive). Remember: **reduction at the cathode**, **oxidation at the anode** (both "c" and both vowels).
:::

## Key points

- **Electrolysis** decomposes an electrolyte using d.c.; **ions** carry the current.
- **Anode (+):** anions are **oxidised** (lose electrons). **Cathode (−):** cations are **reduced** (gain electrons).
- Which ion is discharged depends on **position in the series**, **concentration** and the **electrode**.
- Products: **acidified water** → H₂ and O₂ (2 : 1); **brine** → H₂, Cl₂ and NaOH; **CuSO₄** with inert electrodes → Cu and O₂; with copper electrodes → copper transferred from anode to cathode.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define electrolysis and the electrodes**, to **predict and explain products** at each electrode (with half-equations) for molten salts, acidified water, brine and copper(II) sulphate, and to **explain preferential discharge**. Many questions compare inert and copper electrodes in CuSO₄.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is an electrolyte?
2. Name the products at each electrode when molten lead(II) bromide is electrolysed.
3. Why is sodium not formed at the cathode when sodium chloride solution is electrolysed?
4. What happens to the colour of copper(II) sulphate solution when it is electrolysed with graphite electrodes? Why?

**Answers:**

1. A compound that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved, and is decomposed by it.
2. Lead at the cathode; bromine at the anode.
3. H⁺ ions are lower in the electrochemical series than Na⁺, so they are discharged more easily; hydrogen forms instead.
4. The blue colour fades, because Cu²⁺ ions are removed as copper at the cathode and are not replaced.
