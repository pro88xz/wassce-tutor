Why does a fish have gills and not lungs? Why do mangrove trees grow strange roots that stick up out of the mud? Why is a chameleon hard to see on a branch? Each of these is an **adaptation**: a feature that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its particular habitat. Adaptations are produced over many generations by natural selection, and studying them shows how closely living things are fitted to their surroundings. This lesson classifies adaptations, then examines them in plants of dry, wet and salty places, and in animals of water, land, trees and air, as well as parasites and the use of colour.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define adaptation and distinguish structural, physiological and behavioural adaptations
- describe adaptations of xerophytes, hydrophytes, mesophytes and halophytes
- describe adaptations of aquatic, arboreal, burrowing and flying animals
- explain camouflage, warning colours and mimicry
- describe the adaptations of parasites

## What is an adaptation?

:::callout{type=definition}
An **adaptation** is an inherited feature that makes an organism better suited to survive and reproduce in its environment.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **structural** (morphological) | a body feature | the fish's streamlined shape; the cactus's spines |
| **physiological** | a body process | mangroves excreting salt; desert animals producing very concentrated urine |
| **behavioural** | a way of acting | lizards basking in the morning sun; birds migrating; animals hiding by day |
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Saying an organism "adapts itself" in its lifetime.** Adaptations are **inherited** features that arose by natural selection over many generations. A goat taken to a dry area does not grow new kidneys; its population changes only over many generations.
:::

## Adaptations of plants

### Xerophytes: dry habitats

Plants of deserts and dry savanna (e.g. cactus, *Euphorbia*, some grasses) must **reduce water loss**:

- thick, **waxy cuticle**; **few** stomata, often **sunken** in pits or on the lower surface
- leaves reduced to **spines** (less surface for transpiration; also protect against grazers)
- **succulent** stems or leaves that store water
- **long tap roots** or wide, shallow roots to collect water
- leaves that **roll** up in dry weather, trapping moist air
- hairy leaves that trap a layer of moist air

### Hydrophytes: in water

Water plants (e.g. water lily, water lettuce) have plenty of water but little oxygen and support:

- large **air spaces** (aerenchyma) for buoyancy and to store oxygen
- **stomata on the upper surface** of floating leaves, facing the air
- thin or absent cuticle; **little supporting tissue**, since the water supports them
- reduced roots; finely divided submerged leaves that resist currents

### Mesophytes

Most crops and garden plants (maize, cassava, hibiscus) live where water is moderate. They have broad leaves, stomata mainly on the lower surface, and close their stomata in hot, dry weather.

### Halophytes: salty habitats

![Mangroves on salty, airless mud: a red mangrove propped on arching stilt roots with a seedling growing on the parent tree, and a white mangrove with pencil-like breathing roots (pneumatophores) rising from the mud](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/mangrove-adaptations.svg)

**Mangroves**, on the coasts and estuaries of Sierra Leone, live in salty, waterlogged, soft mud with little oxygen:

- **stilt (prop) roots** support the tree in soft mud (red mangrove, *Rhizophora*)
- **pneumatophores** (breathing roots) grow upwards out of the mud to take in air at low tide (white mangrove, *Avicennia*)
- **salt glands** excrete excess salt; thick, waxy leaves reduce water loss
- **vivipary**: the seed germinates while still on the parent tree, and the seedling drops ready to root quickly in the mud

## Adaptations of animals

### Aquatic animals (fish)

- **streamlined** body to reduce water resistance
- **gills** with a large surface and many capillaries to absorb dissolved oxygen
- **fins** for balance and steering; a muscular **tail** for propulsion
- **scales** and mucus for protection and smooth movement
- **swim bladder** to control buoyancy
- **lateral line** to detect vibrations and movements in the water

### Arboreal animals (in trees: monkeys, chimpanzees)

- **grasping hands and feet** with opposable thumbs
- long arms; some monkeys have **prehensile tails** that grip branches
- **forward-facing eyes** with stereoscopic vision to judge distances when leaping

### Burrowing animals (earthworm)

- **streamlined, pointed** body with no projections
- **chaetae** (bristles) to grip the soil
- **mucus** to ease movement and allow gas exchange through the moist skin

### Flying animals (birds)

- **feathers** and wings; **hollow, light bones** with air sacs
- **streamlined** body; powerful **flight muscles** attached to a large **keel** on the breastbone
- **no teeth** (a light beak instead); high body temperature and rapid respiration

### Desert animals (camel)

- stores **fat** in the hump (energy, and water released when the fat is respired)
- produces **concentrated urine** and dry faeces; can tolerate a big rise in body temperature
- broad, padded feet for sand; long eyelashes and closable nostrils against sand

## Colour: camouflage, warning and mimicry

| Adaptation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **camouflage** (cryptic colouring) | colour and shape blend with the surroundings | chameleon changing colour; stick insects; grasshoppers coloured like grass |
| **warning colouration** | bright colours warn that the animal is poisonous or stings | wasps and bees (yellow and black); some brightly coloured frogs |
| **mimicry** | a harmless species looks like a harmful one | hoverflies that look like wasps |
| **countershading** | dark back, pale belly, making the animal look flat and harder to see | many fish |

## Adaptations of parasites

Parasites live on or in a host, so they are adapted to hold on, feed and reproduce there:

| Feature | Tapeworm (gut) | *Plasmodium* (malaria) |
|---|---|---|
| attachment | **hooks and suckers** on the scolex | lives inside liver and red blood cells |
| feeding | absorbs digested food **through its body surface**; no gut | feeds on cell contents |
| protection | thick **cuticle** resists the host's enzymes | hides inside cells from the immune system |
| reproduction | each segment has male and female organs; produces **huge numbers** of eggs | multiplies rapidly; uses a **vector** (the female *Anopheles* mosquito) |

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Mudskippers in the mangroves.** On the mudflats of the Sierra Leone River estuary you can see mudskippers: fish that leave the water at low tide. They "walk" on strong front fins, keep water in their gill chambers, take in oxygen through their moist skin and mouth lining, and have eyes high on the head to look around above the mud. They show that adaptations fit an animal to a very particular way of life.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Give three adaptations of xerophytes that reduce water loss."}
Any three: thick waxy cuticle; few or sunken stomata; leaves reduced to spines; rolled leaves; hairy leaves.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Why do water lilies have stomata on the upper surface of their leaves?"}
The lower surface rests on the water, so only the **upper surface** is in contact with the air for gas exchange.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Explain two ways mangroves are adapted to waterlogged mud."}
Any two: **pneumatophores** grow above the mud to take in air; **stilt roots** support the tree in soft mud; **vivipary** lets seedlings root quickly.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. State three adaptations of a bird for flight."}
Any three: feathers and wings; hollow light bones; streamlined body; large keel for flight muscles; no teeth.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. How is a tapeworm adapted to life in the human gut?"}
Hooks and suckers for attachment; absorbs digested food through its surface (no gut needed); thick cuticle resists digestive enzymes; produces huge numbers of eggs.
:::

## Key points

- An **adaptation** is an inherited feature that helps survival and reproduction: **structural, physiological or behavioural**.
- **Xerophytes** reduce water loss; **hydrophytes** have air spaces and upper stomata; **halophytes** (mangroves) have stilt roots, pneumatophores, salt glands and vivipary.
- **Fish**: streamlined, gills, fins, swim bladder, lateral line. **Birds**: feathers, hollow bones, keel. **Arboreal**: grasping hands, prehensile tails, stereoscopic vision.
- **Camouflage, warning colours, mimicry**.
- **Parasites**: attachment, absorption, protection, huge reproduction.

Give each adaptation **with its function**: "sunken stomata **reduce water loss**", not just "sunken stomata".
