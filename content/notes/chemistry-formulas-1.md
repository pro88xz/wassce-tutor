How do chemists know that water is H₂O and rust contains Fe₂O₃? Not by looking at the molecules, but by **measuring masses** and turning them into a **mole ratio**. The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound is its **empirical formula**. This lesson shows how to find it from percentages and from experimental masses.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the empirical formula of a compound
- calculate an empirical formula from percentage composition
- calculate an empirical formula from experimental masses
- handle mole ratios that are not whole numbers
- describe an experiment to find the formula of magnesium oxide

## What an empirical formula is

:::callout{type=definition}
The **empirical formula** of a compound is the **simplest whole-number ratio** of the atoms of each element in it.
:::

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃ |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO |
| Benzene | C₆H₆ | CH |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O |

For ionic compounds such as NaCl and MgO, the formula is always an empirical formula, because there are no molecules.

## The method

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Write the **mass or percentage** of each element |
| 2 | **Divide** each by its **relative atomic mass**, giving moles |
| 3 | **Divide** every answer by the **smallest** one |
| 4 | If needed, **multiply** to get whole numbers |
| 5 | Write the formula |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen. Find its empirical formula.** (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16)

| | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| % (or mass in 100 g) | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| ÷ Aᵣ | 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33 | 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7 | 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 |
| ÷ smallest (3.33) | 1 | 2.0 | 1 |

Empirical formula: **CH₂O**.
:::

## Ratios that are not whole numbers

If the ratio comes out as about **1.5**, multiply everything by **2**; about **1.33** or **1.67**, multiply by **3**; about **1.25**, multiply by **4**. A value such as 1.98 or 2.03 is just experimental error: round it to 2.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**An oxide of iron contains 70% iron and 30% oxygen. Find its empirical formula.** (Fe = 56, O = 16)

| | Fe | O |
|---|---|---|
| % | 70 | 30 |
| ÷ Aᵣ | 70 ÷ 56 = 1.25 | 30 ÷ 16 = 1.875 |
| ÷ smallest (1.25) | 1 | 1.5 |
| × 2 | 2 | 3 |

Empirical formula: **Fe₂O₃** (iron(III) oxide, the main part of rust and of iron ore).
:::

## From experimental masses

You can use masses directly instead of percentages.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**2.8 g of iron combines completely with 1.6 g of sulphur. Find the formula of the product.** (Fe = 56, S = 32)

- Fe: $2.8 \div 56 = 0.05$ mol
- S: $1.6 \div 32 = 0.05$ mol
- Ratio 1 : 1, so the formula is **FeS**.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen.**

- C: $85.7 \div 12 = 7.14$; H: $14.3 \div 1 = 14.3$
- Ratio: $7.14 \div 7.14 = 1$; $14.3 \div 7.14 = 2.0$

Empirical formula: **CH₂** (the empirical formula of all alkenes).
:::

## Experiment: the formula of magnesium oxide

![Magnesium heated in a crucible with a lid on a pipe-clay triangle; masses before and after heating give 0.24 g magnesium and 0.16 g oxygen, a 1 : 1 mole ratio](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/crucible-mgo.svg)

*Figure 1. Finding the formula of magnesium oxide by heating magnesium. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Method:**

1. Weigh a clean, dry crucible and lid.
2. Add a coil of cleaned magnesium ribbon and weigh again.
3. Heat strongly, **lifting the lid slightly from time to time** to let air in while keeping the white smoke of magnesium oxide inside.
4. When the magnesium stops glowing, heat strongly for a few more minutes, then cool and weigh.
5. **Repeat heating, cooling and weighing until the mass is constant** (heating to constant mass), to be sure all the magnesium has reacted.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Results:** crucible + lid = 20.10 g; with magnesium = 20.34 g; with magnesium oxide = 20.50 g.

- Mass of magnesium $= 20.34 - 20.10 = 0.24$ g → $0.24 \div 24 = 0.010$ mol
- Mass of oxygen $= 20.50 - 20.34 = 0.16$ g → $0.16 \div 16 = 0.010$ mol
- Ratio 1 : 1, so the formula is **MgO**.
:::

**Sources of error:** some magnesium oxide smoke escapes when the lid is lifted (making the oxygen mass too low); not all the magnesium reacts; some magnesium reacts with nitrogen in the air to form magnesium nitride.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Iron ore mined at Tonkolili is mainly **haematite**, Fe₂O₃. Using the empirical formula, 112 g of iron is combined with 48 g of oxygen in every 160 g of pure haematite, so pure ore is **70% iron** by mass. Mining companies use exactly this kind of calculation to judge the grade of their ore.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not round a ratio such as 1 : 1.5 to 1 : 2. That would give FeO₂ instead of Fe₂O₃. Multiply to the nearest whole-number ratio instead.
:::

## Key points

- The **empirical formula** is the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms in a compound.
- Method: **mass or % → ÷ Aᵣ → ÷ smallest → whole numbers**.
- Ratios near **1.5, 1.33 or 1.25** must be multiplied by **2, 3 or 4**.
- The magnesium oxide experiment needs heating to **constant mass**, lifting the lid to let in air.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Every year WASSCE asks for an **empirical formula from percentage composition or masses**. Set out the calculation in a table, show the ÷ Aᵣ and ÷ smallest steps, and watch for ratios that need multiplying. Practical questions may ask why the crucible is heated to constant mass.
:::

## Quick check

1. Define empirical formula.
2. A compound contains 52.2% C, 13.0% H and 34.8% O. Find its empirical formula.
3. 0.48 g of magnesium forms 0.80 g of oxide. Find the formula of the oxide.
4. Why is the crucible heated to constant mass?

**Answers:**

1. The simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound.
2. C 4.35, H 13.0, O 2.175 → 2 : 6 : 1, so C₂H₆O.
3. Mg 0.02 mol; O $0.32 \div 16 = 0.02$ mol; 1 : 1, so MgO.
4. To make sure all the magnesium has reacted.
