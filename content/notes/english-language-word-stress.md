Say *"PREsent"* and you are talking about a gift. Say *"preSENT"* and you are giving it. In English, the syllable you stress can change a word's meaning, its word class, or simply whether a listener understands you at all. WASSCE Oral English tests word stress every year, usually by asking you to choose the word that is stressed on a given syllable. Stress feels unpredictable, but most words follow a small set of patterns. This lesson teaches those patterns, the exceptions you must know, and the words Sierra Leonean speakers most often stress differently.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain what word stress is and how to hear it
- count syllables accurately
- apply the main patterns for two-syllable words, suffixes and compounds
- recognise noun–verb pairs that change stress
- answer stress-pattern questions in the WAEC format

## What stress is

:::callout{type=definition}
**Word stress** is the extra force given to one syllable in a word. The stressed syllable is **louder**, **longer** and often **higher** in pitch, and its vowel is said clearly. Unstressed syllables are weaker, and their vowels are often reduced to the schwa /ə/.

In dictionaries, stress is marked with ˈ before the stressed syllable: /ˈteɪbl/ *table*, /bɪˈɡɪn/ *begin*. In this lesson stressed syllables are also written in CAPITALS: TAble, beGIN.
:::

### Counting syllables

A syllable is a beat built around a vowel **sound**. Clap the beats:

| Word | Syllables | Count |
|---|---|---|
| school | school | 1 |
| teacher | TEA-cher | 2 |
| tomorrow | to-MOR-row | 3 |
| education | ed-u-CA-tion | 4 |
| responsibility | re-spon-si-BIL-i-ty | 6 |

:::callout{type=tip}
To find the stressed syllable, say the word several times, each time stressing a different syllable. The right one sounds natural; the others sound strange: *TOmorrow?* *toMORrow?* *tomorROW?* Only *toMORrow* sounds right.
:::

## Pattern 2: noun–verb pairs

Many two-syllable words are spelt the same as a noun and a verb, and the **stress tells you which**:

| Noun (stress 1st) | Verb (stress 2nd) |
|---|---|
| a **PRE**sent (a gift) | to pre**SENT** (to give formally) |
| a **RE**cord | to re**CORD** |
| an **IM**port | to im**PORT** |
| an **EX**port | to ex**PORT** |
| a **CON**duct (behaviour) | to con**DUCT** |
| a **CON**test | to con**TEST** |
| an **OB**ject | to ob**JECT** |
| a **PRO**test | to pro**TEST** |
| a **RE**bel | to re**BEL** |
| a **SUS**pect | to sus**PECT** |
| a **PER**mit | to per**MIT** |
| an **IN**crease | to in**CREASE** |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
*"The government will in**CREASE** the price of rice." / "There has been an **IN**crease in the price of rice."*

Same spelling, same letters, but the first is a **verb** (stress on *-crease*) and the second is a **noun** (stress on *In-*).
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Keeping the stress of the root word.** *PHOtograph* stresses the first syllable, but *phoTOgraphy* and *photoGRAPHic* move the stress. *POlitics* becomes *poLItical* and *poliTIcian*. When a suffix is added, check the stress again.
:::

## Pattern 4: compound words

| Type | Stress | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Compound nouns** | the **first** part | **BLACK**board, **CLASS**room, **FOOT**ball, **AIR**port, **SUN**glasses, **TOOTH**brush |
| **Compound adjectives** | often the **second** part | old-**FASH**ioned, bad-**TEM**pered, well-**KNOWN** |
| **Phrasal verbs** | usually the **particle** | wake **UP**, give **IN**, turn **OFF** |

**Listen:** [blackboard /ˈblækbɔːd/](https://wasscetutor.com/audio/oral/blackboard.mp3?v=d3001790d5) [classroom /ˈklɑːsruːm/](https://wasscetutor.com/audio/oral/classroom.mp3?v=82f278cdcb) [football /ˈfʊtbɔːl/](https://wasscetutor.com/audio/oral/football.mp3?v=35f60bba65)

:::callout{type=tip}
Compare *a **BLACK**board* (the thing teachers write on) with *a black **BOARD*** (any board that happens to be black). In compound nouns the first part carries the stress, and that is how a listener knows it is one word.
:::

## How the questions work

WAEC usually gives a word with its syllables shown and asks where the stress falls, or gives four words and asks which one is stressed on a particular syllable:

> *Choose the word that is stressed on the **second** syllable: A. MARket B. PENcil C. deCIDE D. VILlage*

**Answer: C. deCIDE.**

> *Which of the following is stressed on the **third** syllable? A. inFORmation B. inforMAtion C. INformation D. informaTION*

**Answer: B. inforMAtion** (*-tion* puts the stress on the syllable just before).

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Which word is stressed on the first syllable? A. arrive B. pencil C. forget D. decide"}
**B. PENcil**: a two-syllable noun. The others are verbs stressed on the second syllable.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. In 'They will EXport cocoa', is the stress correct?"}
**No.** Here *export* is a **verb**, so the stress falls on the second syllable: *They will ex**PORT** cocoa.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Where is the stress in 'university'?"}
uni**VER**sity: the syllable just before **-ity**.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Which word is stressed on the last syllable? A. teacher B. engineer C. doctor D. lawyer"}
**B. engineer** (engi**NEER**): the ending *-eer* is stressed.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Mark the stress: photograph, photographer, photographic"}
**PHO**tograph, pho**TO**grapher, photo**GRA**phic.
:::

:::reveal{title="6. Which is the compound noun: 'a GREENhouse' or 'a green HOUSE'?"}
**a GREENhouse** (a glass building for growing plants). *A green HOUSE* is simply a house that is green.
:::

## Key points

- **Stress** makes a syllable louder, longer and clearer; unstressed vowels often weaken to /ə/.
- **Two-syllable** nouns and adjectives usually stress the **first** syllable; verbs often the **second**.
- **Noun–verb pairs** change stress: **PRE**sent (noun), pre**SENT** (verb).
- **-tion, -ic, -ity, -ical, -ian**: stress the syllable **before**. **-ee, -eer, -ese, -ique**: stress the **ending**.
- **Compound nouns** stress the **first** part.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect several questions asking which word is stressed on the **first, second or third syllable**, or which word has a given **stress pattern**. Noun–verb pairs, suffix patterns and words commonly mis-stressed in West African English appear most often. Say each word in your head with different stresses until one sounds right.
:::
