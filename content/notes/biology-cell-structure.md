# Cell structure: the organisation of plant and animal cells

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to define a cell; identify major structures in typical plant and animal cells; explain the functions of common organelles; compare plant and animal cells; and relate specialised cell structures to their functions.

## Introduction

A cell is the smallest unit of an organism that can carry out the processes associated with life. Organisms may consist of one cell, as in many bacteria and some protozoans, or many cells working together, as in flowering plants and mammals. In multicellular organisms, cells become specialised. Groups of similar cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work together in systems.

Most cells are too small to be seen clearly with the unaided eye. A light microscope makes their general shape and some internal structures visible. Cell theory states that living organisms are made of one or more cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure and function, and that new cells arise from pre-existing cells through cell division.

## The basic organisation of a cell

The cell surface membrane surrounds the cytoplasm and controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell. It is selectively permeable: some substances cross more readily than others. This regulation helps maintain suitable internal conditions.

The cytoplasm is the material between the cell membrane and the nucleus in a typical animal cell. It contains water, dissolved substances and structures called organelles. Many chemical reactions take place in the cytoplasm. The nucleus contains genetic material, mainly DNA, and helps control cell activities by regulating gene expression. The nuclear envelope separates the nucleus from the cytoplasm and contains pores through which selected materials pass.

Mitochondria are sites of aerobic respiration, the process by which energy is released from food molecules and transferred into ATP for cellular activities. Cells with high energy requirements, such as many muscle cells, commonly contain numerous mitochondria. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis. They may occur freely in the cytoplasm or be attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum.

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes. Rough endoplasmic reticulum has ribosomes and is involved in the production and transport of proteins. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum lacks attached ribosomes and participates in lipid synthesis and other functions. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids into vesicles for transport. Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes that break down certain materials and worn-out cell components; they are especially characteristic of many animal cells.

## Structures characteristic of plant cells

A typical plant cell has a cellulose cell wall outside its cell membrane. The wall provides support, protection and a more regular shape. It is freely permeable to many small substances, unlike the selectively permeable cell membrane.

Many plant cells contain a large permanent vacuole filled with cell sap. The vacuole is surrounded by a membrane called the tonoplast. Water entering the vacuole helps maintain turgor pressure, which supports non-woody plant tissues. When plant cells lose water, they may become flaccid; severe water loss can cause the cell membrane to pull away from the cell wall, a condition called plasmolysis.

Photosynthetic plant cells commonly contain chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll in internal membrane systems and absorb light energy used in photosynthesis. Not every plant cell contains chloroplasts: many cells in roots that grow underground are not photosynthetic and normally lack them.

## Comparing plant and animal cells

Both typical plant and animal cells are eukaryotic. They have a nucleus, cytoplasm, a cell surface membrane, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and a Golgi apparatus. Plant cells commonly have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues and a large permanent vacuole. Animal cells do not have a cellulose cell wall or chloroplasts and generally have smaller temporary vacuoles when present.

| Feature | Typical plant cell | Typical animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell surface membrane | Present | Present |
| Nucleus | Present in most living cells | Present in most cells |
| Cell wall | Cellulose wall present | Absent |
| Chloroplasts | Present in photosynthetic cells | Absent |
| Large permanent vacuole | Common | Not typical |
| Shape | Often more regular because of the wall | Often more flexible or irregular |

These are general patterns, not absolute rules for every cell. Mature mammalian red blood cells, for example, lack a nucleus, while some plant cells lose their living contents at maturity. In a comparison answer, avoid claiming that every cell in a group has every feature.

## Specialised cells and structure-function relationships

A root hair cell has a long projection that increases the surface area available for absorbing water and mineral ions from the soil. Its thin wall provides a short pathway for water movement. A red blood cell has a biconcave shape that gives a relatively large surface area for gas exchange; in mammals it lacks a nucleus when mature, leaving more space for haemoglobin. A sperm cell has a flagellum for movement and many mitochondria in its middle region to supply energy for swimming. A palisade mesophyll cell contains many chloroplasts and is positioned near the upper surface of a leaf, where it can receive light.

The key principle is that cell structure is related to the role the cell performs. In examination answers, identify the feature and explain how it helps the cell carry out its function.

## WASSCE examination focus

You should be able to draw and label typical plant and animal cells, identify organelles from diagrams, and compare the two cell types. Practise giving a function for each labelled structure rather than listing names alone.

For a question such as “State three differences between a plant cell and an animal cell”, give three distinct paired comparisons. For a question asking for the function of mitochondria, state that they are the site of aerobic respiration and explain that respiration supplies energy in the form of ATP. Do not write that mitochondria “make energy”; energy is transferred from food molecules into ATP.

When drawing biological diagrams, use a sharp pencil, clear continuous label lines that do not cross, and a suitable title. Make the drawing large enough for labels to be read. Do not shade diagrams unless the question specifically requests it.

## Check your understanding

1. State the meaning of the term cell.
2. Name the structure that controls the movement of substances into and out of a cell.
3. Explain why a muscle cell may contain many mitochondria.
4. Give two structures found in a typical plant cell but not in a typical animal cell.
5. Explain how the structure of a root hair cell helps absorption.
6. Distinguish between the cell wall and the cell surface membrane.

## Answers for self-assessment

1. A cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of a living organism.
2. The cell surface membrane.
3. Muscle cells require substantial energy for contraction; mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration, supplying ATP.
4. A cellulose cell wall and chloroplasts in photosynthetic plant cells; a large permanent vacuole is also characteristic of many plant cells.
5. Its long projection increases surface area for absorption, and its thin wall provides a short pathway for water movement.
6. The cell wall is a supportive, freely permeable outer layer in plant cells; the cell surface membrane is selectively permeable and regulates movement into and out of the cell.
