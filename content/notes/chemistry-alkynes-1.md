A welder cutting steel uses a flame hot enough to melt it: around 3,000 °C. The fuel is **ethyne** (acetylene), the simplest **alkyne**, burned in pure oxygen. Alkynes contain a carbon–carbon **triple bond**. This lesson covers their structure and the preparation and properties of ethyne.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the general formula of the alkynes and name the first members
- describe the structure and shape of the C≡C triple bond
- describe the laboratory preparation of ethyne from calcium carbide
- explain how calcium carbide is manufactured
- describe the physical properties and combustion of ethyne
- state the uses of ethyne

## Structure and names

:::callout{type=definition}
**Alkynes** are **unsaturated hydrocarbons** that contain **one carbon–carbon triple bond (C≡C)**. General formula: **CₙH₂ₙ₋₂**.
:::

| Name | Molecular formula | Condensed formula | Boiling point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ethyne** (acetylene) | C₂H₂ | HC≡CH | −84 °C (sublimes) |
| **Propyne** | C₃H₄ | CH₃C≡CH | −23 °C |
| **But-1-yne** | C₄H₆ | HC≡CCH₂CH₃ | 8 °C |
| **But-2-yne** | C₄H₆ | CH₃C≡CCH₃ | 27 °C |

**The triple bond** is made of **one σ bond and two π bonds**. The two π bonds make alkynes highly **unsaturated**: they can add **two** molecules of hydrogen or bromine.

**Shape.** The two carbons of the triple bond and the atoms attached to them lie in a **straight line**: ethyne is a **linear** molecule with bond angles of **180°** (sp hybridisation).

The C≡C bond (0.120 nm) is **shorter and stronger** than C=C (0.134 nm), which is shorter and stronger than C–C (0.154 nm).

## Laboratory preparation of ethyne

![Water drips from a dropping funnel onto calcium carbide in a flask; ethyne is collected over water](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/ethyne-prep.svg)

*Figure 1. Preparing ethyne from calcium carbide and water. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Drip **water** from a dropping funnel onto lumps of **calcium carbide** (CaC₂) in a flask. **No heating** is needed:

$$\text{CaC}_2\text{(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

- Water is added **drop by drop** because the reaction is **vigorous and exothermic**.
- Ethyne is collected **over water**.
- Gas made from commercial carbide smells of garlic because of impurities such as **phosphine** (PH₃) and **hydrogen sulphide**; these can be removed by passing the gas through **acidified copper(II) sulphate** solution.
- Pure ethyne is **odourless**.

## Manufacture of calcium carbide

Calcium carbide is made by heating **calcium oxide (quicklime) with coke** in an **electric furnace** at about **2,000 °C**:

$$\text{CaO} + 3\text{C} \rightarrow \text{CaC}_2 + \text{CO}$$

The calcium oxide comes from heating limestone. Ethyne can also be made industrially by heating methane strongly for a very short time.

## Physical properties of ethyne

- Colourless gas, **odourless when pure**
- Slightly less dense than air
- Slightly soluble in water, very soluble in acetone (propanone)
- **Explosive under pressure**: it is stored in cylinders dissolved in propanone and soaked into a porous material

## Combustion

In plenty of oxygen, ethyne burns with an extremely hot flame:

$$2\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad \Delta H = -2600 \text{ kJ}$$

In **air**, ethyne burns with a **very luminous, very sooty flame**, because it has a very high proportion of carbon (92.3% by mass).

| Hydrocarbon | % carbon by mass | Flame in air |
|---|---|---|
| Ethane, C₂H₆ | 80.0% | Blue, non-luminous, clean |
| Ethene, C₂H₄ | 85.7% | Luminous, slightly smoky |
| Ethyne, C₂H₂ | 92.3% | Very luminous, very sooty |

## Uses of ethyne

- **Oxy-acetylene flame** (about 3,000 °C) for **welding and cutting** metals
- **Carbide lamps**: once used by miners and fishermen, burning ethyne from carbide and water
- Making **chloroethene** (for PVC), **ethanal** and **ethanoic acid**
- Ripening fruit (calcium carbide is misused for this; see below)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Volume of ethyne from carbide.** What volume of ethyne at s.t.p. is produced when 16 g of calcium carbide reacts with excess water? (CaC₂ = 64; molar volume = 22.4 dm³)

- Moles of CaC₂ $= 16 \div 64 = 0.25$ mol
- 1 mol CaC₂ gives 1 mol C₂H₂, so moles of ethyne $= 0.25$ mol
- Volume $= 0.25 \times 22.4 = 5.6$ dm³
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Some fruit sellers in West Africa use **calcium carbide** ("carbide") to ripen mangoes and bananas quickly. Moisture turns it into ethyne, which acts like ethene. But commercial carbide contains **arsenic and phosphorus** impurities, and its use on food is **banned** in many countries. Fruit ripened this way can look ripe outside but taste unripe inside. Natural ripening, or ripening with controlled ethene, is safer.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Ethyne is made from **calcium carbide**, CaC₂, not calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. Read the formula carefully.
:::

## Key points

- **Alkynes**, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, contain a **C≡C** triple bond (one σ and two π bonds) and are **linear** around it (**180°**).
- **Ethyne** is prepared by dripping **water onto calcium carbide**: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂; collected over water.
- **CaC₂** is made from **CaO and coke** in an electric furnace.
- Ethyne burns in air with a **very sooty flame** (92.3% C) and in oxygen at about **3,000 °C** (welding).
- It is stored dissolved in **propanone** because it explodes under pressure.

## Quick check

1. Write the general formula of the alkynes.
2. Write the equation for the preparation of ethyne.
3. What is the shape of the ethyne molecule, and what are its bond angles?
4. Why does ethyne burn with a sootier flame than ethane?

**Answers:**

1. CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
2. CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂.
3. Linear; 180°.
4. Ethyne has a much higher proportion of carbon (92.3% against 80%), so some carbon is not completely burned in air.
