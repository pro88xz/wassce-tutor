**Incomplete records** (single entry) describe the books of a business that does **not keep a full double-entry system**: often only a cash book, bank statements and a list of debts. Many small traders keep records this way. Profit can still be found by comparing the owner's **capital at the start and end of the year**, using a **statement of affairs**: a list of the business's assets and liabilities on a given date, with capital as the difference. This lesson explains incomplete records, the statement of affairs, and the capital comparison (net worth) method of finding profit.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and disadvantages of incomplete records
- distinguish a statement of affairs from a statement of financial position
- prepare opening and closing statements of affairs
- calculate profit by the capital comparison method
- adjust for drawings and capital introduced

## Incomplete records

:::callout{type=definition}
**Incomplete records** (single entry) are records in which transactions are **not** recorded on the double-entry system: usually only cash and personal accounts are kept, and nominal and real accounts are missing.
:::

**Disadvantages:**

1. A **trial balance** cannot be prepared, so there is no arithmetical check.
2. A full **income statement** cannot be drawn up directly.
3. **Errors and fraud** are hard to detect.
4. The figures may not satisfy **banks** or the **tax authority**.
5. **Assets** are not properly controlled.

This lesson covers **method 1**; the next lesson covers **method 2**.

## Statement of affairs

A **statement of affairs** lists the assets and liabilities on a given date to find the capital:

$$\text{Capital} = \text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities}$$

| Statement of affairs | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|
| prepared from **incomplete records** | prepared from a **full set** of double-entry books |
| asset values often **estimated** | values come from **ledger balances** |
| capital is the **balancing figure** | capital comes from the capital account |

## Capital comparison method

Profit increases capital; drawings reduce it; new capital put in increases it. So:

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Closing capital} - \text{Opening capital} + \text{Drawings} - \text{Capital introduced}$$

A negative answer is a **loss**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Fatu keeps a provisions shop with incomplete records. Find her profit for 2026 (NLe).**

| | 1 Jan 2026 | 31 Dec 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Shop equipment | 20,000 | 18,000 |
| Inventory | 12,000 | 15,000 |
| Trade receivables | 5,000 | 7,000 |
| Bank | 0 | 9,000 |
| Cash | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| **Total assets** | **40,000** | **51,000** |
| Trade payables | 4,000 | 6,000 |
| Loan | 0 | 5,000 |
| **Total liabilities** | **4,000** | **11,000** |
| **Capital** | **36,000** | **40,000** |

During the year Fatu took drawings of NLe 14,000 and put in NLe 3,000 she inherited.

Profit $= 40\,000 - 36\,000 + 14\,000 - 3\,000 = 15\,000$
:::

**Why add drawings?** They were taken out of profit, so the closing capital is lower than it would otherwise be. **Why deduct capital introduced?** It increased capital without being earned as profit.

## Limitations of the method

- It shows **only the total profit**, not sales, gross profit or expenses.
- It depends on **estimated values** of assets.
- It cannot show **where** profit was made or lost.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many traders in Sierra Leone's markets keep only a notebook of debts and a rough idea of their stock. At the end of a year, listing what the business owns and owes at the start and end, and adding back what the trader took out for the family, gives a fair estimate of the profit for a loan application or tax return.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Remember to **add drawings** and **deduct capital introduced**. Simply subtracting opening capital from closing capital gives the **increase in capital**, not the profit.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Statement of affairs**: a statement of a business's estimated assets and liabilities at a date, used to find its capital when full records are not kept.
:::

## Key points

- **Incomplete records:** no full double entry; no trial balance.
- **Statement of affairs:** assets − liabilities = capital, often with estimated values.
- **Profit = closing capital − opening capital + drawings − capital introduced.**
- The method gives **total profit only**, without detail.

## Quick check

1. What are incomplete records?
2. How is capital found in a statement of affairs?
3. Opening capital NLe 50,000; closing capital NLe 62,000; drawings NLe 9,000. Find the profit.
4. Why are drawings added back?
5. Give two disadvantages of incomplete records.

**Answers:**

1. Records not kept on the double-entry system, usually only cash and personal accounts.
2. Total assets minus total liabilities.
3. $62\,000 - 50\,000 + 9\,000 = 21\,000$: NLe 21,000.
4. Because they were taken out of the year's profit and reduced the closing capital.
5. Any two: no trial balance; no full income statement; errors and fraud hard to find; may not satisfy banks or tax authorities; poor control of assets.
