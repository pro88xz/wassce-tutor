Without friction you could not walk, a car could not brake, a nail would slide out of wood and a knot would come undone. Yet friction also wears out engines and wastes fuel. This lesson explains what friction is, how big it can get, and how to calculate it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define friction and distinguish static, limiting and kinetic (dynamic) friction
- state the laws of solid friction
- define and calculate the coefficient of friction
- solve problems involving friction on horizontal surfaces

## What is friction?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Friction** is the force that **opposes relative motion** (or the tendency to move) between two surfaces in contact. It acts **along** the surfaces, opposite to the direction of motion or attempted motion.
:::

Friction arises because even smooth-looking surfaces are rough on a microscopic scale. Their tiny bumps interlock and form temporary bonds where they touch.

## Static, limiting and kinetic friction

![Graph of friction force against applied force: friction rises equal to the applied force up to the limiting value, then drops slightly to a constant kinetic value once the block slides](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/friction-graph.svg)

*Figure 2. How friction responds as the pull increases. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Type | When | Size |
|---|---|---|
| **Static friction** | the body is at rest and a force tries to move it | adjusts itself to equal the applied force, up to a maximum |
| **Limiting friction** | the body is **just about to move** | the **maximum** value of static friction, $F = \mu_s R$ |
| **Kinetic (dynamic, sliding) friction** | the body is sliding | roughly constant, $F = \mu_k R$, a little **less** than limiting friction |

This explains why it takes more force to **start** pushing a heavy box than to **keep** it sliding.

## Coefficient of friction

:::callout{type=definition}
The **coefficient of static friction** $\mu_s$ is the ratio of the **limiting friction** to the **normal reaction**:

$$\mu_s = \dfrac{F_{lim}}{R}$$

The **coefficient of kinetic friction** $\mu_k$ is the ratio of the kinetic friction to the normal reaction. Both are **pure numbers** (no unit).
:::

On a horizontal surface with no other vertical forces, $R = mg$, so $F = \mu mg$.

| Surfaces | Approximate $\mu_s$ |
|---|---|
| Rubber tyre on dry tarmac | 0.8–1.0 |
| Rubber tyre on wet road | 0.4–0.6 |
| Wood on wood | 0.3–0.5 |
| Steel on steel (dry) | 0.6 |
| Steel on ice | 0.03 |

## The laws of solid friction

1. Friction **opposes** the relative motion of the surfaces.
2. Friction is **independent of the area** of contact (for the same normal reaction).
3. Limiting friction is **directly proportional to the normal reaction**: $F \propto R$.
4. Friction depends on the **nature of the surfaces** in contact.
5. Kinetic friction is roughly **independent of the speed** of sliding (for moderate speeds).

## Worked examples

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**1. A 20 kg crate rests on a floor. The coefficient of static friction is 0.4 and of kinetic friction 0.3. ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)**

**(a) What horizontal force just starts it moving?**

$R = mg = 200$ N, so $F_{lim} = \mu_s R = 0.4 \times 200 = 80$ N.

**(b) What force keeps it moving at constant speed?**

$F = \mu_k R = 0.3 \times 200 = 60$ N.

**(c) A force of 100 N is applied. Find the acceleration.**

Once sliding, friction is 60 N. Resultant $= 100 - 60 = 40$ N, so $a = \dfrac{40}{20} = 2$ m s⁻².

**(d) A force of 50 N is applied to the crate at rest. What is the friction?**

50 N is less than 80 N, so the crate does not move: static friction is just **50 N**, equal to the push. Friction is not automatically $\mu R$.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
$F = \mu R$ gives the **maximum** static friction. If the push is smaller than this, friction equals the push and the body stays at rest.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**2. A car of mass 1000 kg travelling at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes hard and skids to a stop. The coefficient of kinetic friction between tyres and road is 0.8. Find the stopping distance. ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)**

Friction $F = 0.8 \times 1000 \times 10 = 8000$ N; deceleration $a = \dfrac{8000}{1000} = 8$ m s⁻².

$$0 = 20^2 - 2(8)s \Rightarrow s = \dfrac{400}{16} = 25\ \text{m}$$

On a wet road with $\mu = 0.4$, the deceleration halves and the stopping distance **doubles** to 50 m.
:::

## Pulling at an angle

If a rope pulls at angle θ above the horizontal, part of the pull lifts the body, so the normal reaction is **reduced**:

$$R = mg - P\sin\theta \qquad F = \mu(mg - P\sin\theta)$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 10 kg box is pulled by a rope at 30° above the horizontal; $\mu = 0.5$. Find the tension needed to keep it moving steadily. ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)**

Horizontally: $P\cos 30° = 0.5(100 - P\sin 30°)$

$$0.866P = 50 - 0.25P \Rightarrow 1.116P = 50 \Rightarrow P = 44.8\ \text{N}$$

Pushing **down** at 30° instead would increase $R$ and need more force.
:::

## Key points

- Friction opposes relative motion and acts along the surfaces.
- Static friction matches the applied force up to the **limiting** value $\mu_s R$; kinetic friction $\mu_k R$ is slightly less.
- $\mu = F/R$ has no unit; friction does not depend on contact area.
- Use the **resultant** force (applied minus friction) in $F = ma$.

## Quick check

1. What is the unit of the coefficient of friction?
2. A 50 N block needs 20 N to start it sliding on a horizontal bench. Find $\mu_s$.
3. Does increasing the area of contact (same weight) increase friction?

**Answers:** 1. It has no unit. 2. $\mu_s = 20 \div 50 = 0.4$. 3. No: friction is independent of the area of contact.
