Why does powdered sugar dissolve faster than a cube, food keep longer in a refrigerator, and an enzyme in yeast turn sugar into alcohol at room temperature? Each is a factor that changes the rate of a reaction. This lesson explains each factor with collision theory, so you can answer "explain" questions, not just "state" questions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the effect of concentration and pressure on rate
- explain the effect of temperature on rate, using the distribution of particle energies
- explain the effect of surface area (particle size) on rate
- describe how catalysts work and give examples, including enzymes
- explain the effect of light on some reactions
- describe experiments to investigate these factors

## Concentration (and pressure for gases)

- **Higher concentration** → more particles per unit volume → **more frequent collisions** → **faster rate**.
- For **gases**, increasing the **pressure** squeezes the particles closer together, which has the same effect as increasing concentration.

**Experiment:** react magnesium ribbon with hydrochloric acid of different concentrations and time how long the magnesium takes to disappear; or use sodium thiosulphate of different concentrations and time the disappearing cross.

## Temperature

![Distribution of particle energies at a lower temperature T1 and a higher temperature T2: at T2 many more particles have energy above the activation energy Ea; a catalyst lowers Ea](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/energy-distribution.svg)

*Figure 1. Raising the temperature increases the number of particles able to react. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Raising the temperature increases the rate for **two reasons**:

1. Particles **move faster**, so they **collide more often**.
2. More importantly, a **much larger fraction** of particles have energy **equal to or above the activation energy**, so a larger fraction of collisions are **successful** (the shaded area in Figure 1).

**Rule of thumb:** for many reactions, a **10 °C rise roughly doubles** the rate.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A reaction takes 80 s at 30 °C. Estimate the time at 50 °C.**

A 20 °C rise is two 10 °C steps, so the rate roughly **doubles twice** (× 4). Time ≈ $80 \div 4 = 20$ s.
:::

## Surface area (particle size)

- Breaking a solid into **smaller pieces** or a **powder** increases its **surface area**.
- More particles are exposed to the other reactant, so collisions are **more frequent** and the rate **increases**.

**Experiment:** react equal masses of large marble chips, small chips and powder with the same acid; measure the volume of CO₂ against time. Powder gives the steepest curve, but the **same final volume**, because the mass of marble is the same.

**Dust explosions:** fine flour, coal dust or sawdust in air has an enormous surface area and can burn explosively, a danger in flour mills and mines.

## Catalysts

:::callout{type=definition}
A **catalyst** is a substance that **increases the rate** of a chemical reaction **without being used up**. It is **chemically unchanged** at the end of the reaction.
:::

**How a catalyst works:** it provides an **alternative reaction pathway** with a **lower activation energy**. So at the same temperature, a much larger fraction of collisions have enough energy to react (the green line in Figure 1).

**Properties of catalysts:**

- needed only in **small amounts**
- **chemically unchanged** at the end (though they may change physically, e.g. lumps become powder)
- **specific**: each catalyses particular reactions
- do **not** change the yield or the position of equilibrium, or the value of ΔH
- can be **poisoned** by impurities

| Catalyst | Reaction |
|---|---|
| **Manganese(IV) oxide** | Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ |
| **Iron** | Haber process: making ammonia |
| **Vanadium(V) oxide** | Contact process: making sulphuric acid |
| **Platinum** | Oxidation of ammonia (nitric acid); car exhaust catalytic converters |
| **Nickel** | Hydrogenation of vegetable oils to make margarine |
| **Enzymes** (biological catalysts) | Zymase in yeast: fermentation of sugar; amylase: starch digestion |

**Enzymes** are proteins. They work best at a particular **optimum temperature** (about 37 °C in the body) and pH. High temperatures **denature** them, and they stop working.

A **negative catalyst (inhibitor)** slows a reaction. Examples are preservatives in food and the glycerol added to hydrogen peroxide.

## Light

Some reactions are speeded up by light, which supplies the energy to break bonds:

- **Photosynthesis** in green plants.
- **Hydrogen and chlorine** react explosively in sunlight but only slowly in the dark.
- **Silver bromide** on photographic film decomposes in light.
- Some medicines are stored in **brown bottles** to keep light out.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Explaining a result.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes slowly at room temperature. When a little manganese(IV) oxide is added, oxygen bubbles off rapidly, and the black powder can be filtered off, dried and weighed at the end with its mass unchanged.

- The manganese(IV) oxide is a **catalyst**: it gives a pathway with lower activation energy.
- Its mass is unchanged, so it was **not used up**.
- The **total volume** of oxygen is the same with or without the catalyst; only the **rate** changes.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Palm wine ferments quickly in the heat because the **yeast enzymes** work fast at warm temperatures. Tappers sell it fresh in the morning, because by evening it has become more alcoholic and then sour. **Cool storage slows** the enzyme-catalysed reactions. Pressure cookers do the opposite for cooking: the higher pressure raises the boiling point, so food cooks at a higher temperature and much faster.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A catalyst does **not** increase the **amount** of product, and it does not give particles more energy. It lowers the **activation energy**, so the same amount of product forms **faster**.
:::

## Key points

- **Concentration/pressure ↑** → more frequent collisions → faster.
- **Temperature ↑** → faster particles **and** many more with energy ≥ Ea; a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate.
- **Surface area ↑** (smaller particles) → more exposed particles → faster; same final yield.
- **Catalysts** lower Ea via an alternative pathway; they are unchanged, needed in small amounts and do not change the yield. **Enzymes** are biological catalysts.
- **Light** speeds photochemical reactions (photosynthesis, H₂ + Cl₂, AgBr).

## Quick check

1. Explain why powdered marble reacts faster with acid than marble chips.
2. Give two reasons why a rise in temperature increases the rate of reaction.
3. How does a catalyst increase the rate of a reaction?
4. Name the catalyst used in the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

**Answers:**

1. Powder has a larger surface area, so more particles are exposed and collisions are more frequent.
2. Particles collide more often, and a larger fraction of them have energy at least equal to the activation energy.
3. It provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so more collisions are successful.
4. Manganese(IV) oxide.
