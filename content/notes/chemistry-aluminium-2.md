Aluminium is light, strong when alloyed, an excellent conductor and resistant to corrosion, a combination no other common metal matches. That is why it is used for cooking pots, roofing sheets, drink cans, window frames and overhead power cables. This lesson covers the properties and reactions of aluminium, its compounds and the reasons behind its many uses.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the physical properties of aluminium and relate them to its uses
- explain why aluminium resists corrosion despite being reactive
- describe the reactions of aluminium with oxygen, acids and alkalis
- explain the amphoteric nature of aluminium oxide and hydroxide
- describe the uses of aluminium compounds, including alum
- describe the test for aluminium ions

## Physical properties and uses

| Property | Use it makes possible |
|---|---|
| **Low density** (2.7 g/cm³, about a third of steel) | **Aircraft**, cars, bicycles (as **duralumin** alloy) |
| **Good conductor of electricity** | **Overhead power cables** (with a steel core for strength): lighter than copper |
| **Good conductor of heat** | **Cooking pots** and pans |
| **Resists corrosion** | **Roofing sheets**, window frames, outdoor fittings |
| **Malleable and ductile** | **Foil** for wrapping food; drink cans |
| **Non-toxic** | Food packaging and cans |
| **Reflects light and heat** | Mirrors, emergency blankets, insulation |

## Why aluminium resists corrosion

Aluminium is high in the reactivity series, but it is covered by a thin, **tough, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide** that forms the moment fresh metal meets the air:

$$4\text{Al(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)}$$

This layer **stops air and water** reaching the metal underneath. Unlike rust, it does not flake off. **Anodising** thickens the oxide layer by electrolysis (with the aluminium as the **anode**), giving even better protection and allowing it to be dyed.

## Chemical reactions

**1. With oxygen:** powdered aluminium burns brilliantly (used in fireworks): 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃.

**2. With dilute hydrochloric acid:** slow at first while the oxide layer dissolves, then vigorous:

$$2\text{Al(s)} + 6\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3\text{(aq)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

With **concentrated nitric acid**, aluminium becomes **passive**: the oxide layer thickens and protects it. That is why nitric acid can be transported in aluminium tanks.

**3. With alkalis:** aluminium reacts with **sodium hydroxide** solution, giving hydrogen:

$$2\text{Al(s)} + 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaAl(OH)}_4\text{(aq)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

The product is sodium aluminate. This is why **washing soda or strong alkaline cleaners damage aluminium pots**.

**4. Displacement and the thermite reaction:** aluminium displaces less reactive metals from their oxides, releasing great heat:

$$2\text{Al(s)} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{Fe(l)}$$

This is used to weld railway lines and to extract metals such as chromium.

## Amphoteric oxide and hydroxide

Aluminium oxide and hydroxide react with **both acids and alkalis**:

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 6\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{as a base})$$

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{NaOH} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaAl(OH)}_4 \quad (\text{as an acid})$$

This amphoteric behaviour is the basis of the Bayer process for purifying bauxite.

## Aluminium compounds

| Compound | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Aluminium oxide**, Al₂O₃ | Very hard, high melting point, amphoteric | **Abrasives** (emery, corundum); refractory linings; gemstones (ruby and sapphire are coloured Al₂O₃) |
| **Aluminium hydroxide**, Al(OH)₃ | White, gelatinous, amphoteric | **Antacids**; **mordant** in dyeing (fixes dye to cloth); fire retardant |
| **Aluminium sulphate**, Al₂(SO₄)₃ | Soluble | **Water treatment** (coagulant); paper sizing |
| **Potash alum**, K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O | A double salt | Clearing water; **mordant**; stopping bleeding from small cuts |
| **Aluminium chloride**, AlCl₃ | Fumes in moist air; covalent when anhydrous | Catalyst in making petrochemicals |

## Test for aluminium ions

| Reagent | Observation |
|---|---|
| **Sodium hydroxide**, a few drops | **White** gelatinous precipitate, Al(OH)₃ |
| **Sodium hydroxide**, in excess | Precipitate **dissolves** (colourless solution) |
| **Aqueous ammonia**, in excess | White precipitate that does **not** dissolve |

The ammonia test distinguishes Al³⁺ from Zn²⁺ (whose precipitate dissolves in excess ammonia).

## Group III

Aluminium is in **group 3 (III)** with **boron**. Boron is a metalloid; its compound **borax** is used in glass (heat-resistant glassware) and as a mild antiseptic. Group 3 elements have **three outer electrons**, and aluminium forms the Al³⁺ ion.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Across West Africa, including Sierra Leone, artisans **recycle scrap aluminium** (old pots, car parts, cans) by melting it and casting new **cooking pots** in sand moulds. Recycling uses far less energy than extracting aluminium from bauxite. Aluminium roofing sheets are also popular near the coast because they resist the salty air better than ordinary galvanised iron.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Aluminium is **not** unreactive: it is high in the reactivity series. It only **appears** unreactive because of its protective oxide layer. Remove the layer and it reacts readily, even with water.
:::

## Key points

- Aluminium is **light, a good conductor, malleable, non-toxic** and **resists corrosion**: used for aircraft, power cables, pots, roofing, foil and cans.
- A thin **oxide layer** protects it; **anodising** thickens the layer.
- It reacts with **acids** (H₂), with **alkalis** (aluminate + H₂), and in the **thermite** reaction; concentrated HNO₃ makes it **passive**.
- Al₂O₃ and Al(OH)₃ are **amphoteric**.
- Compounds: Al₂O₃ (abrasives), Al(OH)₃ (antacid, mordant), **alum and Al₂(SO₄)₃** (water treatment).
- **Test:** white precipitate with NaOH, **soluble in excess**; insoluble in excess ammonia.

## Quick check

1. Give two properties that make aluminium suitable for overhead power cables.
2. Why should aluminium pots not be cleaned with washing soda?
3. Write an equation for aluminium oxide reacting with sodium hydroxide.
4. How would you distinguish Al³⁺ from Zn²⁺ in solution?

**Answers:**

1. Any two: good electrical conductor, low density (light), resists corrosion, ductile.
2. Washing soda is alkaline and aluminium reacts with alkalis, dissolving the pot.
3. Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄.
4. Add excess aqueous ammonia: the white precipitate dissolves for Zn²⁺ but not for Al³⁺.
