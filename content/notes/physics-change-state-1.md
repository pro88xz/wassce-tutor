Put a pot of water on the fire and watch the thermometer. It climbs steadily to 100 °C — and then stops, even though the fire still burns. Where is all that heat going? Into changing water to steam. That hidden heat is latent heat.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the changes of state and interpret a heating curve
- define latent heat, specific latent heat of fusion and of vaporisation
- use $Q = mL$ together with $Q = mc\Delta\theta$
- explain latent heat using the kinetic theory

## Changes of state

| Change | Name | Heat |
|---|---|---|
| solid → liquid | melting (fusion) | absorbed |
| liquid → solid | freezing (solidification) | given out |
| liquid → gas | boiling / evaporation (vaporisation) | absorbed |
| gas → liquid | condensation | given out |
| solid → gas directly | sublimation (e.g. iodine, camphor, naphthalene) | absorbed |

## The heating curve

![Temperature against time as ice at minus 10 degrees Celsius is heated: the temperature rises to 0, stays flat while the ice melts, rises to 100, stays flat while the water boils, then rises again as steam](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/heating-curve.svg)

*Figure 1. Heating curve of water. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

1. Ice warms from −10 °C to 0 °C (temperature rises).
2. **At 0 °C the ice melts**: the temperature stays constant until all the ice has melted.
3. Water warms from 0 °C to 100 °C.
4. **At 100 °C the water boils**: the temperature stays constant until all the water has become steam.
5. Steam warms above 100 °C.

The flat sections show that heat is being absorbed **without a change in temperature**. This heat is **latent** ("hidden") heat.

## Latent heat

:::callout{type=definition}
**Latent heat** is the heat absorbed or released when a substance **changes state without a change in temperature**.

**Specific latent heat of fusion** $L_f$: the quantity of heat needed to change **1 kg** of a substance from **solid to liquid** at its **melting point**, without a change in temperature.

**Specific latent heat of vaporisation** $L_v$: the quantity of heat needed to change **1 kg** of a substance from **liquid to vapour** at its **boiling point**, without a change in temperature.

$$Q = mL \quad (L \text{ in J kg}^{-1})$$
:::

| Substance | Value |
|---|---|
| Ice (fusion) | $3.36 \times 10^{5}$ J kg⁻¹ (336 kJ kg⁻¹) |
| Water (vaporisation) | $2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J kg⁻¹ (2260 kJ kg⁻¹) |

It takes almost **seven times** as much energy to boil away water as to melt the same mass of ice.

### Kinetic explanation

- During **melting**, the energy supplied does not speed the particles up (temperature unchanged); it **breaks the bonds** holding them in fixed positions.
- During **boiling**, the energy separates the particles **completely**, overcoming their attraction and pushing back the atmosphere as the vapour expands. This needs far more energy, which is why $L_v > L_f$.

## Worked examples

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How much heat is needed to melt 2 kg of ice at 0 °C? ($L_f = 3.36 \times 10^{5}$ J kg⁻¹)**

$$Q = mL_f = 2 \times 3.36 \times 10^{5} = 6.72 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Find the heat needed to change 0.5 kg of ice at −10 °C completely into steam at 100 °C.** ($c_{ice} = 2100$, $c_{water} = 4200$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, $L_f = 3.36 \times 10^{5}$, $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J kg⁻¹)

| Stage | Heat |
|---|---|
| Warm ice −10 → 0 °C | $0.5 \times 2100 \times 10 = 10\,500$ J |
| Melt ice at 0 °C | $0.5 \times 3.36 \times 10^{5} = 168\,000$ J |
| Warm water 0 → 100 °C | $0.5 \times 4200 \times 100 = 210\,000$ J |
| Boil water at 100 °C | $0.5 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 1\,130\,000$ J |
| **Total** | **1 518 500 J ≈ 1.52 MJ** |

Boiling takes three-quarters of the total.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Ice at 0 °C is added to 0.4 kg of water at 30 °C until the water just reaches 0 °C with all the ice melted. What mass of ice was added?**

Heat lost by water $= 0.4 \times 4200 \times 30 = 50\,400$ J.

Heat gained by ice (melting only) $= m \times 3.36 \times 10^{5}$.

$$m = \dfrac{50\,400}{3.36 \times 10^{5}} = 0.15\ \text{kg}$$
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
A scald from steam is worse than one from boiling water at the same temperature. Each kilogram of steam that condenses on the skin releases 2.26 MJ of latent heat **before** it even starts to cool as water.
:::

## Key points

- Temperature stays constant during a change of state; the heat goes into breaking bonds (latent heat).
- $Q = mL$: $L_f$ for melting, $L_v$ for boiling.
- For ice at 0 °C, $L_f \approx 3.36 \times 10^{5}$ J kg⁻¹; for water at 100 °C, $L_v \approx 2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J kg⁻¹.
- Multi-stage problems: add $mc\Delta\theta$ for each temperature change and $mL$ for each change of state.

## Quick check

1. Why does the temperature stay constant while ice melts?
2. How much heat is released when 0.2 kg of steam condenses at 100 °C? ($L_v = 2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J kg⁻¹)
3. Name a substance that sublimes.

**Answers:** 1. The heat supplied breaks the bonds between the particles instead of increasing their kinetic energy. 2. $0.2 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 4.52 \times 10^{5}$ J. 3. For example: iodine, camphor or naphthalene.
