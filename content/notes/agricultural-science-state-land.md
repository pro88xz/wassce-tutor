The Western Area Peninsula forest, the land under a government agricultural research station, and the plots of a youth farming scheme set up by a ministry have one thing in common: they are controlled by the **state**. When the government owns or controls land, it can decide how the land is used for the whole nation, for farming, forests, roads or settlement. This is **state land ownership**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define state land ownership
- explain how the state obtains land, including compulsory acquisition
- describe how state land is used in agriculture
- state the advantages and disadvantages of state land ownership

## What is state land ownership?

:::callout{type=definition}
**State land ownership** is a land tenure system in which land is owned or controlled by the **government**, which decides how it is used and may lease or allocate it to farmers, companies or public projects.
:::

:::callout{type=definition}
**Compulsory acquisition** is the taking of private or customary land by the government for a public purpose, such as a road, dam or research station, with **compensation** paid to the owners.
:::

## How the state obtains land

1. **Crown or government land** held since colonial times, especially around Freetown.
2. **Compulsory acquisition** for public purposes, with compensation.
3. **Purchase or lease** from landowners, like any other buyer or lessee.
4. **Reserves**: forest reserves, national parks and protected areas set aside by law.

## How state land is used in agriculture

- **Research stations and institutes**, where improved crop varieties and animal breeds are developed and tested.
- **Government farms and farm settlements**, which demonstrate modern methods and train young farmers.
- **Youth and graduate farming schemes**, which give land to young people who would otherwise have none.
- **Leasing to investors** for large plantations, which bring jobs and export earnings.
- **Forest reserves and national parks**, which protect watersheds, soil, timber and wildlife.

## Advantages of state land ownership

1. The government can plan land use for the **whole nation**, for food production, forests and infrastructure.
2. Land can be provided for **large-scale projects** (irrigation, plantations, research) that need big areas.
3. Land can be **allocated to the landless**, young people and settlers.
4. **Speculation** is reduced: land is not held idle waiting for prices to rise.
5. **Conservation**: forests, watersheds and soils can be protected.
6. Fewer disputes where boundaries are surveyed and allocation rules are clear.

## Disadvantages of state land ownership

1. Farmers who are only **allocated** land, not owners, may lack security and fail to invest in it.
2. **Bureaucracy** and delays in allocating land.
3. **Corruption** and favouritism in who gets land.
4. **Compulsory acquisition** can displace families, especially if compensation is low or late.
5. Poorly managed government farms may produce **low output**.
6. Allocated land usually **cannot be sold or mortgaged**, so it is hard to borrow against it.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Compulsory acquisition is **not** confiscation. The government takes land for a **public purpose** and must pay **compensation**; it is not taking land for private gain or as a punishment.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Research stations of the Sierra Leone Agricultural Research Institute (SLARI), such as Rokupr for rice and Njala for a range of crops, stand on land used by the state for agricultural research. The Western Area Peninsula Forest is protected as a national park because it supplies Freetown's water; farming and building inside it are restricted to protect the catchment.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Weighing a decision.** A ministry wants land for a rice irrigation scheme in a valley farmed by several families.

- Advantage: one large, planned, irrigated scheme can produce far more rice than scattered rain-fed plots and employ many farmers.
- Disadvantage: the families lose their land; they must be **consulted** and **fairly compensated**, or given plots within the scheme.

A good answer weighs both sides and shows how the disadvantage can be reduced.
:::

## Key points

- **State land ownership**: land owned or controlled by the government, which decides its use.
- The state obtains land as government land, by **compulsory acquisition** with compensation, by purchase or lease, and through **reserves**.
- Uses: research stations, government farms, youth schemes, leases to investors, forest reserves.
- Advantages: national planning, large projects, land for the landless, less speculation, conservation.
- Disadvantages: insecurity for users, bureaucracy, corruption, displacement and low output from poorly run farms.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **explain state (government) land ownership** and to **state its advantages and disadvantages**, often alongside communal, freehold and leasehold systems. If asked about **compulsory acquisition**, say that it is for a public purpose and that compensation is paid.
:::
