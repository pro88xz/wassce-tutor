:::callout{type=definition}
**Map reading** is the skill of getting information from a map: measuring **distances** and **areas** with the **scale**, finding **directions** and **bearings**, and locating places with **grid references**.
:::

A **topographical map** shows both natural features (relief, rivers, vegetation) and human features (settlements, roads, land use) using **conventional signs**. The **scale** tells you how much smaller the map is than the ground; on a **1:50,000** map, **2 cm on the map represents 1 km on the ground**. This lesson covers scale, distance, area, direction, bearings and grid references, the skills tested in every WASSCE Geography map question.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the three ways of stating a map scale and convert between them
- measure straight and curved distances on a map
- estimate the area of a feature using grid squares
- give directions using the points of the compass
- measure and calculate bearings and back bearings
- give four-figure and six-figure grid references

## Map scale

:::callout{type=definition}
The **scale** of a map is the ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground.
:::

| Method | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** (verbal scale) | "2 cm represents 1 km" | easy to understand |
| **Representative fraction (RF)** | 1:50,000 or $\frac{1}{50\,000}$ | no units: 1 cm on the map is 50,000 cm on the ground; works in any unit |
| **Linear scale** (scale line) | a line divided into kilometres | stays correct if the map is enlarged or reduced |

### Converting scales

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Convert the RF 1:50,000 to a statement.**

1 cm on the map represents 50,000 cm on the ground.

$50\,000 \text{ cm} = 500 \text{ m} = 0.5 \text{ km}$.

So **1 cm represents 0.5 km**, or **2 cm represent 1 km**.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Convert the statement "4 cm represents 1 km" to an RF.**

1 km $= 100\,000$ cm, so 4 cm represent 100,000 cm.

1 cm represents $100\,000 \div 4 = 25\,000$ cm.

RF $=$ **1:25,000**.
:::

**Large-scale maps** (such as 1:10,000) show a small area in great detail; **small-scale maps** (such as 1:1,000,000) show a large area with little detail.

## Measuring distance

- **Straight-line distance:** measure with a ruler, or mark it on the straight edge of a paper strip and lay the strip against the linear scale.
- **Curved distance** (along a road, river or railway): lay a strip of paper or a thread along the route, pivoting it at each bend and marking it, then measure the total length against the scale.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**On a 1:50,000 map, a straight road measures 7.4 cm. What is its length on the ground?**

1 cm represents 0.5 km, so $7.4 \times 0.5 = 3.7$ km.
:::

## Estimating area

On a 1:50,000 map, the grid lines are 2 cm apart, so each **grid square represents 1 km × 1 km = 1 km²**.

1. Count the **full squares** inside the feature.
2. Count the **part squares** and divide by two (assuming they average half a square each).
3. Add the two numbers to get the area in km².

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A forest covers 12 full grid squares and 10 part squares on a 1:50,000 map. Estimate its area.**

Area $\approx 12 + \frac{10}{2} = 17$ km².
:::

## Direction and bearings

![Left: the sixteen points of the compass. Right: the bearing of Y from X is 60 degrees measured clockwise from north, and the back bearing from Y to X is 240 degrees](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/compass-bearings.svg)

*Figure 1. The sixteen points of the compass, and bearings. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **Direction** is described with the **compass points**: the four **cardinal points** (N, E, S, W), the four **intercardinal points** (NE, SE, SW, NW) and eight more (NNE, ENE and so on), making **sixteen** in all.
- A **bearing** is the angle measured **clockwise from north** to the line joining two places, written with **three figures**: 045°, 120°, 270°.
- The **back bearing** is the bearing of the return journey: add 180° if the bearing is less than 180°, or subtract 180° if it is more.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The bearing of a school from a church is 060°. What is the bearing of the church from the school?**

Back bearing $= 060° + 180° = 240°$.
:::

**Steps to measure a bearing on a map:** join the two places with a line; draw a north line through the starting point (parallel to the eastings); place the protractor's zero on north and measure clockwise to the line.

## Grid references

![A grid with eastings 21 to 25 and northings 46 to 49; the shaded square has the four-figure reference 2247 and point A, a church inside it, has the six-figure reference 224477](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/grid-reference.svg)

*Figure 2. Four-figure and six-figure grid references. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **Eastings** are the vertical grid lines, numbered from west to east along the bottom and top of the map.
- **Northings** are the horizontal grid lines, numbered from south to north up the sides.
- Always give the **easting first, then the northing**: "along the corridor, then up the stairs".

| Reference | How to find it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Four-figure** | identifies a whole **grid square**, using the easting and northing of its **bottom left corner** | square **2247** |
| **Six-figure** | pinpoints a **place** to the nearest tenth of a square: easting + tenths, then northing + tenths | church at **224477** |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Never give the northing first. **224477** and **477224** are completely different places. Eastings (along) always come before northings (up).
:::

## Conventional signs

Topographical maps use **conventional signs** (symbols) explained in a **key**: for example, roads in red or brown, rivers in blue, contours in brown, forests in green, settlements as black squares or shaded areas, churches and mosques, schools, post offices, railways and boundaries. Always read the key before answering questions.

## Key points

- Scale: **statement**, **representative fraction**, **linear scale**; on **1:50,000**, **2 cm represent 1 km**.
- Measure curved distances with a **paper strip or thread**.
- Area on a 1:50,000 map: **full squares + part squares ÷ 2** km².
- **Bearings** are measured **clockwise from north** in three figures; **back bearing** $=$ bearing ± 180°.
- **Grid references:** **eastings first**, then northings; four-figure for a square, six-figure for a point.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Every map question tests these skills: **converting scales**, **measuring distances** (often along a road or river), **estimating areas**, **giving bearings and directions**, and **giving six-figure grid references** for features. Show your working for every calculation.
:::

## Quick check

1. Convert the RF 1:25,000 to a statement in km.
2. On a 1:50,000 map, a river measures 9 cm. How long is it on the ground?
3. A lake covers 6 full squares and 8 part squares on a 1:50,000 map. Estimate its area.
4. The bearing of B from A is 300°. What is the bearing of A from B?
5. What does a four-figure grid reference identify?

**Answers:**

1. 1 cm represents 25,000 cm, which is 0.25 km (or 4 cm represent 1 km).
2. $9 \times 0.5 = 4.5$ km.
3. $6 + \frac{8}{2} = 10$ km².
4. $300° - 180° = 120°$.
5. A whole grid square, named by the easting and northing of its bottom left corner.
