# Meaning, scope and branches of Geography

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- define Geography and explain its central concern with places, patterns and relationships
- distinguish physical, human and environmental Geography
- explain the main tools used by geographers
- apply geographical thinking to a familiar Sierra Leone example

## What is Geography?

Geography is the systematic study of the Earth’s surface, the distribution of physical and human features, and the relationships between people and their environments. It asks **where** something occurs, **why** it occurs there, **how** it is connected to other places, and **what changes** as a result.

Geography is more than memorising the names of countries, rivers and mountains. A geographer investigating flooding in Freetown, for example, studies rainfall, slope, drainage, vegetation, building patterns, waste disposal and the decisions that influence where people live. The aim is to explain the pattern and understand how risk can be reduced.

## The main branches of Geography

### Physical Geography

Physical Geography studies natural processes and features of the Earth. Its areas include:

- **Geomorphology:** landforms and the processes that create and modify them.
- **Climatology:** climate patterns, their causes and their effects.
- **Hydrology:** water on land, including rivers, lakes and drainage basins.
- **Biogeography:** the distribution of plants and animals.
- **Soil geography:** soil formation, properties, distribution and conservation.

### Human Geography

Human Geography examines people, their activities and the spatial organisation of society. It includes population, settlement, agriculture, industry, transport, trade and tourism.

For example, population Geography examines why people are concentrated in some places, why others are sparsely populated, and how migration changes the size and character of settlements.

### Environmental Geography

Environmental Geography connects physical and human Geography. It investigates how people use natural resources, how human activities change ecosystems, and how environmental problems can be managed.

Deforestation, coastal erosion, pollution and climate change are environmental issues because they involve interactions between natural systems and human decisions.

## Geography as a spatial subject

:::callout{type=definition}
A **spatial pattern** is the way a feature is arranged across an area.
:::

Settlements may form a linear pattern along a road or river, while industries may cluster around a port or an urban market.

Geographers use several key ideas:

- **Location:** the position of a feature. It may be absolute, using coordinates, or relative, describing a place in relation to another.
- **Place:** the distinctive physical and human characteristics of a location.
- **Distance:** the separation between places, measured in units such as kilometres or in travel time.
- **Scale:** the level of geographical study, from local to regional, national and global.
- **Interaction:** the movement and connections between places, people and environments.
- **Distribution:** where a feature occurs and how frequently it is found.

## Tools used by geographers

Maps show the location and distribution of features. Satellite images and aerial photographs provide views of the Earth's surface. Statistical tables, graphs and diagrams help compare geographical data. Fieldwork allows geographers to observe, measure and record conditions directly.

A map is a model of reality, not reality itself. Its scale, symbols, orientation, date and purpose affect what it can show. A small-scale map covers a large area with less detail; a large-scale map covers a smaller area with more detail.

## Worked example: investigating flooding in Freetown

Suppose a study examines repeated flooding in a low-lying neighbourhood of Freetown.

1. **Physical factors:** measure rainfall, identify the slope of the land and map nearby streams.
2. **Human factors:** record building density, blocked drains, paved surfaces and waste disposal practices.
3. **Spatial pattern:** map the locations where floodwater accumulates and compare them with elevation and drainage.
4. **Relationships:** explain how intense rainfall, reduced infiltration and restricted drainage combine to increase runoff.
5. **Response:** recommend drain maintenance, suitable land-use planning, protection of waterways and public awareness.

The example demonstrates why Geography combines physical and human evidence rather than explaining an environmental problem from one cause alone.

## Key points

- Geography studies the Earth’s physical and human features, their distribution and their relationships.
- Physical Geography examines natural systems; Human Geography examines people and their activities; Environmental Geography links the two.
- Location, place, distance, scale, interaction and distribution are central geographical ideas.
- Maps, images, statistics and fieldwork are important geographical tools.

## WASSCE examination focus

When asked to define Geography, include both the study of the Earth's features and the spatial relationships between people and their environment. When asked to distinguish branches, give the focus of each branch and a relevant example. Avoid describing Geography as map-reading alone.
