# Earthquakes, volcanoes, hazards and risk reduction

## Learning objectives
You should be able to explain how earthquakes and volcanic eruptions occur, interpret basic hazard terminology, compare their effects, and describe measures that reduce disaster risk.

## Earthquakes: causes and measurement
An earthquake is the sudden shaking of the ground caused by the rapid release of stored energy, usually when rocks slip along a fault. Stress builds as tectonic forces act on rocks. When the strength of the fault is overcome, rupture occurs and seismic waves travel through the Earth.

The **focus** or hypocentre is the point within the Earth where rupture begins. The **epicentre** is the point on the surface directly above the focus. Seismometers record ground motion; seismograms are used to study the event.

Magnitude describes the size of an earthquake at its source. Modern magnitude estimates commonly use the moment magnitude scale. Intensity describes the observed effects and damage at a particular place; it varies with distance, ground conditions, building quality and local vulnerability. Magnitude and intensity are not interchangeable.

## Volcanoes: eruption processes
A volcano is an opening or landform through which magma, gases and volcanic materials reach the surface. Magma may rise because it is less dense than surrounding rock and because gases expand as pressure decreases.

Eruptions vary. Low-viscosity basaltic lava can flow over long distances, while more viscous, gas-rich magma may trap gases and produce explosive eruptions. Volcanic materials include lava, ash, lapilli and larger fragments. Hazards include lava flows, ash fall, pyroclastic density currents, volcanic gases, lahars and landslides.

## Effects on people and the environment
Earthquakes may collapse buildings, damage roads and bridges, rupture water and gas pipes, trigger landslides and generate tsunamis when seafloor displacement occurs. Volcanic eruptions can destroy settlements and crops, disrupt aviation with ash, contaminate water and force evacuation.

There can also be benefits. Volcanic materials may weather into fertile soils; geothermal energy can be used where conditions are suitable; and volcanic landscapes may attract tourism. These benefits do not remove the serious risks to exposed communities.

## Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and risk
A **hazard** is a potentially damaging event or process. **Exposure** refers to people and assets located where the hazard may occur. **Vulnerability** is the degree to which they may be harmed. **Capacity** includes resources and systems available to prepare, respond and recover.

Disaster risk rises when a severe hazard affects highly exposed and vulnerable communities. A moderate earthquake can cause catastrophic losses where buildings are weak, while a stronger event may cause fewer casualties where construction and emergency systems are effective.

## Risk reduction
- Enforce earthquake-resistant building standards and retrofit vulnerable structures.
- Map hazard zones and guide land-use planning away from the most dangerous locations.
- Monitor seismic activity, ground deformation, gas emissions and other volcanic indicators.
- Maintain public warning systems, evacuation routes, drills and emergency supplies.
- Educate communities and protect critical infrastructure.
- Plan for recovery, including safe water, shelter, healthcare and restoration of transport.

Monitoring can improve warnings but cannot predict the exact time and place of every earthquake.

## WASSCE examination focus
Separate the physical cause from the effects and management measures. Use terms such as focus, epicentre, fault, seismic wave, magnitude and intensity accurately. In evaluation questions, explain why the same hazard produces different levels of damage in places with different exposure and vulnerability.
