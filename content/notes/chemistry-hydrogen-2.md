Industry needs hydrogen by the million tonnes, mainly to make ammonia, so it cannot come from zinc and acid. This lesson covers how hydrogen is produced industrially, why it is hard to place in the periodic table, its isotopes, its main compounds, including hydrogen peroxide, and its promise as a clean fuel.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the industrial production of hydrogen
- explain the unique position of hydrogen in the periodic table
- name the isotopes of hydrogen
- distinguish ionic and covalent hydrides
- describe the properties and uses of hydrogen peroxide
- discuss the advantages and problems of hydrogen as a fuel

## Industrial production

**1. Steam reforming of natural gas (methane)**, the main method today:

$$\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

This needs a nickel catalyst at about 800 °C. The carbon(II) oxide is then converted to more hydrogen by the **water-gas shift** reaction with more steam (iron catalyst):

$$\text{CO(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

The CO₂ is removed by dissolving it in water under pressure or in alkali.

**2. From coke (water gas):** steam is passed over white-hot coke.

$$\text{C(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

**3. Electrolysis:** of water (acidified) or as a **by-product** of the electrolysis of brine. It gives very pure hydrogen but uses a lot of electricity. With cheap solar or hydro power, this "**green hydrogen**" may become more important.

## Hydrogen in the periodic table

Hydrogen (1 electron, configuration 1s¹) does not fit neatly into any group.

| Like the alkali metals (group 1) | Like the halogens (group 7) |
|---|---|
| **One electron** in its outer shell | Needs **one electron** to fill its shell |
| Forms a **positive ion**, H⁺ | Forms a **negative ion**, H⁻, in hydrides (NaH) |
| Acts as a **reducing agent** | Exists as **diatomic molecules**, H₂ |
| Combines with halogens and oxygen | Forms **covalent bonds** with non-metals |

| Unlike the alkali metals | Unlike the halogens |
|---|---|
| It is a **non-metal gas** | It is not an oxidising agent |
| Its oxide (water) is **neutral** | Its ion H⁻ is unstable in water |

So it is usually placed **on its own** at the top of the table.

## Isotopes of hydrogen

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protium** | ¹H | 1 | 0 (about 99.98% of hydrogen) |
| **Deuterium** | ²H or D | 1 | 1 |
| **Tritium** | ³H or T | 1 | 2 (radioactive) |

**Heavy water**, D₂O, is used in some nuclear reactors. Tritium is used in luminous signs.

## Hydrides

| Ionic (saline) hydrides | Covalent hydrides |
|---|---|
| Formed with **very reactive metals** (Na, K, Ca) | Formed with **non-metals** |
| Contain the **H⁻** ion | Hydrogen **shares** electrons |
| White solids with high melting points | Mostly gases or liquids |
| React with water to give hydrogen: NaH + H₂O → NaOH + H₂ | Examples: CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, H₂S, HCl |

Across period 2, covalent hydrides change from neutral (CH₄) to basic (NH₃), neutral (H₂O) and acidic (HF).

## Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂

- A colourless liquid, usually sold as an **aqueous solution** ("20 volume" means 1 cm³ gives 20 cm³ of oxygen at s.t.p.).
- It **decomposes** slowly on its own, and quickly with a catalyst such as manganese(IV) oxide; it is stored in **dark bottles** because light speeds up the decomposition:

$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$

- It is both an **oxidising agent** (it releases iodine from KI) and, with very strong oxidisers, a **reducing agent** (it decolourises acidified KMnO₄).

**Uses:**

- **antiseptic**: cleaning wounds, mouthwash
- **bleaching** hair, textiles and paper pulp
- **laboratory source of oxygen**
- rocket propellant (concentrated)
- treating waste water

## Hydrogen as a fuel

| Advantages | Problems |
|---|---|
| Burns to give only **water**: no CO₂, smoke or sulphur | Mostly made from natural gas, which **does** release CO₂ |
| **Highest energy per kilogram** of any fuel | **Explosive** with air; leaks are dangerous |
| Can be made by electrolysis of water using solar or hydro power | Hard to **store and transport**: a gas needing high pressure or very low temperature |
| Fuel cells convert it to electricity efficiently | Expensive equipment; few filling stations |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Hospitals and clinics in Sierra Leone stock **hydrogen peroxide** (often 3% or 6% solution) to clean wounds; the fizzing seen on a cut is oxygen released as an enzyme in blood decomposes the peroxide. Bottles should be kept tightly closed in a cool, dark place, or the solution slowly turns into plain water.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Hydrogen peroxide is H₂O₂, not "hydrogen dioxide". It contains an O–O bond, and oxygen has an oxidation number of **−1** in it, not −2.
:::

## Key points

- Industrially, hydrogen comes from **steam reforming of methane** (Ni catalyst), **water gas** (C + H₂O) and **electrolysis**.
- Hydrogen resembles both **group 1** (one electron, H⁺) and **group 7** (needs one electron, H⁻, diatomic), so it stands alone.
- Isotopes: **protium, deuterium and tritium**.
- **Ionic hydrides** (NaH, contain H⁻) and **covalent hydrides** (CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, HCl).
- **Hydrogen peroxide** decomposes to water and oxygen; it is an antiseptic, bleach and oxygen source.
- Hydrogen is a **clean fuel** but hard to store and mostly made from fossil fuels today.

## Quick check

1. Write the equation for steam reforming of methane.
2. Give one way hydrogen resembles the halogens.
3. Name the isotope of hydrogen with one neutron.
4. Why is hydrogen peroxide stored in dark bottles?

**Answers:**

1. CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ (nickel catalyst, about 800 °C).
2. Any one: needs one electron to fill its shell; forms H⁻ ions; exists as diatomic molecules; forms covalent bonds with non-metals.
3. Deuterium.
4. Light speeds up its decomposition into water and oxygen.
