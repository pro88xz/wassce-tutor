# Food, nutrition and digestion: the human alimentary canal

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- trace the route taken by food through the alimentary canal
- describe mechanical and chemical digestion
- explain the roles of digestive enzymes and bile
- describe absorption in the small intestine
- distinguish absorption from assimilation and egestion

## The alimentary canal

The human alimentary canal is a continuous muscular tube through which food passes. Its main parts, in order, are the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus. Accessory organs that release digestive substances include the salivary glands, liver and pancreas. Food does not pass through the liver, gall bladder or pancreas.

## Ingestion and digestion in the mouth

Ingestion is taking food into the body. Teeth carry out mechanical digestion by cutting, tearing and grinding food into smaller pieces. The tongue mixes food with saliva and forms it into a bolus for swallowing. Saliva moistens food and contains salivary amylase, which begins the digestion of starch into smaller carbohydrates, including maltose.

Swallowing moves the bolus through the pharynx into the oesophagus. The epiglottis helps prevent food from entering the trachea. Waves of muscular contraction called peristalsis move food along the alimentary canal. Peristalsis is not simply the effect of gravity; it can move food when a person is lying down.

## The stomach

The stomach temporarily stores food and churns it, mixing it with gastric juice. Gastric glands release hydrochloric acid and enzymes. The acidic conditions help activate pepsin and provide a suitable pH for its action. Pepsin begins protein digestion by breaking proteins into shorter peptides. Hydrochloric acid also helps destroy many microorganisms swallowed with food.

The stomach lining is protected by mucus. If this protective barrier is damaged, acid and digestive enzymes can injure the stomach wall. Food leaves the stomach gradually through the pyloric sphincter into the small intestine.

## The small intestine and digestive secretions

Most chemical digestion and absorption occur in the small intestine. The first section, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver (stored and concentrated in the gall bladder) and pancreatic juice from the pancreas.

Bile is not an enzyme. It is alkaline and helps neutralise acidic material arriving from the stomach. Bile salts emulsify fats: they break large fat globules into tiny droplets, increasing the surface area available to lipase. Pancreatic amylase digests starch; proteases such as trypsin digest proteins and peptides; pancreatic lipase digests fats into fatty acids and monoglycerides. Enzymes in the intestinal lining complete digestion to absorbable products such as glucose and amino acids.

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up reactions without being used up. Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and pH range. Very high temperatures can denature an enzyme, changing the shape of its active site so that its substrate no longer fits effectively.

## Absorption and assimilation

The ileum is adapted for absorption. Its lining has numerous villi and microscopic microvilli, giving a large surface area. The epithelium is thin, and each villus has a rich network of blood capillaries and a lymph vessel called a lacteal.

Glucose and amino acids enter blood capillaries and are transported to the liver and then to body tissues. Products of fat digestion are absorbed into intestinal cells and are largely transported through the lacteals in the lymph before entering the blood circulation. Water, mineral salts and vitamins are also absorbed.

Absorption is the movement of digested soluble substances through the intestinal wall into blood or lymph. Assimilation is their use by body cells: glucose may be respired or stored as glycogen, amino acids may build proteins, and absorbed fats may be used or stored.

## The large intestine and egestion

The large intestine absorbs much of the remaining water and some mineral salts. Undigested material becomes faeces, which is stored in the rectum and expelled through the anus. Egestion is the removal of undigested food from the alimentary canal. It is different from excretion, which removes metabolic waste produced by cells, such as urea and carbon dioxide.

## WASSCE examination focus

Be able to draw and label the alimentary canal in the correct order. For enzyme questions, state the enzyme, its substrate and its products. Remember that bile emulsifies fats but does not chemically digest them because it is not an enzyme. Distinguish egestion from excretion and absorption from assimilation.

4. Give two adaptations of an ileum villus for absorption.
