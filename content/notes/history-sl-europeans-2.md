The **slave trade in Sierra Leone** took tens of thousands of people from the Upper Guinea coast to the Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. Its most famous centre was **Bunce Island**, a British fort in the Sierra Leone River from about **1670**, which shipped captives to **South Carolina and Georgia**, where their **rice-farming skills** were highly valued. After Britain banned the trade in **1807**, an **illegal trade** continued, especially at the **Gallinas**, where the Spanish trader **Pedro Blanco** held captives at **Lomboko**. One of them, **Sengbe Pieh**, led the famous **Amistad revolt of 1839** and returned home free in **1842**. This lesson explains **the slave trade in Sierra Leone, Bunce Island, the Gallinas trade and the Amistad story**.

## How the trade worked in Sierra Leone

1. **Sources of captives:** **wars** between chiefdoms (some fought to obtain captives), **raids and kidnapping**, people sold for **crimes, debt or witchcraft accusations**, and captives brought from the interior by traders.
2. **Middlemen:** local rulers and **Afro-European traders** (such as the Caulkers and Clevelands) collected captives and sold them to European ships and forts.
3. **Holding places:** captives were held in **barracoons** (slave pens) and forts until ships arrived.
4. **Payment:** captives were exchanged for **cloth, iron bars, guns, gunpowder, alcohol, tobacco and beads**.

## Bunce Island

**Bunce Island** lies in the **Sierra Leone River**, about 30 km upriver from Freetown.

| Period | Owners |
|---|---|
| **about 1670–1728** | the **Royal African Company** of England |
| **1748–1784** | **Grant, Oswald and Company** of London (Richard Oswald) |
| **1784–1807** | **John and Alexander Anderson** of London |

**Why it was important:**

1. It was one of the largest British slave-trading forts on the **Upper Guinea coast**.
2. Many captives were shipped to **Charleston (South Carolina)** and **Savannah (Georgia)**, where planters paid high prices for Africans from the **"Rice Coast"** who knew how to grow rice.
3. The fort was attacked by **pirates** and by the **French**, showing its value.
4. Its ruins are now a **national monument** and a place of remembrance.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **Gullah** people of the coast of **South Carolina and Georgia** are descendants of enslaved Africans, many from Sierra Leone and the Rice Coast. Their language and culture keep Sierra Leonean words, songs and foods. In **1989** a Gullah delegation visited Sierra Leone, and in **2005** "**Priscilla's Homecoming**" brought an American descendant of a girl shipped from Sierra Leone in 1756 back to Bunce Island. These visits have built strong links between Sierra Leone and the Gullah.
:::

## The illegal trade after 1807

Britain abolished the slave trade in **1807** and made **Freetown** the base of the anti-slavery **West Africa Squadron**. But Spanish, Portuguese and Brazilian traders kept buying captives along the coast south of Freetown, especially at the **Gallinas** (near the present border with Liberia).

1. The Spanish trader **Pedro Blanco** built a large slaving base at **Lomboko** in the **1820s and 1830s**, working with local rulers such as the Rogers family.
2. In **1840** the Royal Navy officer **Joseph Denman** destroyed the barracoons at the Gallinas and freed hundreds of captives.
3. Britain then signed **anti-slave-trade treaties** with local chiefs, offering payments in return for ending the trade.

## Sengbe Pieh and the Amistad

![The Amistad story in six steps: Sengbe Pieh and other Mende captured early in 1839 and sold at Lomboko; shipped illegally to Havana, Cuba; revolt on the schooner La Amistad in July 1839; seized off Long Island in August 1839; freed by the US Supreme Court in March 1841; 35 survivors return to Sierra Leone in January 1842](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/amistad-journey.svg)

*Figure 1. The Amistad captives, 1839–1842. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Sengbe Pieh** (known in America as **Joseph Cinqué**) was a **Mende** rice farmer captured early in **1839** and sold at **Lomboko**. He and others were shipped illegally to **Havana, Cuba**, and sold. In **July 1839**, while being taken along the Cuban coast on the schooner **La Amistad**, the captives **rose up**, took control of the ship and ordered the survivors of the crew to sail to Africa. The crew secretly steered north, and in **August 1839** the ship was seized off **Long Island** in the United States.

The case went to the **United States Supreme Court**, where former President **John Quincy Adams** argued for the captives. In **March 1841** the court ruled that they had been **illegally enslaved** and were **free**. In **January 1842**, **35 survivors** returned to Sierra Leone with American missionaries, who began the **Mende Mission** in the south.

## Effects of the slave trade on Sierra Leone

1. **Loss of population**, especially of young men and women.
2. **Wars and raids** between chiefdoms, and general **insecurity**; people built **stockaded towns** for defence.
3. **Growth of powerful middlemen**, including Afro-European families, and of rulers who traded in slaves.
4. **Spread of firearms** and imported goods; decline of some crafts.
5. **Growth of domestic slavery**, which continued long after the export trade ended.
6. **Founding of Freetown** as a home for freed Africans, and the use of Sierra Leone as the base for **ending** the trade.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The slave trade did **not** end in Sierra Leone in 1807. The British Act stopped **British** trading, but **illegal trading** by others continued at places such as the **Gallinas** until the **1840s and 1850s**.
:::

## Key points

- Captives came from **wars, raids, courts and debt**, sold through **middlemen** for European goods.
- **Bunce Island** (from about **1670**) shipped captives to **South Carolina and Georgia**; links with the **Gullah**.
- After **1807**, illegal trade at the **Gallinas** (**Pedro Blanco**, Lomboko) until the Royal Navy attack of **1840**.
- **Sengbe Pieh** led the **Amistad revolt (1839)**; freed by the **US Supreme Court (1841)**; home in **1842**.
- Effects: **depopulation, wars, insecurity, firearms, domestic slavery**, and the founding of **Freetown**.
