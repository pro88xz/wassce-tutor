You can open a heavy door easily by pushing at the handle and hardly at all by pushing near the hinges. Same force, different effect. That turning effect, the moment, explains spanners, see-saws, wheelbarrows and why lorries carrying tall loads overturn.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the moment of a force and state its unit
- state and apply the principle of moments
- state the conditions for equilibrium of a body under parallel forces
- define a couple and calculate its moment

## Moment of a force

:::callout{type=definition}
The **moment** of a force about a point is the product of the **force** and the **perpendicular distance** from the point to the **line of action** of the force:

$$\text{moment} = F \times d$$

Its SI unit is the **newton metre (N m)**.
:::

The point about which the body turns is the **pivot** or **fulcrum**. Moments are either **clockwise** or **anticlockwise**.

- A 20 N force at the end of a 0.25 m spanner, at right angles to it: moment $= 20 \times 0.25 = 5$ N m.
- The same force applied with a 0.5 m spanner: 10 N m. A longer handle gives a bigger turning effect for the same force.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Use the **perpendicular** distance to the **line of action**. If a force of 50 N acts at the end of a 2 m rod but at 30° to the rod, the perpendicular distance is $2\sin 30° = 1$ m, so the moment is $50 \times 1 = 50$ N m, not 100 N m.
:::

## The principle of moments

:::callout{type=definition}
**Principle of moments:** when a body is in equilibrium, the **sum of the clockwise moments** about any point equals the **sum of the anticlockwise moments** about the same point.
:::

![A beam balanced on a central pivot: 300 newtons hang 2 metres to the left and 200 newtons hang 3 metres to the right; 300 times 2 equals 200 times 3](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/moments-seesaw.svg)

*Figure 1. The principle of moments on a balanced beam. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A see-saw is pivoted at its centre. Musa (300 N) sits 2 m from the pivot. Where must Aminata (200 N) sit to balance it?**

$$300 \times 2 = 200 \times d \Rightarrow d = 3\ \text{m}$$

on the other side of the pivot.
:::

## Conditions for equilibrium under parallel forces

For a body acted on by **parallel forces** to be in equilibrium:

1. The **sum of the forces in one direction equals the sum in the opposite direction** (upward forces = downward forces): no resultant force, so no translation.
2. The **sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments** about any point: no resultant moment, so no rotation.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A uniform metre rule weighing 1 N is pivoted at the 50 cm mark. A 2 N weight hangs at the 10 cm mark. Where must a 4 N weight hang to balance the rule? Find the reaction at the pivot.**

The rule's own weight acts at its centre (50 cm), right at the pivot, so it has no moment.

Anticlockwise moment: $2 \times (50 - 10) = 80$ N cm.

Clockwise moment: $4 \times x = 80 \Rightarrow x = 20$ cm to the right of the pivot, so at the **70 cm mark**.

Upward reaction = total downward force $= 1 + 2 + 4 = 7$ N.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A uniform plank 6 m long and weighing 400 N rests on two supports, one at each end. A 600 N man stands 2 m from the left end. Find the reactions at the supports.**

Take moments about the **left** support (so its reaction has no moment). Let the right reaction be $R_B$:

$$R_B \times 6 = 600 \times 2 + 400 \times 3 = 1200 + 1200 = 2400 \Rightarrow R_B = 400\ \text{N}$$

Upward forces = downward forces: $R_A + 400 = 600 + 400 \Rightarrow R_A = 600$ N.

The support nearer the man carries more of the load.
:::

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**Choose the pivot cleverly.** Taking moments about a point where an unknown force acts removes that force from the equation. Then use "upward forces = downward forces" for the second unknown.
:::

## Couples

:::callout{type=definition}
A **couple** is a pair of **equal**, **parallel** and **opposite** forces whose lines of action do **not** coincide. It produces rotation only, with no translation.

$$\text{moment of a couple} = F \times d$$

where $F$ is **one** of the two forces and $d$ is the **perpendicular distance between them**. This moment is also called the **torque** of the couple.
:::

Examples: turning a tap, a steering wheel, a screwdriver, a bottle top; the forces on the coil of an electric motor.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A driver applies forces of 30 N at opposite ends of a steering wheel of diameter 0.4 m. Find the moment of the couple.**

$$\text{moment} = 30 \times 0.4 = 12\ \text{N m}$$
:::

## Key points

- Moment = force × **perpendicular** distance from the pivot (N m).
- Principle of moments: clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments in equilibrium.
- Parallel forces in equilibrium: upward = downward, and clockwise = anticlockwise.
- A couple: two equal, opposite, parallel forces; moment = force × distance between them.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**State the principle of moments** precisely, then solve **beam problems**: a uniform rod with loads and supports, or a metre rule balanced on a knife edge. Remember that a **uniform** rod's weight acts at its **midpoint**. A frequent practical asks you to find the **mass of a metre rule** or of an unknown object by balancing.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is the SI unit of the moment of a force?
2. A 40 N force acts at right angles to a door, 0.8 m from the hinges. Find its moment.
3. State the two conditions for a body to be in equilibrium under parallel forces.

**Answers:** 1. Newton metre (N m). 2. $40 \times 0.8 = 32$ N m. 3. The sum of upward forces equals the sum of downward forces; the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about any point.
