Whether the crop is rice, maize, cassava or tomato, growing it well follows the same principles: choose a good site, prepare the land, use healthy planting material, plant at the right time and spacing, feed and protect the crop, and harvest it at the right stage. Farmers who follow these principles get far higher yields from the same land. This lesson sets out the stages of crop production, methods of planting, plant population and seed rate calculations, and the main cropping systems used in Sierra Leone.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the stages of crop production from site selection to harvest
- describe methods of planting and explain the importance of correct spacing
- calculate plant population and seed rate
- describe and compare cropping systems
- explain the importance of crop rotation and draw up a simple rotation
- interpret a cropping calendar

## Classifying crops

Crops are grouped by their **main use**. The group tells you a lot about how the crop is grown, processed and sold.

![Crops grouped by use with local examples: cereals, legumes, roots and tubers, vegetables, fruits, beverages, oil crops, spices, fibre and latex](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/crop-classification.svg)

Crops can also be grouped by **life span**: **annuals** complete their life in one season (rice, maize, groundnut, tomato); **biennials** take two seasons; **perennials** live for many years (cocoa, oil palm, citrus, mango).

## Stages of crop production

| Stage | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **site selection** | suitable soil, slope and drainage; water supply; access to roads and markets; security |
| **land preparation** | clearing, ploughing, harrowing, ridging or making beds (see the machinery lesson) |
| **choice of planting material** | **improved, certified seed** or healthy cuttings of a suitable variety; free of pests and diseases |
| **seed treatment** | dressing seeds with a fungicide or insecticide; soaking or scarifying hard seeds |
| **planting** | at the right **time**, **depth** and **spacing** |
| **nursery and transplanting** | for crops such as rice, tomato, pepper, cocoa and oil palm |
| **thinning and supplying** | removing extra seedlings; replacing seeds that failed to germinate |
| **fertiliser application** | basal at planting; top dressing later |
| **weeding** | especially in the first 4–6 weeks |
| **water management** | irrigation in the dry season; drainage of waterlogged land |
| **pest and disease control** | integrated pest management |
| **harvesting** | at the right stage of maturity |
| **processing and storage** | drying, threshing, cleaning, safe storage |

:::callout{type=tip}
**Timing matters.** Planting at the **start of the rains** gives the crop the full season of moisture, and many pests build up later. A crop planted three weeks late can lose a large part of its yield even if everything else is done well.
:::

## Methods of planting

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Broadcasting**: scattering seeds evenly by hand over the land (rice in some uplands, pasture grasses). Quick, but uneven spacing and weeding is harder.
- **Drilling**: sowing seeds in a continuous line in furrows (rice, millet).
- **Dibbling**: making holes with a stick or dibber and placing seeds in each (maize, groundnut, cowpea).
- **Transplanting**: raising seedlings in a nursery and moving them to the field (swamp rice, tomato, pepper, cabbage, cocoa, oil palm).
- **Planting vegetative parts**: cuttings (cassava, sweet potato), suckers (plantain), setts (yam, sugarcane).
:::

## Spacing and plant population

:::callout{type=definition}
**Spacing** is the distance between rows and between plants within a row.
:::

Correct spacing gives each plant enough light, water and nutrients without wasting land.

- Too wide: wasted land, more weeds, lower yield per hectare.
- Too close: competition, thin weak plants, more pests and diseases, lower yield per plant.

:::callout{type=definition}
$$\text{plant population per hectare} = \frac{10\,000 \text{ m}^2}{\text{area per stand (m}^2)} \times \text{plants per stand}$$

(1 hectare $= 10\,000$ m².)
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Maize is planted at 75 cm between rows and 25 cm within rows, one plant per hole. Find the plant population per hectare.**

Area per plant $= 0.75 \times 0.25 = 0.1875$ m².

$$\text{population} = \frac{10\,000}{0.1875} \approx 53\,333 \text{ plants per hectare}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Groundnut is planted at 50 cm × 20 cm, with 2 seeds per hole. 100 seeds weigh 50 g. Find the seed rate in kg per hectare.**

Holes per hectare $= \dfrac{10\,000}{0.5 \times 0.2} = 100\,000$. Seeds $= 2 \times 100\,000 = 200\,000$.

Mass of one seed $= 0.5$ g, so seed rate $= 200\,000 \times 0.5 = 100\,000$ g $=$ **100 kg per hectare**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Mixing centimetres and metres.** Always convert spacing to **metres** before dividing into 10 000 m². 75 cm × 25 cm is $0.75 \times 0.25$ m², not $75 \times 25$.
:::

## Cropping systems

| System | Meaning | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **monocropping** (sole cropping) | one crop on the land at a time | easy mechanization, weeding and harvest | more pest build-up; total failure risk |
| **mixed cropping** | two or more crops together with **no definite pattern** | insurance against failure; better use of land; covers soil | hard to mechanize or weed |
| **intercropping** | two or more crops in **definite rows or patterns** (e.g. maize with cowpea) | as mixed cropping, but easier to manage; legumes add nitrogen | competition if badly planned |
| **relay cropping** | planting a second crop **before** the first is harvested | uses the season fully | competition at overlap |
| **crop rotation** | growing **different crops in sequence** on the same land | maintains fertility, breaks pest and disease cycles, controls weeds | needs planning and land |
| **shifting cultivation / bush fallow** | farming land for one to three years, then leaving it to rest | cheap; fertility restored naturally | needs much land; short fallows now reduce yields |

### Crop rotation

**Principles:** alternate **deep- and shallow-rooted** crops; alternate **heavy feeders** (maize) with **legumes**; do not follow a crop with another of the **same family** (which shares pests and diseases); include a **fallow or cover crop**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Suggest a four-year rotation for an upland farm.**

| Year | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | upland rice or maize | heavy feeder; uses fertility from fallow |
| 2 | groundnut or cowpea | legume: adds nitrogen; different pests |
| 3 | cassava | deep-rooted; tolerant of poorer soil |
| 4 | fallow or cover crop (e.g. *Mucuna*) | restores organic matter and structure |
:::

## The cropping calendar

![A cropping calendar for upland rice: brushing and felling in January to March, land preparation in March and April, planting in May and June, weeding from June to August, bird scaring in August and September, and harvesting from September to November](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/cropping-calendar.svg)

A **cropping calendar** shows when each operation should be done. In Sierra Leone, the year is divided into a **dry season** (about November to April) and a **rainy season** (about May to October), and most rain-fed crops are planted at the start of the rains.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**The "hungry season".** In many farming communities, food from the last harvest runs short in July and August, before the new rice is ready. Planting an early-maturing variety, growing **cassava and sweet potato** that can be harvested when needed, and storing grain well help families through this period. A good cropping plan is also a food security plan.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. List six stages of crop production in order."}
For example: site selection, land preparation, choice of planting material, planting, weeding and fertiliser application, pest and disease control, harvesting, processing and storage (any six in a sensible order).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Distinguish between dibbling and drilling."}
**Dibbling** places seeds in individual holes made with a stick; **drilling** sows seeds in a continuous line along a furrow.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Cassava is planted at 1 m × 1 m. Find the plant population per hectare."}
$10\,000 \div (1 \times 1) = 10\,000$ plants per hectare.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Distinguish between mixed cropping and intercropping."}
Both grow two or more crops together. **Mixed cropping** has no definite pattern; **intercropping** uses definite rows or arrangements.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Give three advantages of crop rotation."}
Any three: maintains soil fertility (legumes add nitrogen); breaks pest and disease cycles; controls weeds; makes better use of soil nutrients at different depths; reduces erosion.
:::

## Key points

- Stages: **site → land preparation → planting material → planting → care (weeding, fertiliser, water, pests) → harvest → processing and storage**.
- Planting methods: **broadcasting, drilling, dibbling, transplanting, vegetative parts**.
- Population $= \dfrac{10\,000}{\text{area per stand}} \times$ plants per stand; work in **metres**.
- Systems: **mono, mixed, inter, relay cropping, rotation, bush fallow**.
- Rotation alternates **heavy feeders, legumes, deep- and shallow-rooted crops, fallow**.
