If households save more, do firms sell less? If the government spends more on roads, does national income grow? The circular flow has leaks and injections that decide whether an economy expands or shrinks. This lesson completes the picture, then looks at what national income figures are used for and why they are hard to measure in West Africa.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain leakages and injections in the circular flow and the condition for equilibrium
- explain the multiplier in simple terms
- state the uses of national income statistics
- explain the difficulties of measuring national income in developing countries
- explain why income per head is an imperfect measure of welfare, and what determines the size of national income

## Leakages and injections

In a real economy, not all income is spent on home-produced goods.

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **leakage (withdrawal)** is income that is **not passed on** in spending on domestic output: **saving (S)**, **taxes (T)** and **imports (M)**.
- An **injection** is spending that **enters** the circular flow from outside the household–firm loop: **investment (I)**, **government spending (G)** and **exports (X)**.
:::

![Diagram of the circular flow of income in the centre, with saving, taxes and imports leaking out on the left and investment, government spending and exports injected on the right](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/leakages-injections.svg)

*Figure 1. Leakages and injections in the circular flow. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| If… | National income… |
|---|---|
| injections > leakages ($I + G + X > S + T + M$) | **rises** |
| injections < leakages | **falls** |
| injections = leakages | is in **equilibrium** |

### The multiplier

An increase in injections raises national income by **more than** the original spending, because each person's spending becomes another person's income.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The government spends NLe 100 million on a new road.** The contractors' workers receive NLe 100 million and spend 80% of it (NLe 80 million) in local shops; the shopkeepers spend 80% of that (NLe 64 million), and so on.

The **multiplier** $= \dfrac{1}{\text{fraction leaked}} = \dfrac{1}{1 - 0.8} = 5$.

National income rises by $100 \times 5 = $ **NLe 500 million**.
:::

The more income leaks out in saving, taxes and imports, the **smaller** the multiplier. In Sierra Leone, where much spending goes on imports, a lot of the extra income leaks abroad.

## Uses of national income statistics

1. To **measure economic growth** over time (using real GDP).
2. To **compare living standards** between countries (income per head).
3. To show the **structure of the economy**: the shares of agriculture, mining, industry and services.
4. To help the government **plan** and set economic policy.
5. To estimate **tax revenue** and plan the budget.
6. To guide **international bodies** (World Bank, IMF) in classifying countries and allocating aid and loans.
7. To help **firms** judge the size of markets and plan investment.

## Difficulties of measuring national income in West Africa

1. **Subsistence output**: much food is grown and eaten by the farmer's family and never sold, so it has no market price.
2. **The informal sector**: many traders, artisans and transport operators keep no records.
3. **Illiteracy and poor record keeping** by small producers.
4. **Double counting**: separating final from intermediate goods is difficult.
5. **Inadequate statistics staff and funds**.
6. **Changing prices**: inflation makes comparisons between years misleading unless real values are used.
7. **Illegal and unrecorded activities**, such as smuggling of diamonds or goods across borders.
8. **Valuing government services** such as defence and free education, which are not sold.
9. **Unreliable population figures**, which distort income per head.

## Income per head and welfare

A higher income per head does not always mean people are better off:

- **Unequal distribution**: a few may be very rich while most remain poor.
- **Composition of output**: spending on weapons adds to GDP but not to welfare.
- **Non-market activities** (housework, subsistence farming, community work) are left out.
- **Leisure and working conditions**: longer hours raise GDP but may lower welfare.
- **Environmental damage** from mining or logging is not subtracted.
- **Prices and exchange rates** make international comparisons unreliable.
- **Quality of life**: life expectancy, literacy and freedom are not captured. This is why the **Human Development Index** combines income with health and education (see *Economic Growth and Development*).

## What determines the size of national income?

The **quantity and quality of factors of production** (land and natural resources, labour and its skills, capital, entrepreneurship); the **state of technology**; **political stability**; the **efficiency of government**; **infrastructure**; the **terms of trade** and world prices of exports; and the level of **total spending** in the economy.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Because so much of Sierra Leone's output is subsistence farming and informal trade, official GDP figures **understate** what is really produced. Statistics Sierra Leone estimates these activities, but the figures are less reliable than in countries where most output is sold and recorded.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Saving is a **leakage**, but investment is an **injection**. Saving in a bank helps national income only if the bank lends it to firms that **invest** it.
:::

## Key points

- **Leakages**: saving, taxes, imports. **Injections**: investment, government spending, exports.
- Income **rises** when injections exceed leakages; equilibrium when they are equal.
- The **multiplier** = 1 ÷ fraction of income leaked.
- National income figures measure **growth, living standards and structure**, and guide **planning**.
- Measurement is hard because of **subsistence output, the informal sector, poor records and inflation**.
- Income per head is a limited measure of **welfare**.
