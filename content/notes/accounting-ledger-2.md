**Balancing off a ledger account** means finding the **difference between the two sides** at the end of a period, entering it as the **balance carried down (c/d)** on the smaller side so both sides total the same, and then **bringing the balance down (b/d)** on the opposite side at the start of the next period. A **debit balance** usually shows an **asset or expense** (or an amount owed **to** the business); a **credit balance** shows a **liability, capital or income** (or an amount owed **by** the business). This lesson shows how to balance accounts step by step and how to interpret their balances.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why ledger accounts are balanced
- balance off a ledger account using balance c/d and balance b/d
- interpret debit and credit balances
- close accounts that have equal totals
- explain how balances are used in the trial balance and final accounts

## Why accounts are balanced

1. To find **how much is owed** to or by each person.
2. To find the **total of each income and expense** for the period.
3. To find the **value of each asset and liability**.
4. To provide the figures for the **trial balance** and **final accounts**.

## Steps in balancing

![A cash account for April 2026 balanced off: debits of capital 20,000 and sales 8,000 total 28,000; credits of purchases 6,500, rent 2,000, wages 1,500 and balance carried down 18,000 total 28,000; balance brought down 18,000 on 1 May](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/balancing-account.svg)

*Figure 1. Balancing off a cash account. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

1. **Add up** each side separately.
2. Find the **difference** between the two totals.
3. Enter the difference on the **smaller side** as **"Balance c/d"** (carried down), dated the last day of the period.
4. **Total both sides**: the totals are now equal. Write them on the same line, double-underlined.
5. Enter the same figure on the **opposite side**, below the totals, as **"Balance b/d"** (brought down), dated the first day of the next period.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Balance Kamara's account at 31 May 2026 (NLe).**

| Dr | NLe | Cr | NLe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (3 May) | 7,000 | Returns in (8 May) | 800 |
| Sales (19 May) | 4,500 | Bank (21 May) | 6,000 |
| | | Balance c/d | 4,700 |
| **Total** | **11,500** | **Total** | **11,500** |
| Balance b/d (1 Jun) | 4,700 | | |

**Working:** debit total $= 7\,000 + 4\,500 = 11\,500$; credit items $= 800 + 6\,000 = 6\,800$; balance $= 11\,500 - 6\,800 = 4\,700$.

The **debit balance** of NLe 4,700 means Kamara **owes** the business NLe 4,700: a **trade receivable**.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Balance Sesay's account at 31 May (NLe): credit purchases 8,000 and 3,000; debit returns outwards 500 and payment by cheque 7,500.**

Credit total $= 8\,000 + 3\,000 = 11\,000$; debit items $= 500 + 7\,500 = 8\,000$.

Balance c/d on the **debit** side $= 11\,000 - 8\,000 = 3\,000$; balance b/d on the **credit** side on 1 June $= 3\,000$.

The **credit balance** means the business **owes** Sesay NLe 3,000: a **trade payable**.
:::

## Interpreting balances

| Balance | Usually means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | an **asset**, an **expense**, drawings, or someone **owes us** | cash, motor van, wages, Kamara (debtor) |
| **Credit** | a **liability**, **capital**, an **income**, or **we owe** someone | loan, capital, sales, Sesay (creditor) |

## Accounts with one entry or equal sides

- If an account has **only one entry**, that amount is the balance: no balancing is needed; the entry is simply brought into the trial balance.
- If both sides are **equal**, the account is **closed**: total both sides and double-underline. There is no balance to bring down.

## Where balances go next

- All balances are listed in the **trial balance** to check the double entry.
- **Income and expense** balances go to the **income statement**.
- **Asset, liability and capital** balances go to the **statement of financial position**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The balance is carried down on the **smaller** side but brought down on the **larger** side. Kamara's account has a larger debit side, so its balance is **c/d on the credit side** and **b/d on the debit side**, showing a debit balance.
:::

## Key points

- **Balance c/d** goes on the **smaller** side so both totals agree; **balance b/d** goes on the **opposite** side in the next period.
- A **debit balance** is an asset, expense or debtor; a **credit balance** is a liability, capital, income or creditor.
- Accounts with **equal sides** are closed with no balance.
- Balances feed the **trial balance** and the **final accounts**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Almost every ledger question ends with **"balance the accounts"**. Show **balance c/d**, equal **totals** on the same line, and **balance b/d** dated the first day of the next period. Then be ready to **say what each balance means** (debtor, creditor, asset, expense).
:::

## Quick check

1. What is meant by balance c/d and balance b/d?
2. A customer's account has debits of 9,000 and 2,000 and credits of 7,500. Find the balance and say what it means.
3. On which side is the balance carried down when the debit side is larger?
4. What does a credit balance on a supplier's account show?
5. What happens to an account whose two sides are equal?

**Answers:**

1. Balance c/d (carried down) is the difference entered on the smaller side to make both sides equal; balance b/d (brought down) is the same amount entered on the opposite side at the start of the next period.
2. $11\,000 - 7\,500 = 3\,500$: a debit balance of NLe 3,500, so the customer owes the business NLe 3,500.
3. On the credit side.
4. That the business owes the supplier money (a trade payable).
5. It is closed: both sides are totalled and there is no balance to bring down.
