**Control accounts** (total accounts) are accounts kept in the general ledger that summarise, **in totals**, all the entries made in a subsidiary ledger. The **sales ledger control account** summarises all the customers' accounts, and its balance should equal the **total of all the individual debtor balances**; the **purchases ledger control account** does the same for suppliers. Because they are written up from the **totals in the books of original entry**, control accounts give a quick **arithmetical check** on each ledger and help to locate errors and fraud. This lesson explains their purposes and how to prepare them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and purposes of control accounts
- state the source of each entry in a control account
- prepare a sales ledger control account
- prepare a purchases ledger control account
- explain why some items never appear in a control account

## Purposes of control accounts

1. To check the **arithmetical accuracy** of each subsidiary ledger.
2. To **locate errors** quickly: if the control account and ledger disagree, the error is in that ledger.
3. To help prevent **fraud**, when the control account is kept by a different clerk from the ledger.
4. To give **total receivables and payables** at once, for final accounts and management.
5. To help prepare accounts from **incomplete records**.

## Where the figures come from

![Totals from the sales day book, returns inwards book, cash book and general journal are posted to the sales ledger control account, whose balance should agree with the total of customers' balances in the sales ledger](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/control-account-sources.svg)

*Figure 1. Sources of the figures in a sales ledger control account. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Sales ledger control account | Source | Side |
|---|---|---|
| opening receivables | last period's balance | Dr |
| credit sales | sales day book total | Dr |
| dishonoured cheques | cash book | Dr |
| interest charged on overdue accounts | journal | Dr |
| cash and cheques received | cash book | Cr |
| discount allowed | cash book discount column | Cr |
| returns inwards | returns inwards book | Cr |
| bad debts written off | journal | Cr |
| set-offs (contras) against the purchases ledger | journal | Cr |
| closing receivables | balancing figure | Cr (c/d) |

The **purchases ledger control account** is the mirror image: credit purchases, opening payables and refunds on the **credit** side; payments, discount received, returns outwards and set-offs on the **debit** side.

## Worked example: sales ledger control account

![A sales ledger control account for January 2026: debits of balance b/d 45,000, credit sales 120,000, dishonoured cheques 1,500 and interest 300; credits of cash and cheques 98,000, discount allowed 2,400, returns inwards 3,600, bad debts 1,800, set-off 2,000 and balance c/d 59,000, each side totalling 166,800](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/sales-ledger-control.svg)

*Figure 2. A completed sales ledger control account. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Working:**

- Debits $= 45\,000 + 120\,000 + 1\,500 + 300 = 166\,800$
- Credits before the balance $= 98\,000 + 2\,400 + 3\,600 + 1\,800 + 2\,000 = 107\,800$
- Balance c/d $= 166\,800 - 107\,800 = 59\,000$

The total of the customers' balances listed from the sales ledger should also be **NLe 59,000**.

## Worked example: purchases ledger control account

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**January 2026 (NLe): payables at 1 January 30,000; credit purchases 85,000; paid to suppliers 72,000; discount received 1,800; returns outwards 2,200; set-off against the sales ledger 2,000. Find the closing balance.**

- **Credit side:** balance b/d 30,000; credit purchases 85,000; total **115,000**
- **Debit side:** bank 72,000; discount received 1,800; returns outwards 2,200; set-off 2,000; total $78\,000$
- **Balance c/d** (debit) $= 115\,000 - 78\,000 = 37\,000$

The business owes its suppliers **NLe 37,000** in total; this is brought down as a **credit** balance on 1 February.
:::

## Items that do not appear

- **Cash sales and cash purchases**: they never pass through a customer's or supplier's account.
- **Trade discount**: it is deducted on the invoice and not recorded.
- **Provision for doubtful debts**: it is a separate account; only actual **bad debts** written off go in the control account.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not put **discount received** in the sales ledger control account, or **discount allowed** in the purchases ledger control account. Discount allowed relates to **customers** (sales ledger); discount received relates to **suppliers** (purchases ledger).
:::

## Key points

- A **control account** summarises a whole ledger in **totals** from the books of original entry.
- Its balance should equal the **total of the individual balances** in that ledger.
- Sales ledger control: **credit sales** debit; **receipts, discount allowed, returns inwards, bad debts, set-offs** credit.
- Purchases ledger control: **credit purchases** credit; **payments, discount received, returns outwards, set-offs** debit.
- Cash sales, trade discount and the provision for doubtful debts are **left out**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **prepare sales and purchases ledger control accounts** from a list of totals, to **state their purposes**, and to identify which items are **not** included. Examiners often include cash sales or the provision for doubtful debts as distractors.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is a control account?
2. From which book does the credit sales figure come?
3. Give two purposes of control accounts.
4. On which side of the purchases ledger control account is discount received?
5. Why are cash sales left out of the sales ledger control account?

**Answers:**

1. An account in the general ledger that summarises in totals all the entries in a subsidiary ledger, so its balance should equal the total of that ledger's balances.
2. The sales day book.
3. Any two: check the arithmetical accuracy of the ledger; locate errors; prevent fraud; give total receivables or payables quickly; help with incomplete records.
4. The debit side.
5. Because cash sales are never recorded in customers' personal accounts.
