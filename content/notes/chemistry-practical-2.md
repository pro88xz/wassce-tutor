Most marks in the WASSCE practical paper are won or lost on technique: reading a burette correctly, keeping results to the right number of decimal places, choosing the right indicator, and working safely. This lesson covers the **skills** behind volumetric analysis and other common practical work, so that your results are accurate and your report earns full marks. (The step-by-step titration method and its calculations are in the **Acid–Base Titration** topic.)

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- choose the right measuring apparatus for a given accuracy
- read a burette and pipette correctly and avoid parallax error
- record titration results in the standard table with concordant values
- prepare a standard solution
- identify sources of error and suggest improvements
- follow laboratory safety rules and handle chemicals safely

## Choosing apparatus

| Apparatus | Measures | Typical precision | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Burette** (50 cm³) | Variable volumes delivered | ±0.05 cm³ (read to 2 d.p.) | Adding titrant |
| **Pipette** (25.0 cm³) | One fixed volume | ±0.05 cm³ | Measuring the aliquot |
| **Volumetric flask** (250 cm³) | One fixed volume | ±0.2 cm³ | Making standard solutions |
| **Measuring cylinder** | Approximate volumes | ±0.5 cm³ or more | Adding excess reagent; not for titration |
| **Beaker** | Rough volumes only | Poor | Mixing, heating |
| **Electronic balance** | Mass | ±0.01 g or ±0.001 g | Weighing solids |
| **Thermometer** | Temperature | ±0.5 °C (0–110 °C) | Rates, energy changes |

Use the **most precise** apparatus that the job needs: a pipette, not a measuring cylinder, for the 25.0 cm³ portion in a titration.

## Reading volumes

![Close-up of a burette scale with the meniscus at 22.80 cm³; an eye level with the meniscus reads correctly, an eye above reads too small and an eye below reads too large](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/burette-reading.svg)

*Figure 1. Avoiding parallax error when reading a burette. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- Read at the **bottom of the meniscus** (for clear, colourless liquids), with your **eye level** with it. Reading from above or below gives **parallax error**.
- A burette scale **increases downwards**; record readings to **two decimal places**, with the last figure 0 or 5 (e.g. 22.80, 23.45).
- For **deeply coloured** solutions such as KMnO₄, read the **top** of the meniscus.
- Use a **white tile** under the flask and a **white card** behind the burette to see colour changes and the meniscus clearly.
- Remove the funnel before titrating, and make sure the **jet is full** (no air bubble) before taking the first reading.

## Recording titration results

| | Rough | 1st | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Final reading (cm³)** | 24.10 | 23.85 | 23.90 | 24.60 |
| **Initial reading (cm³)** | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.10 | 0.55 |
| **Volume used (cm³)** | 24.10 | 23.85 | 23.80 | 24.05 |

- **Concordant** titres agree within **0.10 cm³** (some examiners accept 0.20).
- Here 23.85 and 23.80 are concordant. The rough titre is never used, and 24.05 is more than 0.10 cm³ from both, so it is left out.
- Average titre $= (23.85 + 23.80) \div 2 = 23.83$ cm³ (give 2 decimal places).
- State the **indicator** and the **colour change at the end point** (e.g. methyl orange: yellow → orange).

Also report the **volume of pipette** used (25.0 cm³), and show **every step** of the calculation with units.

## Preparing a standard solution

:::callout{type=definition}
A **standard solution** is a solution whose **concentration is known accurately**. A **primary standard** is a pure, stable substance that can be weighed accurately to make one (for example anhydrous sodium carbonate or oxalic acid).
:::

1. Weigh the solid accurately in a **weighing bottle** (for example 2.65 g of anhydrous Na₂CO₃).
2. Dissolve it in a little **distilled water** in a beaker; rinse the bottle and add the rinsings.
3. Transfer the solution to a **250 cm³ volumetric flask** through a funnel, **rinsing** the beaker and funnel into the flask.
4. Add distilled water until the **bottom of the meniscus is on the mark**, adding the last drops with a dropper.
5. Stopper and **invert** the flask several times to mix.

Concentration $= (2.65 \div 106) \times (1000 \div 250) = 0.100$ mol/dm³.

## Rinsing correctly

| Apparatus | Rinse last with | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Burette** | The solution it will contain (e.g. the acid) | Water would dilute the acid |
| **Pipette** | The solution it will measure (e.g. the base) | Water would dilute the base |
| **Conical flask** | **Distilled water only** | The number of moles pipetted must not change; water does not affect it |

## Sources of error and improvements

| Error | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Parallax when reading | Wrong volume | Eye level with the meniscus |
| Air bubble in burette jet | Titre too large | Run some liquid through before the first reading |
| Overshooting the end point | Titre too large | Add dropwise near the end point; swirl constantly |
| Burette or pipette rinsed with water | Solutions diluted | Rinse with the solution to be used |
| Solid lost when transferring | Concentration too low | Rinse all apparatus into the flask |
| Heat lost to surroundings (energy experiments) | Temperature rise too small | Insulate; use a lid; use a polystyrene cup |

## Other practical skills

- **Heating:** heat test tubes at an angle, pointing away from people; heat solids gently at first.
- **Filtration:** fold the filter paper into a cone, wet it to stick, and keep the liquid below the top of the paper.
- **Evaporation and crystallisation:** heat to concentrate, then leave to cool so crystals form; test with a glass rod for a saturated solution.
- **Drawing graphs:** label axes with quantities and **units**, choose sensible scales (graph covering more than half the paper), plot points accurately, and draw a **smooth curve or best-fit line**.

## Laboratory safety

- Wear **safety goggles** (and a lab coat); tie back long hair.
- Never **pipette by mouth**: use a **pipette filler**.
- Always **add concentrated acid to water**, slowly, never water to acid.
- Use a **fume cupboard** for toxic gases (chlorine, SO₂, NO₂, H₂S).
- Keep **flammable liquids** (ethanol, ether) away from flames; heat them in a water bath.
- Know the **hazard symbols**: corrosive, toxic, flammable, oxidising, harmful/irritant.
- Clean up spills at once: neutralise acid spills with **sodium hydrogencarbonate**; wash skin with plenty of water.
- Dispose of chemicals as instructed; never pour heavy-metal solutions down the sink.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Spotting the error.** A student's titres were 24.60, 23.80 and 23.85 cm³. She rinsed the conical flask with the alkali before each titration. Which result should be left out, and how does her rinsing affect the titre?

- 24.60 is far from the others: it is the **rough** (or inaccurate) titre and is left out. Average of concordant titres $= 23.83$ cm³.
- Rinsing the flask with **alkali** leaves extra alkali in it, so **more acid** is needed: the titres are **too large**. The flask should be rinsed with **distilled water** only.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Where school laboratories lack electronic balances or enough burettes, students in Sierra Leone often share apparatus or use improvised kits. The principles still apply: use the **most precise** measuring tool available, **repeat** measurements until they agree, and record **exactly what you measured**. Good technique with simple apparatus beats careless use of expensive equipment.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not round burette readings to one decimal place or write whole numbers. **23.8** loses a mark; write **23.80**. Do not average all titres blindly: use only the **concordant** ones.
:::

## Key points

- Use a **pipette** and **burette** (±0.05 cm³) for titrations; a **volumetric flask** for standard solutions; a measuring cylinder only for rough volumes.
- Read the **bottom of the meniscus at eye level**; record burette readings to **2 decimal places**.
- Use **concordant** titres (within 0.10 cm³) to find the average; state the indicator and its colour change.
- Rinse the burette and pipette with **their own solutions**, the conical flask with **distilled water**.
- A **standard solution** is made by weighing a primary standard, dissolving, transferring with rinsings and making up to the mark.
- Follow **safety rules**: goggles, pipette filler, acid into water, fume cupboard.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **complete a titration results table**, to **select concordant titres and average them**, to **state indicators and end-point colours**, to **describe how to prepare a standard solution**, to **name apparatus** and justify the choice, and to **state precautions and sources of error**. Neat, complete recording with correct decimal places earns many marks.
:::

## Quick check

1. To how many decimal places should burette readings be recorded?
2. Why is the conical flask rinsed with distilled water, not with the solution it will hold?
3. Which titres are concordant: 25.40, 25.05, 25.10?
4. Give two safety precautions when diluting concentrated sulphuric acid.

**Answers:**

1. Two (e.g. 23.80 cm³).
2. Water does not change the number of moles of solute pipetted into it; rinsing with the solution would add extra solute.
3. 25.05 and 25.10.
4. Any two: wear goggles; add the acid slowly to water (never water to acid); stir and cool; wear gloves.
