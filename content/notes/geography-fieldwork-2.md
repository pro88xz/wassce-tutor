**Surveying** is measuring and recording the positions of features on the ground so that an accurate **plan or map** can be drawn. The simple survey methods studied for WASSCE are **chain survey**, where distances are measured along a straight **chain line** and features are fixed by **offsets** at right angles; **prismatic compass survey (traversing)**, where **bearings** and distances are measured from station to station; and **plane table survey**, where the map is drawn directly in the field. This lesson explains the equipment, procedure, booking and plotting of each.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define surveying and state its uses
- describe the equipment used in chain survey
- describe the procedure of a chain survey, including offsets and booking
- describe a prismatic compass traverse and the use of back bearings
- describe plane table survey
- compare the advantages and disadvantages of the methods

## Uses of surveying

Surveys are used to make **maps and plans**, mark **plot and farm boundaries**, plan **roads, buildings and drainage**, measure **areas**, and record **land use**.

## Chain survey

![A chain line from station A to station B marked every 20 metres, with dashed offsets at right angles to two corners of a building and to a tree, and a path and stream crossing the line](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/chain-survey.svg)

*Figure 1. A chain survey: the chain line and right-angle offsets. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

### Equipment

| Equipment | Use |
|---|---|
| **Chain** (20 m or 30 m) or **measuring tape** | measures distances along the chain line |
| **Ranging poles** | mark the stations and keep the chain line straight |
| **Arrows (pins)** | mark the end of each chain length |
| **Offset staff** or short tape | measures offsets |
| **Cross staff** or optical square | sets out right angles for offsets |
| **Field book**, pencil | records (books) measurements |
| **Pegs** | mark stations that must remain |

### Procedure

1. **Reconnaissance:** walk over the area and choose the **stations** and lines, making a rough sketch.
2. **Range** the line: place ranging poles at the stations so the chain line is straight.
3. **Chain** along the line from station A to B, counting chain lengths with arrows; the distance from the start is the **chainage**.
4. Measure **offsets** at **right angles** from the chain line to each feature (building corners, trees, paths, streams), recording the **chainage** and **offset length**.
5. For a larger area, divide it into **well-shaped triangles** (no angle smaller than about 30°) and chain every side; **check lines** confirm accuracy.
6. **Book** every measurement in the field book, usually written from the bottom of the page upwards, with the chain line drawn as a column.
7. **Plot** the survey at a suitable scale: draw the chain line, mark the chainages, draw the offsets and join the features.

**Advantages:** simple and cheap equipment; accurate for small, fairly flat areas.

**Disadvantages:** slow; difficult in thick bush, steep slopes, swamps and built-up areas; not suitable for large areas.

## Prismatic compass survey (traversing)

![The sixteen compass points and a bearing measured clockwise from north, with its back bearing](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/compass-bearings.svg)

*Figure 2. Bearings and back bearings are the basis of a compass traverse. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
A **traverse** is a series of connected straight lines (legs) between stations.
:::

At each station:

1. Sight the next station with the **prismatic compass** and read its **forward bearing**.
2. **Measure the distance** to the next station with a tape or chain.
3. At the next station, sight back to the previous one and read the **back bearing**, which should differ from the forward bearing by **180°**. A difference from 180° warns of an error, often caused by **local magnetic attraction** from iron objects, vehicles or power lines.
4. Continue around the area. In an **open traverse** the line does not return to the start (as along a road or river); in a **closed traverse** it returns to the starting station.
5. **Plot** each leg with a protractor and scale ruler. In a closed traverse, the gap between the start and the plotted end is the **closing error**, which is shared out among the legs.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The forward bearing of a leg from station P to station Q is 125°. What back bearing should be read at Q?**

Back bearing $= 125° + 180° = 305°$.

If the reading at Q is 309°, there is a 4° error, possibly from local magnetic attraction.
:::

**Advantages:** fast; suitable for large areas, roads and rivers; works in rough country.
**Disadvantages:** affected by magnetic attraction; less accurate than chain survey for small details.

## Plane table survey

:::callout{type=definition}
A **plane table** is a drawing board on a tripod, with an **alidade** (sighting rule), a spirit level and a trough compass.
:::

The map is **drawn directly in the field** by sighting features and drawing rays towards them.

- **Radiation:** from one station, draw rays to each feature and measure the distances.
- **Intersection:** draw rays to each feature from two stations; where the rays cross fixes the feature, so few distances need measuring.

**Advantages:** the map is made on the spot, so mistakes can be seen and corrected at once; quick for detail.
**Disadvantages:** heavy equipment; cannot be used in rain or wind; less accurate over large areas.

## Comparing the methods

| | Chain survey | Compass traverse | Plane table |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Measures** | distances and offsets | bearings and distances | directions drawn on the board |
| **Best for** | small, flat, open areas | long routes, large areas | filling in detail |
| **Main problem** | slow; needs open ground | magnetic attraction | weather; heavy |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Offsets must be measured at **right angles** to the chain line. A slanting offset puts the feature in the wrong position on the plan.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Chain survey**: a survey of a small, flat, open area by measuring distances along chain lines and offsets from them.
- **Offset**: a short measurement at right angles from the chain line to a feature.
- **Chainage**: the distance along the chain line from the starting station.
- **Station**: a fixed point marked at the end of a survey line.
- **Field book**: the book in which survey measurements are recorded.
- **Prismatic compass**: a compass with a prism for reading bearings accurately in a survey.
:::

## Key points

- **Surveying** measures and records positions to make **plans and maps**.
- **Chain survey:** chain, ranging poles, arrows, offset staff, field book; **chainage** along the line and **right-angle offsets** to features; triangles and check lines.
- **Compass traverse:** forward and **back bearings** (differ by 180°), distances; open and closed traverses; **closing error**; magnetic attraction.
- **Plane table:** map drawn in the field by **radiation** or **intersection**.

## Quick check

1. What is an offset in chain survey?
2. What is the use of ranging poles?
3. Why should forward and back bearings differ by 180°?
4. What is local magnetic attraction?
5. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of plane table survey.

**Answers:**

1. A distance measured at right angles from the chain line to a feature.
2. To mark the stations and keep the chain line straight.
3. Because the back bearing is the reverse direction of the same line.
4. The disturbance of a compass needle by nearby iron objects, vehicles or power lines, causing wrong bearings.
5. Advantage: the map is drawn on the spot so errors are seen at once. Disadvantage: heavy equipment, or it cannot be used in rain or wind.
