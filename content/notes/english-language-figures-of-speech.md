*"The wind whispered through the palm trees."* Wind cannot whisper; but the sentence makes you **hear** it, soft and secret. That is a figure of speech: language used in an unusual way to create a picture, a feeling or an effect. WASSCE asks you to **identify** figures of speech, to **explain** their meaning and to **use** them in your own writing. This lesson covers every figure you are likely to meet, how to tell similar ones apart, what effect each creates, and how to answer the questions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- identify the common figures of speech and tell similar ones apart
- explain what a figurative expression means in plain language
- describe the effect a figure of speech has on the reader
- use figures of speech effectively in your own writing

## Figures of comparison

:::callout{type=definition}
| Figure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | compares two different things using **like** or **as** | *Her voice was **as sweet as honey**.* / *He ran **like a cheetah**.* |
| **Metaphor** | says one thing **is** another, without *like* or *as* | *The classroom **was an oven**.* / *Time **is a thief**.* |
| **Personification** | gives **human** qualities or actions to things, animals or ideas | *The **sun smiled** on the farmers.* / *Fear **gripped** the village.* |
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
**Simile or metaphor?** Look for *like* or *as*. *"He is as strong as a lion"* is a simile. *"He is a lion in battle"* is a metaphor. Both compare a man to a lion; only the simile says so openly.
:::

## Figures of exaggeration and contrast

| Figure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperbole** | deliberate **exaggeration** for effect | *I have told you **a million times**.* / *This bag weighs **a ton**.* |
| **Litotes** (understatement) | says something is **not** the opposite, to suggest it strongly | *She is **not a bad** singer.* (She sings well.) |
| **Oxymoron** | two **opposite** words side by side | *a **deafening silence***, *a **living death***, *bitter-sweet* |
| **Paradox** | a statement that seems **contradictory** but holds a truth | *The more you learn, the more you realise how little you know.* |
| **Antithesis** | **contrasting** ideas in balanced phrases | *Man proposes, God disposes.* / *Speech is silver, silence is golden.* |
| **Irony** | the **opposite** of what is said, meant or expected | *"What a lovely day!" he said, as the rain poured.* |
| **Sarcasm** | bitter irony meant to **mock or hurt** | *"Oh, brilliant work,"* she said, looking at the failed test. |

## Figures of sound

| Figure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alliteration** | the same **consonant sound** at the start of nearby words | ***B**usy, **b**ustling **B**o* / ***P**eter **P**iper **p**icked…* |
| **Assonance** | the same **vowel sound** in nearby words | *The r**ai**n in Sp**ai**n st**ay**s m**ai**nly…* |
| **Onomatopoeia** | words that **sound like** their meaning | *buzz, hiss, crack, bang, splash, sizzle* |

## Figures of substitution and address

| Figure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Euphemism** | a **gentle** way to say something unpleasant | *He **passed away*** (died). *She is **between jobs*** (unemployed). |
| **Metonymy** | names something by a thing **closely associated** with it | ***The crown*** (the king or queen). ***State House** announced…* (the President's office). |
| **Synecdoche** | a **part** stands for the whole (or the reverse) | *All **hands** on deck* (all sailors). *She has **mouths** to feed* (people). |
| **Apostrophe** | addressing someone **absent** or something **not human** | *O Death, where is thy sting?* / *Sierra Leone, land that we love…* |
| **Rhetorical question** | a question asked for **effect**, not for an answer | *Who does not want peace?* |
| **Climax** | ideas arranged in **rising** importance | *I came, I saw, I conquered.* |
| **Anticlimax** | a sudden **drop** from important to trivial | *He lost his house, his job, and his favourite pen.* |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Confusing metonymy and synecdoche.** In **synecdoche**, the part is **part of** the whole (*hands* are part of sailors). In **metonymy**, the thing is **associated with** it but not part of it (*the crown* is worn by the monarch but is not part of her).
:::

## Explaining the effect

Identifying the figure is half the answer. Strong answers also say **what it does**:

:::callout{type=worked-example}
*"The old lorry groaned and coughed its way up the hill."*

| Step | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Name it** | personification (the lorry *groans* and *coughs* like a sick old person) |
| **Meaning** | the lorry moved up the hill slowly, with great difficulty and noise |
| **Effect** | it makes the reader hear the strain of the engine and feel sympathy, almost as if the lorry were an old man struggling |
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leonean speech and music use figures of speech constantly. Praise singers use **hyperbole** (*"the man whose wealth cannot be counted"*); the national anthem uses **apostrophe** (*"High we exalt thee, realm of the free"*); proverbs use **metaphor** (*"Monkey work, baboon chop"*). Noticing figures in what you hear every day is excellent practice.
:::

## Using figures in your own writing

- Use them where they **sharpen a picture** or **strengthen a feeling**, especially in narratives, descriptions and speeches.
- Prefer **fresh** images to worn-out ones: *"as white as snow"* is tired (and few Sierra Leoneans have seen snow); *"as white as a new school shirt on Monday morning"* is fresh and local.
- Don't overload: two or three good figures in an essay beat ten forced ones.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Name the figure: 'The news spread like wildfire.'"}
**Simile** (*like*). Meaning: the news spread very quickly and widely.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Name the figure: 'The examination was a mountain we all had to climb.'"}
**Metaphor**: the examination **is** a mountain. Meaning: it was a great, difficult challenge requiring effort.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Name the figure: 'I'm so hungry I could eat a whole cow.'"}
**Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration to stress how hungry the speaker is.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Name the figure: 'The kettle sang on the fire.'"}
**Personification** (and the singing suggests the sound, close to onomatopoeia). The kettle is given a human action, *singing*, to describe its whistling sound.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Name the figure: 'Parliament has rejected the bill.' (Parliament here means the members of Parliament.)"}
**Metonymy**: the institution stands for the people who make its decisions.
:::

:::reveal{title="6. Name the figure: 'He was a wise fool.'"}
**Oxymoron**: two opposite words side by side.
:::

## Key points

- **Comparison**: simile (*like/as*), metaphor (*is*), personification (human qualities).
- **Exaggeration and contrast**: hyperbole, litotes, oxymoron, paradox, antithesis, irony, sarcasm.
- **Sound**: alliteration (consonants), assonance (vowels), onomatopoeia.
- **Substitution and address**: euphemism, metonymy, synecdoche, apostrophe, rhetorical question, climax, anticlimax.
- In answers, **name** the figure, give its **meaning**, and where asked, its **effect**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Figures of speech are tested in the **comprehension** section (*"What figure of speech is…?"*, *"What does the writer mean by…?"*) and in the objective paper. Examiners reward the **exact name** and a **plain-language explanation** in context. In essays, fresh, controlled figurative language earns **Expression** marks.
:::
