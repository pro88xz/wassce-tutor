## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain human wants, scarcity and choice, and how they are linked
- draw up a scale of preference
- define opportunity cost and calculate it in simple cases

## Wants

:::callout{type=definition}
**Wants** are the desires people have for goods and services. Human wants are **unlimited**: as soon as one want is satisfied, another appears.
:::

A pupil who gets a new pair of school shoes soon wants a bag, then a calculator, then a phone. A family that buys a radio soon wants a television. Wants also **recur**: you eat today, and you will want food again tomorrow.

Wants are often grouped as:

- **necessities**: things needed to live, such as food, water, clothing and shelter
- **comforts**: things that make life easier, such as a fan or a mattress
- **luxuries**: things that are not needed but give extra satisfaction, such as jewellery or an expensive car

## Scarcity

:::callout{type=definition}
**Scarcity** means that the resources available to satisfy wants are limited compared with the wants themselves.
:::

Scarcity does not mean that something is rare. Rice is common in Sierra Leone, but it is still scarce in the economic sense: no family has enough money to buy all the rice, clothes, school fees and other things it would like. Money, land, time, labour and raw materials are all scarce.

## Choice

Because wants are unlimited and resources are scarce, people must **choose** which wants to satisfy now and which to leave for later. Firms choose what to produce, and governments choose what to spend on.

## The scale of preference

To choose sensibly, people rank their wants in order of importance.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **scale of preference** is a list of wants arranged in order of importance, with the most pressing want first.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
Aminata has NLe 300 this month. Her wants, in order of importance, are:

| Rank | Want | Cost (NLe) | Running total (NLe) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Food for the month | 180 | 180 |
| 2 | Transport to school | 60 | 240 |
| 3 | Exercise books | 40 | 280 |
| 4 | New sandals | 70 | 350 |
| 5 | Phone data | 30 | 380 |

With NLe 300 she can satisfy wants 1 to 3 (NLe 280). She cannot also afford the sandals, so they are left unsatisfied this month, even though she has NLe 20 left over.
:::

## Opportunity cost

Every choice means giving something up.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made. It is also called **real cost**.
:::

In the example above, if Aminata had chosen sandals instead of exercise books, the opportunity cost of the sandals would be the **exercise books**, her next best alternative, not everything else on the list.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
A farmer in Kambia has one acre of land. He can plant either rice or groundnuts on it. He chooses rice.

- **Opportunity cost of the rice:** the groundnuts he could have grown on the same acre.
- **Money cost:** the seed, fertiliser and labour he paid for.

The money cost and the opportunity cost are different ideas. Economists care most about opportunity cost, because it shows what was really sacrificed.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Opportunity cost is **only the next best alternative**, not the sum of all the alternatives. If you choose A over B, C and D, and B was your second choice, the opportunity cost of A is B alone.
:::

### Opportunity cost for a government

A government with a fixed budget that builds a new hospital may have to delay a new road. The road is the opportunity cost of the hospital. This is why every budget involves hard choices.

## Key points

- Wants are **unlimited** and **recurring**; resources are **scarce**.
- Scarcity forces **choice**.
- A **scale of preference** ranks wants in order of importance.
- **Opportunity cost** is the next best alternative forgone.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Define each term exactly, then show **how they are linked**: unlimited wants + scarce resources → choice → opportunity cost. Questions often give a short case (a student, a farmer, a government) and ask you to **identify the opportunity cost**. Name only the next best alternative.
:::

## Quick check

1. Why is rice called "scarce" even though it is sold in every market?
2. Musa can spend his Saturday working on his father's farm, revising Chemistry or playing football. He would most like to revise; his second choice is the farm. What is the opportunity cost of revising?
3. What is a scale of preference used for?

**Answers:** 1. Because people's wants for it (and for everything else) are greater than the money and resources available. 2. Working on the farm, his next best alternative. 3. To rank wants in order of importance so that the most pressing are satisfied first.
