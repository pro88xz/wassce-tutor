:::callout{type=definition}
**Ecology** is the study of living things in relation to each other and to their environment.
:::

It asks why mangroves grow at the coast but not on the Loma Mountains, why there are more rats on a farm after harvest, and what happens to a river when a mine washes mud into it. Sierra Leone is an excellent place to study ecology: in a small country there is tropical rainforest, savanna woodland, mangrove swamp, inland valley swamp, rivers, beaches and the open sea. This lesson introduces the vocabulary of ecology, the factors that shape every habitat, and how ecologists measure them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the key terms of ecology
- describe the levels of ecological organisation
- name and describe the main habitats of Sierra Leone
- distinguish abiotic and biotic factors and explain their effects
- name the instruments used to measure abiotic factors
- estimate the density of a population using quadrats

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition}
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **ecology** | the study of the relationships between organisms and their environment |
| **environment** | everything around an organism that affects it, living and non-living |
| **habitat** | the place where an organism lives (e.g. a mangrove swamp) |
| **niche** | the role of an organism in its habitat: what it eats, what eats it, where and when it is active |
| **population** | all the organisms of **one species** living in an area at the same time |
| **community** | all the **populations** of different species living and interacting in an area |
| **ecosystem** | a community together with its **non-living** environment, interacting as a unit |
| **biosphere** | all the parts of the Earth where life exists |
:::

![Five levels from small to large: organism, population, community, ecosystem and biosphere; each level includes all the levels before it](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/ecology-levels.svg)

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Confusing habitat and niche.** The habitat is the organism's **address**; the niche is its **job**. Two species can share a habitat, but if they have exactly the same niche they compete until one is pushed out.
:::

## Habitats of Sierra Leone

| Habitat | Where | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **tropical rainforest** | the east and south-east (e.g. Gola Rainforest) | high rainfall, tall trees in layers, great diversity of species |
| **savanna woodland** | the north (e.g. around Kabala and Outamba-Kilimi) | grasses with scattered trees, a long dry season, bush fires |
| **mangrove swamp** | the coast and estuaries (e.g. the Sierra Leone River estuary, Scarcies) | salty, waterlogged mud; tides; few but specialised plant species |
| **inland valley swamp** | valley bottoms across the country | waterlogged, fertile; widely used for rice |
| **freshwater** | rivers such as the Rokel, Sewa, Moa and Mano | flowing water; fish, insect larvae, water plants |
| **marine** | the Atlantic coast and continental shelf | salty water; plankton, fish, turtles |

## Abiotic and biotic factors

The conditions in a habitat are set by two groups of factors.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Abiotic factors** are the **non-living** parts of the environment:

- **climatic**: temperature, rainfall, light, humidity, wind
- **edaphic** (soil): texture, pH, water content, mineral salts, air, humus
- **topographic**: altitude, slope, aspect (which way a slope faces)
- for water habitats: salinity, current, depth, turbidity, dissolved oxygen

**Biotic factors** are the effects of **living** organisms: food supply, competition, predation, parasites and disease, pollination, and human activities.
:::

### How abiotic factors affect organisms

- **Rainfall** decides the type of vegetation: rainforest in the wetter south and east, savanna in the drier north.
- **Light** controls photosynthesis. In a rainforest, plants on the dark forest floor have large, thin leaves to catch the little light that reaches them.
- **Temperature** affects the rate of enzyme reactions, so it affects growth and activity.
- **Salinity** (saltiness) limits which plants grow in mangrove swamps: only species that can cope with salt.
- **Soil pH and nutrients** decide which crops do well; acid soils need liming.
- **Humidity** affects transpiration; in dry air plants lose water faster.

## Measuring abiotic factors

| Factor | Instrument |
|---|---|
| temperature | **thermometer** (maximum and minimum thermometer for the day's range) |
| rainfall | **rain gauge** |
| relative humidity | **hygrometer** (wet-and-dry bulb thermometer) |
| wind speed | **anemometer** |
| wind direction | **wind vane** |
| light intensity | **light meter** (photometer) |
| soil or water pH | **pH meter** or universal indicator |
| atmospheric pressure | **barometer** |
| water clarity (turbidity) | **Secchi disc** |

:::callout{type=tip}
Pair each instrument with **what it measures** in one phrase: "an anemometer measures **wind speed**". WASSCE often gives a picture or name of an instrument and asks for its use.
:::

## Estimating population density

Counting every plant in a field is impossible. Ecologists count a **sample** using **quadrats** placed at random, then scale up.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Ten 1 m² quadrats were placed at random on a school field. The numbers of a weed counted were 3, 5, 0, 2, 4, 6, 1, 3, 2, 4. The field is 50 m by 40 m. Estimate the number of the weed on the field.**

Total counted $= 30$ in $10$ m², so the mean density is $\dfrac{30}{10} = 3$ per m².

Area of field $= 50 \times 40 = 2000$ m².

Estimated population $= 3 \times 2000 = $ **6000 plants**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Placing quadrats where the plants look thickest.** That gives an overestimate. Quadrats must be placed **at random**, for example by using random numbers as coordinates on a grid, so that the sample is fair.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**A school field study in Bo.** Students compare a shaded area under mango trees with an open area of the school compound. With a light meter, a soil thermometer and quadrats, they find the open area is brighter, warmer and drier, with more grass and fewer mosses. They have shown how **abiotic factors** control the **distribution** of organisms, which is the core of ecology.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Distinguish between a population and a community."}
A **population** is all the organisms of **one species** in an area; a **community** is all the populations of **different species** in that area.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Name the instrument used to measure (a) relative humidity, (b) wind speed, (c) rainfall."}
(a) hygrometer (b) anemometer (c) rain gauge.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give two climatic and two edaphic factors."}
Climatic: any two of temperature, rainfall, light, humidity, wind. Edaphic: any two of soil pH, texture, water content, mineral salts, humus, air.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Five 1 m² quadrats contained 8, 12, 10, 6 and 14 snails. Estimate the number of snails in a 300 m² area."}
Mean $= 50 \div 5 = 10$ per m². Estimate $= 10 \times 300 = 3000$ snails.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Explain why the vegetation of northern Sierra Leone differs from that of the south-east."}
The north has **lower rainfall** and a **longer dry season**, with bush fires, so savanna grasses and fire-resistant trees grow; the south-east has high rainfall all year, which supports tall, dense rainforest.
:::

## Key points

- **Ecology** studies organisms and their environment.
- Levels: **organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere**.
- **Habitat** = where it lives; **niche** = its role.
- **Abiotic** factors (climatic, edaphic, topographic) and **biotic** factors (food, competition, predators, disease, humans) shape every habitat.
- Know each **instrument** and what it measures.
- **Quadrats** at random: mean per m² × total area.
