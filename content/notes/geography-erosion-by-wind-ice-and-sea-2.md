# Wind, glacial and coastal depositional landforms

## Learning objectives
You should be able to explain how wind, glaciers and waves deposit sediment, describe characteristic landforms and compare the conditions under which they develop.

## Wind deposition: dunes
Wind loses carrying power when its speed decreases, when it encounters an obstacle or when sediment supply exceeds transport capacity. Sand accumulates to form dunes.

A simple dune has a gentle windward slope and a steeper leeward slip face. Sand grains move up the windward side by saltation and creep, then fall or avalanche down the slip face when the angle of repose is exceeded. Dunes migrate in the direction of the prevailing wind as erosion occurs on one side and deposition on the other.

Barchan dunes are crescent-shaped and commonly develop where sand supply is limited and wind direction is fairly consistent. Their horns point downwind. Transverse dunes form ridges broadly at right angles to the prevailing wind where sand supply is more abundant. Longitudinal dunes are elongated roughly parallel to the prevailing wind under suitable variable wind regimes.

## Glacial depositional landforms
Glaciers deposit unsorted material called till when ice melts or retreats. Till may contain clay, sand, gravel and large boulders together. Deposits laid down by meltwater are sorted and layered because flowing water separates particles by size.

- **Moraines** are accumulations of glacial debris. Lateral moraines occur along glacier sides; medial moraines form where glaciers join; terminal moraines mark a former maximum advance; ground moraine is spread beneath ice.
- **Drumlins** are streamlined hills of glacial till, commonly aligned with former ice movement.
- **Erratics** are large boulders transported from their original geological source and deposited elsewhere.

A fjord is not a depositional landform: it is a deep glacial trough flooded by the sea after ice retreat. This distinction is important in examination answers.

## Coastal depositional landforms
Beaches form where waves and currents deposit sand or shingle. A spit is a narrow ridge of sediment attached to land at one end and extending into a bay or estuary. Longshore drift transports sediment along the coast; where the coastline changes direction or energy decreases, material may accumulate and extend outwards.

A bar may develop when a spit grows across a bay and joins two sides, sometimes enclosing a lagoon. A tombolo is a ridge of sediment connecting an island to the mainland or another island. The exact shape depends on wave refraction, currents, sediment supply and coastal geometry.

## Managing depositional environments
Dunes can protect inland areas from storm waves and wind-blown sand. Vegetation helps stabilise them, while trampling, vehicle traffic and construction can destabilise the surface. Beaches and spits may shift naturally; poorly designed coastal structures can interrupt sediment movement and increase erosion elsewhere.

## Worked comparison
A crescent-shaped dune with horns pointing downwind indicates a barchan formed by wind deposition under a fairly consistent prevailing wind and limited sand supply. A long narrow ridge attached to a headland and extending into an estuary is more likely a spit formed by longshore drift.

## WASSCE examination focus
Identify the transporting agent and explain the change in energy that causes deposition. Distinguish a fjord from a moraine, and a spit from a tombolo. Label windward and leeward slopes on dune diagrams.

## Quick check
1. What causes sand to accumulate as a dune?
2. What is till?
3. Distinguish a spit from a tombolo.
4. Why is a fjord not a depositional landform?

**Answers:** 1. Reduced wind energy or excess sediment supply causes deposition. 2. Unsorted sediment deposited directly by glacial ice. 3. A spit is attached at one end; a tombolo connects an island to land or another island. 4. It is a sea-flooded glacially eroded valley.
