Light behaves as a wave when it interferes and as a particle when it knocks out electrons. Strangely, electrons — clearly particles — can behave as waves too. This "wave–particle duality" is one of the deepest ideas in physics, and it has practical uses from solar panels to electron microscopes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the dual nature of light and of matter
- use the de Broglie relation $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{h}{mv}$
- describe applications of the photoelectric effect
- describe electron diffraction as evidence for matter waves

## Wave–particle duality of light

| Light behaves as a **wave** in | Light behaves as a **particle** (photons) in |
|---|---|
| interference (Young's double slit) | the photoelectric effect |
| diffraction | the emission and absorption of line spectra |
| polarisation | Compton scattering (X-rays bouncing off electrons) |

Neither picture alone describes light completely: light has a **dual nature**. Which behaviour we see depends on the experiment.

## Matter waves: de Broglie's hypothesis

In 1924, de Broglie suggested that if waves (light) can behave as particles, then **particles can behave as waves**. A particle of momentum $p = mv$ has a wavelength

$$\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{h}{mv}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron (m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg) moving at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s)**

$$\lambda = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.0 \times 10^{6}} = 3.6 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$$

This is similar to the spacing between atoms in a crystal, so electrons can be diffracted by crystals.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Find the de Broglie wavelength of a 0.15 kg cricket ball moving at 20 m s⁻¹.**

$$\lambda = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{0.15 \times 20} = 2.2 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$$

Far too small to observe: everyday objects show no wave behaviour.
:::

### Evidence: electron diffraction

A beam of electrons passed through a thin layer of **graphite** produces a pattern of **concentric rings** on a fluorescent screen — a **diffraction pattern**, which only waves can produce. Faster electrons (larger momentum) give **smaller** rings, because their wavelength is shorter. This confirmed de Broglie's hypothesis.

## Applications of the photoelectric effect

1. **Photocells (photoemissive cells)**: a light-sensitive cathode in a vacuum tube; light causes a current. Used in burglar alarms, automatic doors, counting objects on conveyor belts, and to read the soundtrack on old films.
2. **Solar cells (photovoltaic cells)**: light frees electrons in a semiconductor junction, producing an e.m.f. directly. Used in solar panels, calculators and satellites.
3. **Light meters** in cameras.
4. **Light-dependent resistors (LDRs)**: resistance falls in light; used in automatic street lights that switch on at dusk.
5. **Image sensors** (CCD/CMOS) in digital and phone cameras.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Solar street lights along roads in Freetown and district towns combine two photoelectric devices: solar panels that charge the battery during the day, and an LDR sensor that switches the lamp on automatically when it gets dark.
:::

## Electron microscopes

Because electrons can have wavelengths far **shorter** than visible light, an **electron microscope** can see much finer detail (resolve much smaller objects) than an optical microscope — down to individual large molecules and viruses. Magnetic fields act as "lenses" to focus the electron beam.

## Key points

- Light shows wave behaviour (interference, diffraction, polarisation) and particle behaviour (photoelectric effect).
- de Broglie: every particle has a wavelength $\lambda = h/mv$.
- Electron diffraction by graphite proves that electrons behave as waves.
- Applications: photocells, solar cells, LDRs, camera sensors; electron microscopes use electron waves.

## Quick check

1. Which phenomenon shows that light behaves as particles?
2. What experiment shows that electrons behave as waves?
3. Why does a fast electron have a shorter de Broglie wavelength than a slow one?

**Answers:** 1. The photoelectric effect. 2. Electron diffraction (for example through a thin graphite film). 3. Its momentum $mv$ is larger, and $\lambda = h/mv$.
