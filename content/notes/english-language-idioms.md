If someone tells you that your brother has *"kicked the bucket"*, they don't mean he has damaged a bucket. An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from its separate words. WASSCE tests idioms in the objective paper (*"Choose the option that best explains the meaning of the underlined expression"*), and a well-chosen idiom can lift an essay's expression marks. This lesson teaches the idioms most often tested, grouped so they are easier to remember, with a method for the questions and plenty of practice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain what an idiom is and why it can't be read word by word
- give the meaning of the idioms most often tested in WASSCE
- use context to work out an idiom you have not met before
- answer idiom-interpretation questions accurately
- use idioms appropriately in your own writing

## What makes an idiom

:::callout{type=definition}
An **idiom** is a group of words with a meaning that is different from the meanings of the individual words: *to spill the beans* (to reveal a secret), *a white elephant* (something expensive but useless).

Idioms are **fixed**: you cannot change the words. *"To spill the rice"* or *"a white goat"* means nothing.
:::

## Idioms most often tested, grouped by theme

### Trouble and difficulty

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| in hot water | in trouble |
| between the devil and the deep blue sea | facing two equally bad choices |
| out of the frying pan into the fire | from a bad situation into a worse one |
| a bitter pill to swallow | something unpleasant that must be accepted |
| at one's wits' end | so worried that one does not know what to do |
| to face the music | to accept the punishment or consequences |
| to be in the same boat | to share the same difficult situation |

### Success, failure and effort

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| to burn the midnight oil | to study or work late into the night |
| to pass with flying colours | to succeed very well |
| to throw in the towel | to give up |
| to leave no stone unturned | to try every possible way |
| a white elephant | something costly that is of little use |
| a wild goose chase | a hopeless or pointless search |
| to make ends meet | to manage to live on one's income |

### Speech and secrets

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| to spill the beans / let the cat out of the bag | to reveal a secret |
| to beat about the bush | to avoid coming to the point |
| to hold one's tongue | to keep quiet |
| to give someone a piece of one's mind | to scold someone angrily |
| to take something with a pinch of salt | not to believe it completely |
| by word of mouth | by people telling each other, not in writing |

### People and character

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| a wet blanket | a person who spoils others' fun |

| the black sheep (of the family) | a member regarded as a disgrace |
| a bookworm | a person who loves reading |
| to have a soft spot for | to be fond of |
| a snake in the grass | a hidden enemy, someone treacherous |
| to blow one's own trumpet | to boast |

### Time and chance

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| once in a blue moon | very rarely |
| in the nick of time | just in time |
| at the eleventh hour | at the last possible moment |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Krio is rich in its own idioms and proverbs, and many have English equivalents:

| Krio | Meaning | A close English idiom |
|---|---|---|
| *"Monkey work, baboon chop"* | one person works, another enjoys the reward | *one sows, another reaps* |
| *"Small small, fowl drink water"* | slow, steady effort achieves results | *slow and steady wins the race* |
| *"Na bad bush nor dey for throw bad pikin"* | no child is so bad that they should be abandoned | *blood is thicker than water* (close in spirit) |

In an English essay, use the **English** idiom, or explain the Krio proverb in English if you quote it.
:::

## A method for idiom questions

The question gives a sentence with an idiom underlined and asks for its meaning:

> *"After failing the test twice, Musa decided to **throw in the towel**."*
> A. clean the room B. give up C. try harder D. take a bath

1. **Don't read the words literally.** Towels have nothing to do with it.
2. **Use the context**: he failed twice and *decided* something. Giving up or trying harder both fit.
3. **Use what you know**: *throw in the towel* comes from boxing, where a trainer throws a towel into the ring to stop a fight.
4. **Answer: B. give up.**

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Choosing the literal option.** Test-setters always include an option that matches the words literally (*clean the room*, *take a bath*). It is almost never correct.
:::

## Using idioms in your writing

One or two well-chosen idioms can make an essay livelier, especially in narratives, informal letters and speeches:

> *"I burned the midnight oil for three weeks before the mock examinations, and in the end I passed with flying colours."*

But:

- Use them **correctly** and in the **right register**: *"kicked the bucket"* is far too casual for a formal letter about a death.
- Don't **stack** them: three idioms in one sentence sounds forced.
- Never **change their words**: *"burned the midnight lamp"* is wrong.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. 'The new bridge became a WHITE ELEPHANT because the road to it was never built.' A. a beautiful structure B. a costly but useless project C. a symbol of peace D. a rare animal"}
**B. a costly but useless project.**
:::

:::reveal{title="2. 'Stop BEATING ABOUT THE BUSH and tell me what happened.' A. wasting time in the farm B. avoiding the main point C. punishing someone D. clearing the grass"}
**B. avoiding the main point.**
:::

:::reveal{title="3. 'We reached the examination hall IN THE NICK OF TIME.' A. very early B. just in time C. too late D. at night"}
**B. just in time.**
:::

:::reveal{title="4. 'His sister is THE BLACK SHEEP of the family.' What does it mean?"}
She is regarded as a **disgrace** to the family: the one who behaves badly or differently from the others.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Write a sentence using 'to make ends meet' correctly."}
*Since my father lost his job, my mother has sold cold water and groundnuts to help the family **make ends meet**.*
:::

## Key points

- An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning is not the sum of its words.
- Learn idioms in **themes**: trouble, success and effort, speech and secrets, people, time and chance.
- In questions, **reject the literal option**, use the **context**, and choose the figurative meaning.
- In essays, use **one or two** idioms correctly, in the right register, without changing their words.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The objective paper regularly includes questions asking for the **meaning or interpretation** of an idiomatic expression in a sentence. The literal option is the commonest trap. In essays, appropriate idioms earn credit under **Expression**; misused or invented ones lose it.
:::
