Britain can change its constitution with an ordinary Act of Parliament passed by a simple majority. Sierra Leone cannot: changing its Constitution needs at least two-thirds of all MPs and, for the most important parts, a referendum as well. The difference is between a **flexible** and a **rigid** constitution. This lesson compares the two, explains how Sierra Leone's Constitution is amended, and introduces **constitutionalism**, the idea that government should be limited by the constitution in practice and not only on paper.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish rigid and flexible constitutions, with merits and demerits of each
- describe the procedure for amending the 1991 Constitution of Sierra Leone
- explain entrenched clauses
- define constitutionalism and state its features
- explain why constitutions break down

## Rigid and flexible constitutions

:::callout{type=definition}
A **rigid constitution** can be amended only by a **special procedure** that is more difficult than ordinary law-making. A **flexible constitution** can be amended by the **same procedure as an ordinary law**.
:::

| | Rigid | Flexible |
|---|---|---|
| **Amendment** | Special majority, referendum or approval of states | Simple majority in the legislature |
| **Examples** | Sierra Leone, USA, Nigeria | Britain, New Zealand |
| **Merits** | Stability; protects rights and minorities; prevents hasty or selfish changes by the ruling party; suits federations | Adapts easily to change; avoids revolution caused by outdated rules; cheaper and quicker to amend |
| **Demerits** | Slow and difficult to change; may become outdated; can lead to unconstitutional change (coups) if reform is blocked | Unstable; the ruling party can change it for selfish ends; rights are less secure |

## Amending Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution

![A bill to alter the Constitution must be supported by at least two-thirds of all MPs at the second and third readings; entrenched provisions also need a referendum; then presidential assent](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/amendment-process.svg)

*Figure 1. Amending the 1991 Constitution. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Under **section 108** of the 1991 Constitution:

1. A bill to alter the Constitution is **published** and introduced in Parliament.
2. It must be supported by the votes of **at least two-thirds of all Members of Parliament** at the **second and third readings**.
3. If it alters an **entrenched provision**, it must also be approved at a **referendum**, by the votes of **at least one-half of all registered voters** and **at least two-thirds of the valid votes cast**.
4. It then goes to the **President for assent**.

### Entrenched clauses

:::callout{type=definition}
**Entrenched clauses** are provisions of a constitution that are specially protected, so that they can be changed only by a stricter procedure than the rest of the constitution.
:::

In Sierra Leone, entrenched provisions include **Chapter III (fundamental human rights)**, the **presidential term limit** (section 46), key provisions on **Parliament** and the **judiciary**, and **section 108 itself**, so that the amendment rules cannot be weakened by an ordinary amendment.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why entrench the presidential term limit?** If a two-thirds majority of MPs alone could remove the two-term limit, a President whose party held a large majority could extend their own rule. Requiring a **referendum**, in which at least half of all registered voters must vote yes, means the **people themselves** must agree. This protects democracy against a ruling party that dominates Parliament.
:::

## Constitutionalism

:::callout{type=definition}
**Constitutionalism** is the principle that government should be **limited by the constitution** and should operate according to its rules in practice. It means that rulers accept and obey the constitution, rather than merely having one.
:::

**Features of constitutionalism:**

- **Limited government**: rulers have only the powers the constitution gives them.
- **Supremacy of the constitution.**
- **Rule of law** and **independent judiciary**.
- **Separation of powers** with checks and balances.
- **Protection of fundamental rights.**
- **Periodic free elections** and peaceful transfer of power.
- **Accountability** of public officials.
- Constitutional change only through **lawful procedures**.

### Why constitutions break down

- **Military coups** that suspend or abolish the constitution
- **Leaders who ignore the constitution** or amend it for selfish ends, for example to extend their term
- **Weak institutions**: compromised courts, electoral bodies and legislatures
- **Ethnic and regional conflict** and civil war
- **Corruption** and economic crisis
- **Lack of a democratic culture** and political education
- **Foreign interference**

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's constitutional history shows both breakdown and recovery. The **1978 Constitution** made the APC the only legal party. Military coups in **1967**, **1992** and **1997** suspended constitutional rule. Since the end of the civil war in **2002**, the 1991 Constitution has guided regular elections and two peaceful changes of ruling party, in **2007** and **2018**, which are signs of growing constitutionalism.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Having a written constitution does **not** guarantee constitutionalism. Many dictatorships have had detailed written constitutions that their rulers ignored. Constitutionalism is about **behaviour**: whether rulers actually respect the limits the constitution sets.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Rigid constitution**: a constitution that needs a special, difficult procedure to amend.
- **Flexible constitution**: a constitution that can be amended by the ordinary law-making process.
- **Limited government**: the principle that rulers have only the powers the constitution gives them.
:::

## Key points

- A **rigid** constitution needs a **special procedure** to amend; a **flexible** one is amended like an **ordinary law**.
- **Section 108**: amendments need **two-thirds of all MPs** at the second and third readings; **entrenched provisions** also need a **referendum** (half of registered voters and two-thirds of valid votes).
- **Entrenched clauses** include **Chapter III**, the **term limit** (section 46) and **section 108** itself.
- **Constitutionalism** means government is **limited** by the constitution and obeys it in practice.

## Quick check

1. What is a flexible constitution? Give one example.
2. What majority of MPs is needed to pass a bill altering Sierra Leone's Constitution?
3. What is an entrenched clause?
4. State two features of constitutionalism.

**Answers:**

1. A constitution that can be amended by the same procedure as an ordinary law, for example the British constitution.
2. At least two-thirds of all Members of Parliament, at the second and third readings.
3. A provision of the constitution that can be changed only by a stricter procedure than the rest, such as a referendum.
4. Any two: limited government; supremacy of the constitution; rule of law; independent judiciary; separation of powers with checks and balances; protection of rights; periodic free elections; accountability; lawful constitutional change.
