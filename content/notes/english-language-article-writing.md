An article is written for publication in a newspaper or a magazine, and it is read by the public: people who chose to read it and can stop at any moment. So an article must **catch** the reader with a strong title and opening, **hold** them with clear, lively paragraphs, and **leave** them with something to think about or do. WASSCE sets articles almost every year (*"Write an article for publication in a national newspaper on…"*). This lesson covers the format, the style and the techniques, with a complete model article for a school magazine.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- set out an article with a title and the writer's name
- write a headline and an opening that make people want to read on
- adjust your style for a national newspaper or a school magazine
- organise an article with clear, developed paragraphs
- end with a conclusion that calls readers to act or think

## The format

:::callout{type=definition}
An article has four parts:

1. a **title** (headline) that tells the reader what it is about and makes them want to read it
2. the **body**: an introduction, developed points, a conclusion
3. the **writer's name** (and sometimes a short description: *Fatmata Koroma, Form Five Arts*)
4. **no** addresses, **no** salutation, **no** *Yours faithfully*
:::

| Where the writer's name goes | Accepted forms |
|---|---|
| **below the title** (as a by-line) | *By Fatmata Koroma* |
| **at the end** | *Fatmata Koroma* (and position, if relevant) |

Use one, consistently.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Writing a letter instead of an article.** *"Dear Editor, …Yours faithfully"* is a letter to the editor. If the question asks for an **article**, there is no salutation and no complimentary close: just a title, the article and the writer's name.
:::

## Headlines that work

| Weak title | Strong title |
|---|---|
| ROAD ACCIDENTS | OKADAS, OVERLOADING AND OUR EMPTY CLASSROOMS |
| EDUCATION | WHY OUR GIRLS DROP OUT, AND HOW TO KEEP THEM IN SCHOOL |
| CLEANLINESS | A CLEAN CITY STARTS AT OUR OWN DOORSTEP |

A good title is **specific**, and it often hints at the writer's view.

## Openings that hook

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| A short scene | *It is 7:15 on a Monday morning, and Aminata is already late. Her school is eight miles away, and the poda-poda is full.* |
| A surprising fact or claim | *More students in our school own a smartphone than own a dictionary.* |
| A question | *When did you last read a book that was not on the syllabus?* |
| A quotation or proverb | *"Bad bush nor dey for throw bad pikin." No child is beyond saving, and that includes the boys on our street corners.* |

## Style: newspaper or school magazine?

| National newspaper | School magazine |
|---|---|
| formal-to-neutral tone | friendlier, livelier tone |
| written for adults across the country | written for students, teachers and parents |
| national examples (government, the economy) | school examples (classes, clubs, teachers) |
| *"citizens"*, *"the authorities"* | *"fellow students"*, *"our school"* |

Both use **clear, developed paragraphs**: one idea each, with examples.

:::callout{type=tip}
An article may **inform**, **persuade**, or both. You may give your own opinion, which a report may not, but support it with reasons and examples, just as in an argumentative essay.
:::

## A complete model article

**Question:** *Write an article for your school magazine on the importance of reading for pleasure.*

::::model{title="Read for pleasure, pass with ease" label="Model article"}
**READ FOR PLEASURE, AND WATCH YOUR GRADES CLIMB**

*By Ibrahim Conteh, Form Five Arts*

When did you last read a book that was not on the syllabus? Not a pamphlet, not a set of past questions, not your phone: a real book, read simply because you wanted to know what happened next. If you have to think hard about the answer, you are not alone, and that is exactly why I am writing this article.

In our school, reading has become something we do only when an examination is coming. We read to pass, not to enjoy. Yet the students who read for pleasure are very often the ones who pass with the least stress, and there are good reasons for this.

The first is vocabulary. Every story you read introduces you to words in their natural setting. You do not have to memorise *"reluctant"* from a list if you have met a reluctant hero in a novel and understood him. When that word appears in a WASSCE comprehension passage, it feels like an old friend. No list of difficult words can do that.

Reading also improves your writing. Writers learn their craft from other writers. A student who has read twenty novels has absorbed, without noticing, how to open a story, how to describe a market, how to make dialogue sound real. When the essay question asks you to narrate an experience, that student has a hundred models in his head. The student who has read nothing has only a blank page.

Thirdly, reading builds concentration. In an age of thirty-second videos, the ability to follow one idea for an hour is becoming rare. Yet that is exactly what a three-hour examination demands. Reading a novel is training for the mind, in the same way that running is training for the body.

Finally, reading is a pleasure in itself. A book can take you to the streets of Lagos, the hills of Kenya or the stars, for the price of borrowing it. It can make you laugh in the middle of a dull afternoon, or help you understand a friend who is going through something you have never faced.

So where should you start? Our newly opened library has shelves of novels by African writers such as Chinua Achebe and Ngugi wa Thiong'o, and the librarian, Mrs Sesay, is always happy to recommend something that suits you. Start with one short book. Read ten pages a night before you sleep instead of scrolling. Join the new Book Club that meets every Wednesday at break.

Reading for pleasure is not a waste of study time. It is the easiest, cheapest and most enjoyable study you will ever do. Pick up a book this week, and see where it takes you.
::::

### Why it scores

- **Format**: a headline and a by-line; no salutation, no close.
- **Hook**: a direct question to the reader, then a reason the article matters.
- **Content**: four developed benefits (vocabulary, writing, concentration, pleasure), each in its own paragraph with examples.
- **Style**: lively and friendly for a school magazine (*"an old friend"*, *"training for the mind"*), but every sentence is correct.
- **Conclusion**: practical steps (the library, ten pages a night, the Book Club) and a call to action.
- **Length**: about 460 words.

:::

:::reveal{title="2. Write a hooking opening for an article on keeping the school compound clean."}
*Walk behind the Form Three block at break, and you will find our school's real rubbish dump: a carpet of sachets, wrappers and plastic bags that we have all stepped over so many times that we no longer see it.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. What is wrong with ending an article with 'Yours faithfully, Ibrahim Conteh'?"}
That is the close of a **formal letter**. An article ends with the writer's **name** alone (or has a by-line under the title), with no complimentary close.
:::

## Key points

- Format: **title**, **body**, **writer's name** (by-line or at the end). **No** addresses, salutation or *Yours faithfully*.
- Write a **specific, interesting headline** and an opening that **hooks**.
- Adjust the **tone** to the publication: formal-neutral for a national newspaper, livelier for a school magazine.
- Develop **one idea per paragraph** with examples, and end with a **call to action** or a thought.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Examiners check the **article format** (title and writer's name, no letter features), reward **developed, relevant content**, and look for **expression** suited to the readers of the publication named in the question. Writing a letter instead of an article, or omitting the title, are common and costly errors.
:::
