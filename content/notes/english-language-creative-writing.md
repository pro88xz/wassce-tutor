Creative writing is where you have the most freedom, and where the best essays in the whole examination are often written. A creative question may ask for a story with a twist, an imaginative piece (*"Imagine you woke up as the President for a day"*), a sketch of a character, or a piece that begins with a given line. Freedom is also a trap: without control, creative pieces wander, lose their point and run out of time. This lesson shows you the craft that turns imagination into marks: a strong idea, a controlled structure, a distinctive voice and an ending that lands, with a complete model.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- turn a creative prompt into one strong, focused idea
- use structure, voice and point of view deliberately
- create a believable character in a few sentences
- use imagery, dialogue and pace to hold the reader
- end a creative piece with a twist, a resolution or a resonant image

## Creative questions you may meet

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| **Imaginative situation** | *Imagine you were the President of Sierra Leone for one day. Write about it.* |
| **A given opening** | *Write a story beginning: "The letter had been hidden under the mat for ten years."* |
| **Picture-like prompt** | *Write a story about a storm at sea.* |
| **Character sketch** | *Write about the most unforgettable person you have met.* |

:::callout{type=tip}
**One idea, done well.** In about 500 words you cannot tell a whole life or a whole war. Choose **one moment**, **one change**, **one surprise**, and build everything around it.
:::

## From prompt to idea

Ask three questions of any prompt:

1. **Who** is at the centre? (one main character)
2. **What do they want**, and what stands in the way? (conflict)
3. **What changes** by the end? (the point of the piece)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Prompt:** *Imagine you were the President of Sierra Leone for one day.*

| Weak idea | Strong idea |
|---|---|
| A list of everything I would do: build roads, fix hospitals, give jobs, light the streets… | I plan to change the country in a day, but a single visit to a village clinic teaches me that real change is slower, smaller and more human than a speech. |

The weak idea is a list of wishes with no story. The strong idea has a **character**, a **conflict** (big plans against reality) and a **change** (what she learns).
:::

## The writer's toolkit

### Voice and point of view

- **First person** brings the reader inside one mind: honest, intimate, sometimes funny.
- **Third person** gives you more distance and freedom to show several characters.

Whichever you choose, **keep it**, and keep your **tense** consistent.

### Character in a few strokes

You don't need a full description. One or two telling details create a person:

> *Nurse Kadiatu had the kind of face that had stopped being surprised years ago, and the kind of hands that never stopped moving.*

### Pace

- **Short sentences** speed things up: *The door opened. Nobody was there.*
- **Longer sentences** slow down for reflection and description.
- Mix them, and use the short ones at moments of tension.

### Imagery

A fresh image is worth a paragraph of explanation: *the generator coughed like an old man and died*; *the rain came down in long silver ropes*.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Clichés.** *"Once upon a time"*, *"It was a dark and stormy night"*, *"I woke up and it was all a dream"* tell the examiner you have not tried. The **dream ending** in particular is weak: it cancels everything the reader has just read.
:::

## Endings that land

| Ending | How it works |
|---|---|
| **A twist** | reveals something that changes how we see the whole story |
| **A resolution with a change** | the character has learnt or lost something |
| **A resonant image** | a final picture that carries the feeling of the piece |
| **A return** | the last line echoes the first, now with new meaning |

## A complete model piece

**Question:** *Imagine you were the President of Sierra Leone for one day. Write about your experience.*

::::model{title="President for a day" label="Model creative writing"}
**PRESIDENT FOR A DAY**

At six o'clock in the morning, I was handed a folder, a pen and a country.

"Your Excellency," said the protocol officer, a tall man whose shoes were shinier than any I had ever owned, "you have fourteen hours. Use them well." Then he bowed, and I realised that for the first time in my seventeen years, a grown man was waiting for me to tell him what to do.

I had planned this day for weeks, lying awake in our room in Kissy with the generator next door coughing like an old man. I would announce free electricity for every home. I would order a new road to every district headquarters. I would build a hospital in every chiefdom before the sun went down. I had the speech written in my exercise book, every line underlined twice.

By nine o'clock, I had read the speech to the cabinet. They nodded politely, the way my aunties nod when my little brother explains how he will become a footballer in Spain. The Minister of Finance cleared his throat. "Your Excellency," she said gently, "with respect, a hospital takes three years to build, and doctors take six years to train."

I decided to see for myself. Against the advice of everyone, I drove out to a village clinic two hours from the city. The road ended long before the clinic did, so we walked the last mile, the presidential shoes filling with red dust.

The clinic had one room, one bed and one nurse. Nurse Kadiatu had the kind of face that had stopped being surprised years ago, and the kind of hands that never stopped moving. A baby with a fever lay on a mat in the corner while its mother fanned it with a piece of cardboard. There was no electricity, so the vaccines were kept in a cool box that a boy carried on his bicycle every morning from the next town.

"What do you need most?" I asked her, my folder open, my pen ready to sign anything.

She thought for a long moment. "A solar fridge," she said. "And someone to pay that boy. He has carried that box every day for two years, and nobody has ever thanked him."

I signed two papers that afternoon. They were not the papers in my speech. One bought a solar fridge. The other gave the boy with the bicycle a salary and a new pair of tyres.

At eight o'clock that night, the protocol officer took back the folder. I had not built a single hospital or a single road. But on the drive home through the dark streets of Freetown, I understood something my speech had missed: a country is not changed in a day. It is changed one fridge, one bicycle, one thank-you at a time.
::::

### Why it scores

- **One focused idea**: big plans against reality, ending in a small, real change.
- **A hooking first line**: *"I was handed a folder, a pen and a country."*
- **Character in a few strokes**: the protocol officer's shoes, Nurse Kadiatu's face and hands.
- **Imagery and local detail**: the coughing generator, red dust in presidential shoes, the cool box on the bicycle.
- **Pace**: short dialogue at key moments, longer reflective sentences at the end.
- **An ending that lands**: the lesson grows out of the story, and the final line echoes the opening's *one day*.
- **Length**: about 470 words.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Turn this prompt into a focused idea: 'Write a story about a storm.'"}
*During a night storm in a fishing village near Tombo, a girl who has always been afraid of the sea must row out to guide her father's boat home with a lantern, and discovers that courage is not the absence of fear.* (One character, one conflict, one change.)
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Create a character in two sentences: a strict old headmaster."}
*Mr Bangura measured the length of every boy's hair with a ruler he kept in his breast pocket, next to a pen that had never once written a compliment. Yet on the morning of the WASSCE, he stood at the school gate in the rain and shook every hand.*
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Why is 'Then I woke up and it was all a dream' a weak ending?"}
It **cancels** the whole story: nothing that happened mattered, so the reader feels cheated. It also signals to the examiner that the writer could not think of a real ending.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Conflict**: what stands in the way of what the main character wants; it drives the story.
- **Twist**: an ending that reveals something which changes how we see the whole story.
- **Resolution**: the ending, in which the character has learnt or lost something.
- **First person**: telling a story as "I", which brings the reader inside one mind.
- **Third person**: telling a story about "he", "she" or "they", from outside the characters.
- **Character sketch**: a piece of writing that brings one person to life through their appearance, actions and words.
:::

## Key points

- Build every piece on **one idea**: a character, a conflict and a change.
- Choose a **point of view** and **tense**, and keep them.
- Create characters with **one or two telling details**; control **pace** with sentence length.
- Use **fresh imagery** and local detail; avoid **clichés** and dream endings.
- End with a **twist**, a **change**, a **resonant image** or a **return** to the opening.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Creative pieces earn the highest marks for **expression** when they are original, vivid and controlled, and for **content** when they stay focused on the question. Examiners mark down pieces that are lists of wishes rather than stories, that wander without a point, or that rely on clichés and dream endings.
:::
