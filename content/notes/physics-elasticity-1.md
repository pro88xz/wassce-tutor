A spring scale in the market, the springs under a motorbike seat, a catapult and the rubber in a sandal all stretch and spring back. How far they stretch, and when they stop springing back, is the subject of elasticity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define elasticity, elastic limit and plastic deformation
- state Hooke's law and use $F = ke$
- interpret a load–extension graph for a spring and a metal wire
- describe an experiment to verify Hooke's law

## Elasticity

:::callout{type=definition}
**Elasticity** is the property of a material that enables it to **return to its original shape and size** after the force deforming it is removed.
:::

A material that does not return to its original shape is **plastic** (for example plasticine, wet clay, chewing gum). Many materials are elastic for small forces and plastic for large ones: a spring stretched too far stays permanently longer.

## Hooke's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Hooke's law:** the **extension** of an elastic material is **directly proportional** to the **force (load)** applied, provided the **elastic limit** (more precisely, the limit of proportionality) is **not exceeded**.

$$F = ke$$

where $F$ is the force (N), $e$ the extension (m) and $k$ the **spring constant** (or force constant) in **N m⁻¹**.
:::

:::callout{type=definition}
The **extension** is the **increase** in length: $e = l - l_0$, where $l_0$ is the original (natural) length.
:::

The spring constant measures **stiffness**: a large $k$ means a stiff spring that stretches little.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A spring of natural length 20 cm stretches to 24 cm when a 6 N weight hangs on it. Find (a) the spring constant, (b) the length when the load is 9 N (within the elastic limit).**

(a) Extension $e = 24 - 20 = 4\ \text{cm} = 0.04\ \text{m}$.

$$k = \dfrac{F}{e} = \dfrac{6}{0.04} = 150\ \text{N m}^{-1}$$

(b) $e = \dfrac{9}{150} = 0.06\ \text{m} = 6\ \text{cm}$, so the length is $20 + 6 = 26$ cm.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Use the **extension**, not the stretched length. In part (b) above, many candidates answer 6 cm instead of 26 cm, or use 24 cm as if it were the extension.
:::

## The load–extension graph

![Load against extension for a metal wire: a straight line from the origin up to the limit of proportionality P, then a curve through the elastic limit E, the yield point Y and finally the breaking point B](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/hooke-graph.svg)

*Figure 1. Load–extension graph for a metal wire. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **O to P** | straight line through the origin: **Hooke's law** is obeyed; gradient $= k$ (when load is on the y-axis) |
| **P, limit of proportionality** | beyond this, extension is no longer proportional to load |
| **E, elastic limit** | the greatest load from which the material still returns to its original length |
| **Y, yield point** | the material suddenly stretches a lot for little extra load: it starts to flow (plastic) |
| **B, breaking point** | the wire snaps |

Beyond the elastic limit the deformation is **permanent**: removing the load leaves a **permanent set** (extension).

## Experiment: verifying Hooke's law

1. Clamp a spiral spring vertically with a pointer at its lower end against a vertical metre rule. Hang a light scale pan.
2. Record the pointer reading with no load ($l_0$).
3. Add loads in equal steps (e.g. 0.5 N), recording the pointer reading each time.
4. Remove the loads one at a time and record the readings again (they should match, showing the elastic limit was not exceeded).
5. Calculate the extension for each load. Plot **load (y) against extension (x)**.
6. A **straight line through the origin** verifies Hooke's law; its gradient is $k$.

**Precautions:** avoid parallax when reading the pointer (eye level); allow the spring to come to rest before reading; do not exceed the elastic limit; take readings while loading and unloading.

## Work done in stretching (elastic potential energy)

The work done in stretching a spring is the area under the force–extension graph:

$$W = \tfrac{1}{2}Fe = \tfrac{1}{2}ke^2$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How much energy is stored in the spring above (k = 150 N m⁻¹) when it is stretched by 4 cm?**

$$W = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 150 \times 0.04^2 = 0.12\ \text{J}$$
:::

## Key points

- Elasticity: returning to the original shape when the force is removed.
- Hooke's law: $F = ke$, valid up to the limit of proportionality.
- Extension = new length − natural length.
- Beyond the elastic limit, deformation is permanent; then come the yield point and breaking point.
- Energy stored $= \tfrac{1}{2}Fe = \tfrac{1}{2}ke^2$.

## Quick check

1. A spring of $k = 200$ N m⁻¹ is pulled by 10 N. Find its extension.
2. What does the gradient of a load–extension graph give?
3. What happens if a spring is loaded beyond its elastic limit?

**Answers:** 1. $e = 10 \div 200 = 0.05$ m (5 cm). 2. The spring constant $k$ (with load on the vertical axis). 3. It is permanently stretched and does not return to its original length.
