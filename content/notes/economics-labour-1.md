Why does a doctor earn more than a market porter, though the porter works harder with his body? Why do so many young people in Freetown look for jobs that are not there? The answers come from the labour market, where the demand for workers meets the supply of them and sets a price called the wage.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the labour force and explain what determines its size
- explain the demand for labour as a derived demand, and what affects it
- explain what affects the supply of labour
- show how the wage rate is determined by demand and supply
- explain why wages differ between occupations

## The labour force

:::callout{type=definition}
The **labour force** (working population) is the number of people **of working age who are able and willing to work**, whether they are employed or unemployed and looking for work.
:::

People outside the labour force include children, the very old, full-time students, the sick and disabled, and people who are not looking for work. The labour force supports these **dependants**.

**What determines the size of the labour force?**

1. **The size of the population**: more people usually means more workers.
2. **The age structure**: a country with many children (like Sierra Leone) has a smaller share of people of working age.
3. **The school-leaving and retirement ages**: longer schooling or earlier retirement shrinks the labour force.
4. **Attitudes to women working**: where more women take paid work, the labour force is larger.
5. **Health**: disease and disability keep people out of work.
6. **Migration**: emigration of workers shrinks it; immigration enlarges it.

## The demand for labour

:::callout{type=definition}
The **demand for labour** is the number of workers employers are willing and able to hire at each wage rate. It is a **derived demand**: firms want workers not for their own sake, but for the goods and services they help to produce.
:::

When people buy more bread, bakeries need more bakers. When a mine closes, the demand for miners falls, even though the miners are as skilled as before.

**The demand for labour depends on:**

- **the demand for the product** the workers make (derived demand)
- **the productivity of labour**: more productive workers are worth hiring
- **the wage rate**: at a lower wage, firms employ more workers, so the demand curve slopes downwards
- **the price of other factors**: if machines become cheap, firms may replace workers with them
- **the proportion of total cost that is wages**: where wages are a small part of cost, firms are less sensitive to wage rises

## The supply of labour

:::callout{type=definition}
The **supply of labour** is the number of workers (or hours of work) offered at each wage rate. A higher wage usually draws more people into an occupation, so the supply curve slopes upwards.
:::

**The supply of labour to an occupation depends on:**

- **the wage rate** and other rewards (allowances, housing, pension)
- **working conditions**: hours, safety, comfort, status
- **the training and skill required**: few people can quickly become surgeons or engineers
- **the size and age structure of the population**
- **the mobility of labour**: how easily workers can move between jobs and places
- **social attitudes**, for example to manual work or to women in certain jobs

## How wages are determined

In a free labour market, the wage is the **price of labour**, set where the demand for labour equals the supply of labour.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Construction workers in Freetown.** The table shows the demand for and supply of labourers at different daily wages.

| Daily wage (NLe) | Labourers demanded | Labourers offering to work |
|---|---|---|
| 60 | 200 | 1,000 |
| 50 | 400 | 800 |
| 40 | 600 | 600 |
| 30 | 800 | 400 |

- The **equilibrium wage** is **NLe 40**, where 600 are demanded and 600 offer to work.
- At **NLe 50** there is a **surplus of labour** (unemployment) of $800 - 400 = 400$ workers.
- At **NLe 30** there is a **shortage of labour** of $800 - 400 = 400$ workers, and employers bid wages up.
:::

If demand for houses rises, the demand for labourers shifts **right**, and wages and employment both rise. If thousands of young people arrive in the city looking for work, the supply of labour shifts **right**, and the wage tends to **fall**.

## Why wages differ

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| **Skill and training**: long, costly training limits supply | Doctors, engineers and pilots earn more than unskilled workers |
| **Demand for the product** | Workers in a booming industry (mining, telecoms) are paid more |
| **Unpleasant or dangerous work** needs higher pay to attract workers | Miners, deep-sea fishermen |
| **Trade union strength** | Strong unions can bargain for higher wages |
| **Location and cost of living** | City jobs often pay more than the same job in a village |
| **Responsibility and risk** | Managers and bank officials who handle large sums |
| **Discrimination and custom** | Lower pay for some groups, which is unfair and often illegal |
| **Government policy** | Minimum wages and public-sector pay scales |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Wages are **not** paid according to how hard a job is physically. A porter at Dove Cut works very hard, but because almost anyone can do the work, the **supply** is large and the wage is low. Wages depend on **demand and supply**, not effort alone.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
In Sierra Leone, a large share of workers are in **farming and petty trading** in the informal sector, where earnings are low and irregular. Wages in the formal sector (government, banks, mining, telecoms) are higher because fewer people have the education and skills these jobs require.
:::

## Key points

- The **labour force** is everyone of working age able and willing to work; its size depends on population, age structure, schooling, retirement age, attitudes and health.
- The demand for labour is a **derived demand**, depending on the demand for the product and on productivity.
- The **equilibrium wage** is where the demand for labour equals the supply of labour.
- Wages differ because of **skill, training, conditions, demand, union power, location and government policy**.
