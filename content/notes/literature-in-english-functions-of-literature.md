Why would anyone spend hours with people who never existed? Because a good story, play or poem gives back more than it takes. It makes us laugh, it teaches without preaching, it keeps the memory of a people alive, and it can make the powerful uncomfortable. This lesson sets out the main **functions of literature**, what it does for the reader and for society, and why it is worth studying for WASSCE and beyond.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state and explain the main functions of literature
- give an example of each function from oral or written literature
- explain how literature reflects and criticises society
- explain the value of studying literature

## The functions of literature

:::callout{type=definition}
The **functions** (or **purposes**) **of literature** are the things literature does for its readers and listeners and for the society they live in: to entertain, to educate, to preserve culture, to criticise and reform, to reflect life, and to develop language and understanding.
:::

### 1. To entertain

Literature gives **pleasure**. A gripping plot, a funny character, a clever twist or the music of a well-made line holds our attention and lets us escape for a while. Comedy and farce exist mainly to make us laugh; a folktale told at night keeps a whole compound awake.

### 2. To educate and instruct

Literature **teaches**, often without seeming to. Folktales end with a moral; fables show the fate of the greedy, the proud and the lazy. Novels teach history, geography and the customs of other peoples. A reader of Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* learns how an Igbo community was organised before colonial rule, and what was lost when that world was broken.

### 3. To preserve culture and history

Literature is a **store of memory**. Oral literature carried the history, beliefs, values and wisdom of communities from one generation to the next long before writing. Written literature records the language, customs and experiences of an age so that later readers can know them.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Krio proverbs, Mende and Temne folktales, initiation songs and funeral dirges hold values that elders want the young to keep: respect, patience, hard work, loyalty to the family. When a novelist sets a story during Sierra Leone's civil war, as Aminatta Forna does in *The Memory of Love*, literature also preserves what a nation lived through, so that it is not forgotten.
:::

### 4. To reflect society (literature as a mirror)

Literature **holds up a mirror** to life. Its characters face the same problems as real people: poverty, corruption, conflict between old and new ways, love across family lines, the struggle of women for a voice. Readers recognise their own society and themselves.

### 5. To criticise and reform society

Literature can be a **weapon**. Writers expose injustice, corruption, hypocrisy and bad leadership, and urge change. **Satire** uses humour and exaggeration to make wrongdoing look ridiculous. Many African writers used their work to attack colonial rule and, later, the failures of the governments that followed.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Satire** is writing that uses humour, irony and exaggeration to expose and criticise foolishness or wrongdoing, usually in order to bring about change.
:::

### 6. To develop language and thought

Reading literature widens your **vocabulary**, sharpens your sense of grammar and style, and trains you to read closely and argue from evidence. These skills help in English Language, in every essay subject and in later life.

### 7. To build empathy and understanding

Literature lets us see the world through **other people's eyes**: a child soldier, an old woman, a ruler, an enemy. This builds **empathy** and tolerance, and helps us understand people unlike ourselves.

### 8. To promote unity and identity

Shared stories, songs and poems give a people a **common identity**. National anthems, patriotic poems and stories of heroes bind citizens together. Literature written by Africans about Africa also helped to restore pride after colonial writers had misrepresented the continent.

### 9. To comfort and heal

Literature can **console**. Dirges and elegies help mourners express grief; stories of survival give hope; poems put into words feelings that are hard to say. This is sometimes called the **therapeutic** function.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Question:** *Using one example, show how literature can both entertain and educate.*

**Model answer:** A folktale about Spider, who tricks the other animals out of their share of the harvest, **entertains** because Spider's schemes are funny and the listeners enjoy waiting to see how he will be caught. At the end Spider is caught and shamed, so the tale also **educates**: it teaches children that greed and dishonesty are punished. The two functions work together; the pleasure of the story is what makes the lesson stay in the mind.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Listing functions without explaining them.** "Literature entertains, educates and criticises" scores poorly. For each function, say **how** literature does it and give an **example**, as in the model answer above.
:::

## Why study literature?

Studying literature at school does more than prepare you for an examination:

- it develops **critical thinking**: you learn to read closely, interpret and support your view with evidence
- it improves **writing and speaking**, which every course and career needs
- it introduces you to **other cultures, times and ideas**
- it helps you understand **yourself and your society**
- it gives lifelong **enjoyment**

Literature in English is a main subject for students in the Arts, and the reading, analysis and essay skills it builds are valued in courses such as law, journalism, education and the humanities.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. State four functions of literature."}
Any four: to entertain, to educate or instruct, to preserve culture and history, to reflect society, to criticise and reform society, to develop language, to build empathy, to promote unity and identity, to comfort and heal.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. What does it mean to say that literature is a mirror of society?"}
Literature shows life as it is: its characters face the problems real people face, such as poverty, corruption or conflict between tradition and change, so readers recognise their own society in it.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Define satire."}
Satire is writing that uses humour, irony and exaggeration to expose and criticise foolishness or wrongdoing, usually to bring about change.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. How does oral literature preserve culture?"}
Folktales, proverbs, songs and praise poetry carry a community's history, beliefs and values from one generation to the next by word of mouth, so they are not lost even without writing.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Which function does a funeral dirge mainly serve?"}
The therapeutic function, to comfort and heal: it helps mourners express their grief. It may also preserve culture, since it records the community's way of honouring the dead.
:::

## Key points

- Literature **entertains**, **educates**, **preserves culture**, **reflects** society, **criticises** and **reforms** it, **develops language**, **builds empathy**, **unites** a people and **comforts**.
- Most texts serve several functions at once; pleasure is what makes the lesson memorable.
- **Satire** uses humour and exaggeration to expose wrongdoing.
- In an answer, **explain** each function and give an **example**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Paper 1 may ask you to identify the function a passage or form serves (for example, satire as social criticism, or a dirge as consolation). In the essay papers, questions on set texts often turn on these functions: how a novel **criticises** a society, what a play **teaches**, what a poem **reflects** about its time. Knowing the functions gives you the vocabulary to answer "What is the writer's purpose?" with precision.
:::
