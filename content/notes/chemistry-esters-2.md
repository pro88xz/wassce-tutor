Esterification can run backwards. Boil an ester with water and acid, or with an alkali, and it splits back into an acid (or its salt) and an alkanol: **hydrolysis**. The most important esters of all are **fats and oils**, such as palm oil and shea butter. Hydrolysing them with alkali makes **soap**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the acid and alkaline hydrolysis of esters, with equations
- explain why alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion
- describe fats and oils as esters of glycerol and fatty acids
- distinguish fats from oils, and saturated from unsaturated fats
- describe the hydrogenation of oils and the causes of rancidity
- describe simple tests for fats and oils

## Hydrolysis of esters

:::callout{type=definition}
**Hydrolysis** is the breaking down of a compound by reaction with **water**. Hydrolysis of an ester gives an **alkanoic acid (or its salt)** and an **alkanol**.
:::

![Ethyl ethanoate hydrolysed two ways: with dilute acid it gives ethanoic acid and ethanol reversibly; with sodium hydroxide it gives sodium ethanoate and ethanol completely](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/ester-hydrolysis.svg)

*Figure 1. Acid hydrolysis is reversible; alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**1. Acid hydrolysis.** Heat the ester under reflux with **dilute acid** (H₂SO₄ or HCl) as catalyst:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

This is the **reverse of esterification**, so it reaches an **equilibrium** and is **incomplete**.

**2. Alkaline hydrolysis.** Heat the ester under reflux with **sodium hydroxide solution**:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

The products are the **sodium salt** of the acid and the alkanol. The salt cannot react with the alkanol to re-form the ester, so the reaction **goes to completion**. This is why alkaline hydrolysis is preferred.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Saponification** is the **alkaline hydrolysis of an ester**, especially of a fat or oil, to make **soap**. The word comes from the Latin *sapo*, soap.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Mass of alkali.** What mass of sodium hydroxide is needed to hydrolyse 8.8 g of ethyl ethanoate completely? (CH₃COOC₂H₅ = 88; NaOH = 40)

- Moles of ester $= 8.8 \div 88 = 0.10$ mol
- Ratio 1 : 1, so moles of NaOH $= 0.10$ mol
- Mass $= 0.10 \times 40 = 4.0$ g
:::

## Fats and oils are esters

Fats and oils are **triglycerides**: esters of the alkanol **glycerol** (propane-1,2,3-triol, which has three –OH groups) with three **fatty acid** molecules.

$$\text{glycerol} + 3\ \text{fatty acids} \rightarrow \text{triglyceride} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Fatty acid | Formula | Type | Found in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Palmitic** (hexadecanoic) acid | C₁₅H₃₁COOH | Saturated | Palm oil, animal fat |
| **Stearic** (octadecanoic) acid | C₁₇H₃₅COOH | Saturated | Shea butter, beef fat |
| **Oleic** acid | C₁₇H₃₃COOH | Unsaturated (one C=C) | Groundnut oil, olive oil, palm oil |
| **Linoleic** acid | C₁₇H₃₁COOH | Unsaturated (two C=C) | Soya and sunflower oil |

## Fats compared with oils

| Fats | Oils |
|---|---|
| **Solid** at room temperature | **Liquid** at room temperature |
| Mostly **saturated** fatty acids | More **unsaturated** fatty acids (C=C bonds) |
| Mostly from **animals** (butter, lard), also shea butter | Mostly from **plants** (groundnut, palm, coconut) |
| Do not decolourise bromine water | **Decolourise bromine water** |

Unsaturated chains have "kinks" at the C=C bonds, so the molecules cannot pack closely: weaker forces, lower melting points. Diets high in saturated fat are linked to heart disease.

## Hydrogenation: oils into margarine

Hydrogen is added to some C=C bonds of a vegetable oil at about **150–200 °C** with a finely divided **nickel catalyst**. The oil becomes more saturated and **solid**, giving **margarine** and cooking fats:

$$\text{–CH=CH–} + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni}} \text{–CH}_2\text{–CH}_2\text{–}$$

## Rancidity

Fats and oils go **rancid** (develop bad smells and tastes) when kept too long, especially in warm, light, moist conditions:

- **hydrolysis** by moisture and enzymes releases smelly fatty acids (butanoic acid in rancid butter)
- **oxidation** by air attacks the C=C bonds of unsaturated oils

Storing in **cool, dark, airtight** containers and adding **antioxidants** slows rancidity.

## Tests for fats and oils

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| **Grease-spot test**: rub on paper | A **translucent spot** that does not dry out |
| **Sudan III** solution | Fat or oil layer stains **red** |
| **Emulsion test**: shake with ethanol, then pour into water | **Cloudy white emulsion** |
| **Bromine water** | Decolourised by unsaturated oils |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leonean kitchens use several natural esters. **Palm oil** (red, semi-solid when cool) contains roughly equal amounts of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. **Palm kernel oil** and **coconut oil** are mostly saturated. **Groundnut oil** is mostly unsaturated and stays liquid. **Shea butter**, used in cooking and skin care, is a solid fat rich in stearic acid. All of them can be turned into soap by boiling with alkali.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Alkaline hydrolysis gives the **salt** of the acid (sodium ethanoate), not the free acid. To get the acid itself, the salt solution must be acidified afterwards.
:::

## Key points

- **Hydrolysis** splits an ester into **acid + alkanol**: with **dilute acid** it is **reversible**; with **NaOH** it gives the **sodium salt + alkanol** and goes **to completion**.
- **Saponification** = alkaline hydrolysis of fats and oils to make **soap**.
- **Fats and oils** are **triglycerides**: esters of **glycerol** and three **fatty acids**.
- **Fats** are solid and mostly saturated; **oils** are liquid and more unsaturated (decolourise bromine water).
- **Hydrogenation** (Ni, about 180 °C) turns oils into **margarine**.
- **Rancidity** is caused by hydrolysis and oxidation; slow it with cool, dark, airtight storage.

## Quick check

1. Name the products of the alkaline hydrolysis of methyl ethanoate with sodium hydroxide.
2. Why does alkaline hydrolysis of an ester go to completion?
3. Name the alkanol present in all fats and oils.
4. How is a vegetable oil converted into margarine?

**Answers:**

1. Sodium ethanoate and methanol.
2. The sodium salt formed cannot react with the alkanol to re-form the ester, so the reaction is not reversible.
3. Glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol).
4. By hydrogenation: hydrogen is added to some C=C bonds at about 150–200 °C with a nickel catalyst, so the oil becomes more saturated and solid.
