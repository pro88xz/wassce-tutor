Writers rarely tell you how they feel. They show it through their choice of words, what they praise, what they mock and what they leave out. WASSCE questions on tone and attitude test whether you can hear that voice. This lesson shows you how to name a tone accurately and prove it from the passage.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the difference between tone, attitude, mood and purpose
- use a bank of accurate words to describe tone
- identify the clues in a passage that reveal the writer's attitude
- answer tone questions with a word and evidence

## Four related ideas

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Tone**: the writer's *voice*: how the writing sounds (angry, humorous, sad, formal).
- **Attitude**: the writer's *opinion* or *feeling* about the subject (approving, critical, sympathetic).
- **Mood**: the *feeling the passage creates* in the reader or the atmosphere of a scene (tense, peaceful, gloomy).
- **Purpose**: *why* the writer wrote it (to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to warn, to criticise).
:::

They often overlap. A writer who is **critical** of littering (attitude) may use a **sarcastic** voice (tone) to **persuade** readers to change (purpose).

## A bank of tone words

Use precise words. *"Good"*, *"bad"* and *"nice"* are not tones.

| Positive | Negative | Neutral or other |
|---|---|---|
| admiring | bitter | objective |
| appreciative | critical | factual |
| hopeful | sarcastic | formal |
| sympathetic | contemptuous | informal, conversational |
| humorous | angry, indignant | reflective |
| enthusiastic | pessimistic | nostalgic |
| encouraging | mocking | serious, solemn |

## Finding the clues

Look for these in the passage:

1. **Loaded words**: words with strong feelings attached. *"Our **brave** health workers"* (admiring) versus *"so-called experts"* (contemptuous).
2. **Punctuation**: exclamation marks, rhetorical questions and dashes often show strong feeling.
3. **What is praised or blamed**: who gets the credit and who gets the blame?
4. **Exaggeration or understatement**: hyperbole may signal humour or anger; understatement may signal irony.
5. **Imagery**: dark, heavy images create a gloomy mood; bright, lively ones create a hopeful one.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"Once again, the council has promised to fix the Wilkinson Road potholes before the rains. Once again, the rains have come. The potholes, it seems, have decided to stay and raise families."*

**Question:** What is the writer's attitude to the council? Give a reason.

**Answer:** The writer is **critical and sarcastic** about the council, because he repeats *"once again"* to show broken promises and jokes that the potholes are *"raising families"*, suggesting nothing has been done for a long time.

Notice the answer: **a precise tone word** + **evidence quoted or explained** from the passage.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Compare two sentences about the same event, the reopening of a road in Kailahun District:

- *"At last, after years of patience, the people of Kailahun can travel to market in hours instead of days."* Tone: **hopeful, appreciative**.
- *"The road that was promised in three elections has finally appeared, just in time for the fourth."* Tone: **cynical, sarcastic**.

Same facts, different attitudes. The words chosen make the difference.
:::

## Answering tone questions

1. **Name** the tone or attitude with one or two precise words.
2. **Prove** it with evidence: a word, phrase or action from the passage.
3. Keep it **short**: one or two sentences.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Describing the content instead of the tone.** *"The writer is talking about potholes"* tells the examiner the subject, not the tone. Ask yourself: *How does the writer feel about it, and how does it sound?*
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Giving a tone without evidence.** *"The tone is sarcastic"* may earn half the marks. *"The tone is sarcastic, as shown by…"* earns them all.
:::

## Key points

- **Tone** is how it sounds; **attitude** is what the writer thinks; **mood** is the feeling created; **purpose** is why it was written.
- Use **precise** words from a tone bank: critical, admiring, sarcastic, sympathetic, hopeful, objective.
- Clues: **loaded words, punctuation, praise and blame, exaggeration, imagery**.
- Answer with **a tone word plus evidence**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Questions ask *"What is the writer's attitude to…?"*, *"What is the tone of the passage?"*, *"What mood does the last paragraph create?"* or *"What is the writer's purpose?"*. The mark usually needs **both** an accurate word **and** support from the passage.
:::

## Quick check

1. *"Our farmers, who rise before the sun and feed a nation, deserve far more than our thanks."* What is the writer's attitude to farmers?
2. What is the difference between tone and mood?
3. Give a more precise word than *"bad"* for a writer who writes about a corrupt official with scorn.
4. A passage lists facts and figures about rainfall without comment. What is its tone?

**Answers:** 1. Admiring and appreciative: the writer praises their hard work (*"rise before the sun"*, *"feed a nation"*). 2. Tone is the writer's voice; mood is the feeling the passage creates in the reader. 3. **Contemptuous** or **scornful**. 4. **Objective** or **factual**.
