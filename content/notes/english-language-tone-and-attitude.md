Writers rarely tell you how they feel. They **show** it: in the words they choose, what they praise, what they mock, and what they leave out. WASSCE questions on tone and attitude test whether you can hear that hidden voice. This lesson gives you precise words to name a tone, a method for finding the evidence, and a passage to practise on.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the difference between tone, attitude, mood and purpose
- describe a tone with precise words instead of vague ones
- find the clues in a passage that reveal the writer's attitude
- write a tone answer that names the tone and proves it

## Four ideas that are easy to confuse

:::callout{type=definition}
| Term | The question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tone** | How does the writing **sound**? | angry, humorous, solemn, sarcastic |
| **Attitude** | What does the writer **think or feel** about the subject? | approving, critical, sympathetic |
| **Mood** | What **feeling** does the passage create in the reader, or what is the **atmosphere** of a scene? | tense, peaceful, gloomy, joyful |
| **Purpose** | **Why** was it written? | to inform, persuade, entertain, warn, criticise |
:::

They often work together. A writer who is **critical** of litter (attitude) may use a **sarcastic** voice (tone) in order to **persuade** readers to change their habits (purpose), and may describe overflowing gutters so vividly that the passage feels **disgusting** (mood).

## A bank of precise tone words

*"Good"*, *"bad"*, *"nice"* and *"emotional"* are not tones. Learn words like these:

| Positive | Negative | Neutral / other |
|---|---|---|
| admiring | bitter | objective |
| appreciative | critical | factual, informative |
| hopeful, optimistic | sarcastic | formal |
| sympathetic, compassionate | scornful, contemptuous | conversational, informal |
| humorous, light-hearted | indignant, angry | reflective, thoughtful |
| enthusiastic | pessimistic, gloomy | nostalgic |
| encouraging | mocking | serious, solemn |
| affectionate | cynical | persuasive |

:::callout{type=tip}
Learn the **difference** between close words. **Sarcastic** means saying the opposite of what you mean in order to mock. **Ironic** is broader: a gap between what is said or expected and what is real. **Cynical** means believing people act only from selfish motives. Using the precise word is what earns the mark.
:::

## Five clues to the writer's voice

1. **Loaded words**: words that carry feeling. *"Our **brave** nurses"* (admiring) versus *"so-called experts"* (scornful); *"a **modest** home"* (sympathetic) versus *"a **miserable** shack"* (critical).
2. **Punctuation**: exclamation marks, rhetorical questions and dashes often show strong feeling.
3. **Praise and blame**: who gets the credit and who gets the blame?
4. **Exaggeration or understatement**: hyperbole may signal humour or anger; deliberate understatement often signals irony.
5. **Imagery**: dark, heavy pictures create a gloomy mood; bright, busy ones create a lively, hopeful mood.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage:** *"Once again, the council has promised to fix the Wilkinson Road potholes before the rains. Once again, the rains have come. The potholes, it seems, have decided to stay and raise families."*

**Question:** What is the writer's attitude to the council? Give a reason for your answer.

**Clue hunt:**

- *"Once again… Once again"*: repetition shows the promise has been broken many times.
- *"have decided to stay and raise families"*: humorous personification; the potholes are growing and multiplying because nothing is done.

**Answer:** The writer is **critical and sarcastic** about the council, because he repeats *"once again"* to stress its broken promises and jokes that the potholes are *"raising families"*, suggesting they have been left to grow for years.
:::

## Same facts, different voices

The same event can be written in completely different tones. Notice how the **words**, not the facts, change.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**The event:** a new road between Kailahun and Kenema has just been opened.

| Tone | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **Hopeful, appreciative** | *At last, farmers in Kailahun can reach the Kenema market in hours instead of days, and their produce arrives fresh.* |
| **Cynical, sarcastic** | *The road that was promised in three elections has finally appeared, just in time for the fourth.* |
| **Objective, factual** | *The 80-kilometre road was opened on Monday by the Minister of Works.* |
| **Nostalgic** | *I remember when this journey took two days, with a night under a mango tree at Pendembu.* |
:::

## How to write a tone answer

1. **Name** the tone or attitude with one or two precise words.
2. **Prove** it with evidence: quote or describe the words or details that show it.
3. Keep it short: one or two sentences.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Describing the subject instead of the tone.** *"The writer is talking about potholes"* tells the examiner the topic. Ask: *How does the writer feel about it, and how does it sound?*
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Naming a tone without proof.** *"The tone is sarcastic"* may earn half the mark. *"The tone is sarcastic, as shown by…"* earns it all.
:::

## Practice passage

::::model{label="Practice passage" title="The new phone"}
My cousin Alhaji bought a new phone last week, and since then he has been busy. He is busy photographing his breakfast. He is busy recording videos of himself walking down Siaka Stevens Street, in case the people of Freetown have forgotten what he looks like. He is far too busy, of course, to help his mother carry water, or to look at the books that have been gathering dust on his table since the first day of the holidays. When his mother calls him, he answers, *"One minute, Mama!"* It is a remarkable minute. It has now lasted six days.
::::

:::reveal{title="1. What is the writer's attitude to Alhaji? Give a reason."}
The writer is **critical and mocking** of Alhaji: he ridicules him for filming himself *"in case the people of Freetown have forgotten what he looks like"* and for being *"far too busy"* to help his mother or study.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. What is the tone of the last two sentences?"}
**Sarcastic / ironic**: *"a remarkable minute"* that *"has now lasted six days"* says the opposite of what is meant, to mock Alhaji's broken promise.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. What do you think is the writer's purpose?"}
To **criticise**, in an amusing way, young people who spend all their time on their phones and neglect their duties, and perhaps to **persuade** readers not to behave like Alhaji.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Find one example of repetition and say what it adds."}
*"He is busy… He is busy… He is far too busy"*. The repetition builds up the mockery: each "busy" activity is more pointless than the last, until the final one shows what he is too busy to do.
:::

## Key points

- **Tone** is how it sounds; **attitude** is what the writer thinks; **mood** is the feeling created; **purpose** is why it was written.
- Use **precise** words: *critical, admiring, sarcastic, sympathetic, nostalgic, objective*.
- Find the clues: **loaded words, punctuation, praise and blame, exaggeration, imagery**.
- Answer with **a tone word plus evidence** from the passage.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Questions ask *"What is the writer's attitude to…?"*, *"What is the tone of the passage?"*, *"What mood does the last paragraph create?"* or *"What is the writer's purpose?"*. The mark usually needs **both** an accurate word **and** support from the passage. A vague word (*"bad"*, *"emotional"*) or an unsupported one rarely earns full marks.
:::
