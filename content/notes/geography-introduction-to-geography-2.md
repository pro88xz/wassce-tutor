# Geographical enquiry: spatial patterns, scale and human–environment systems

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- frame a geographical question that can be investigated with evidence
- distinguish location, distribution, pattern, process and interaction
- explain how geographical scale changes the interpretation of an issue
- organise evidence from maps, field observations and statistics
- construct a reasoned geographical conclusion

## From a question to an investigation

Geographical enquiry is a systematic process of asking a question about places or spatial relationships, collecting relevant evidence, analysing it and communicating a conclusion.

A useful question is specific, spatial and answerable. “Why is the environment changing?” is too broad. “How does distance from an unpaved road affect the amount of roadside soil erosion observed after heavy rainfall?” identifies a process, a measurable variable and a study area.

A sound investigation follows a sequence:

1. **Identify the problem.** Describe the geographical issue and define the study area.
2. **Set objectives or hypotheses.** State what the investigation will find out or test.
3. **Select methods.** Decide which observations, measurements, maps or records are needed.
4. **Collect data.** Use a consistent sampling method and record date, location and units.
5. **Present data.** Choose a suitable table, map, graph, profile or diagram.
6. **Analyse and interpret.** Identify patterns, compare groups and consider possible explanations.
7. **Conclude and recommend.** Answer the original question using the evidence, then state practical implications.

## Location, distribution and spatial pattern

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

- **Location** identifies where a feature is found.
- **Distribution** describes the area over which the feature occurs.
- **Pattern** describes the arrangement of features within that distribution.
- **Process** explains how a pattern is produced or changed.
- **Interaction** describes how places, people or environmental systems influence one another.

Settlements along a major road may form a linear pattern. A group of shops around a market may form a clustered pattern. Farms spread across a broad area may appear dispersed. A pattern is a description; explaining why it exists requires evidence about access, relief, water, land ownership, employment and other relevant factors.

## Scale and geographical explanation

Geographical issues operate at different scales.

| Scale | Example of a question |
|---|---|
| Local | Which streets in a Freetown community experience the most surface runoff? |
| National | How is access to major roads distributed across Sierra Leone? |
| Regional | How do transport links connect Sierra Leone with neighbouring countries? |
| Global | How can changes in global climate affect rainfall risks in West Africa? |

An explanation at one scale may be incomplete at another. A local trader's location may depend on foot traffic and rent, while the wider success of the market may depend on national transport networks and regional trade. Good geographical analysis identifies the scale relevant to the question and recognises connections to other scales.

## Human–environment systems

A system is a set of connected components that interact. A drainage basin, for example, receives water through precipitation. Some water infiltrates the soil, some is stored, and some flows over the surface into streams and rivers. Vegetation, soil type, slope and land use influence the movement of water.

Human activity can alter the system. Removing vegetation may reduce interception and expose soil. Roads and roofs create impermeable surfaces that increase rapid surface runoff. Poorly maintained drains can restrict the movement of water. These changes may increase flood risk, especially where people build on floodplains or natural drainage channels.

A geographical explanation should avoid claiming that one factor always causes an outcome. Flooding usually results from several interacting conditions, and their relative importance varies from place to place.

## Working with evidence

Different sources answer different questions:

- **Topographical maps** show relief, drainage, settlements and transport routes.
- **Aerial photographs and satellite images** help compare land cover and settlement expansion.
- **Field observations** record conditions at the time of the visit.
- **Measurements** provide numerical evidence such as slope, distance, rainfall or river width.
- **Census and administrative statistics** describe population and economic conditions, but their dates and definitions must be checked.

Before using evidence, ask whether it is relevant, sufficiently recent, representative and accurately recorded. A single observation cannot establish a long-term trend. A map may omit features because of its scale or date. Statistics from different years may not be directly comparable if the boundaries or definitions changed.

## Worked example: testing a settlement pattern

A student wants to find out whether shops are more concentrated near a main road than farther away.

The student selects three equal-length road sections and records the number of shops in each. The student also measures the perpendicular distance of each shop from the main road.

Suppose the observations show 24 shops within 100 metres of the road and 8 shops beyond 100 metres. This suggests a higher concentration near the road in the sampled area. It does not, by itself, prove that road access is the only cause. The student should consider population density, rent, public transport, market location and the limits of the sample.

A strong conclusion would state what the data show, identify the study area and acknowledge alternative explanations.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not confuse correlation with proof of causation. Two features may occur together because of a third factor, or because the relationship works in both directions. Use evidence and qualify conclusions appropriately.
:::

## Presenting geographical findings

Choose a method that makes the evidence clear:

- use a **dot map** to show the location of individual observations
- use a **bar graph** to compare separate categories
- use a **line graph** to show change over time
- use a **proportional symbol map** to compare quantities by location
- use a **cross-profile** to represent the shape of a slope or valley

Every graph or map should have a clear title, labels, units, a key where necessary and a stated source. A conclusion should refer to the evidence rather than merely repeat the title.

## WASSCE examination focus

For questions on geographical investigation, organise answers in a logical sequence: problem, objectives, methods, presentation, analysis and conclusion. In essays, distinguish description from explanation. Description states the observed pattern; explanation gives the processes and factors responsible for it.
