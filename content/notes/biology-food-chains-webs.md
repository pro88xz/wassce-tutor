Every living thing needs energy, and almost all of it starts as **sunlight**. Green plants trap it; a grasshopper eats the plant; a lizard eats the grasshopper; a snake eats the lizard; a hawk eats the snake. Each step is a link in a **food chain**, and the chains of a habitat join into a **food web**. Following the energy along these links explains why there are always fewer hawks than grasshoppers, why food chains are short, and why eating plants feeds more people than eating meat. This lesson covers food chains, food webs, trophic levels, ecological pyramids and the flow of energy.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- construct food chains and identify trophic levels
- interpret and construct a food web
- predict the effect of removing one organism from a food web
- describe pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy
- explain energy flow and the loss of energy at each trophic level
- calculate the efficiency of energy transfer

## Food chains

:::callout{type=definition}
A **food chain** shows the feeding relationships in which energy passes from one organism to the next. The **arrow** points from the food to the organism that eats it: it shows the **direction of energy flow**.

$$\text{grass} \rightarrow \text{grasshopper} \rightarrow \text{lizard} \rightarrow \text{snake} \rightarrow \text{hawk}$$
:::

| Trophic level | Name | In the chain above |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | **producer** | grass |
| 2nd | **primary consumer** (herbivore) | grasshopper |
| 3rd | **secondary consumer** (carnivore) | lizard |
| 4th | **tertiary consumer** | snake |
| 5th | **quaternary consumer** (top carnivore) | hawk |

**Aquatic example (the coast):** phytoplankton → zooplankton → bonga (small fish) → barracuda → human.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Arrows pointing the wrong way.** "grass ← grasshopper" means the grass eats the grasshopper. The arrow always points **towards the eater**, the way energy moves.
:::

## Food webs

In a real habitat most animals eat more than one kind of food, and are eaten by more than one predator. The chains interlink into a **food web**.

![A farmland food web: grass and maize are eaten by grasshoppers, caterpillars, rats and weaver birds; lizards eat grasshoppers and caterpillars; snakes eat lizards, rats and weaver birds; the hawk eats snakes, lizards, rats and weaver birds](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/food-web.svg)

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Using the farmland food web, (a) write a food chain with four organisms, (b) name an organism that is both a secondary and a tertiary consumer, (c) predict what happens if the snakes are killed.**

**(a)** maize → rat → snake → hawk

**(b)** The **hawk**: it is a secondary consumer when it eats a rat, and a tertiary (or higher) consumer when it eats a snake.

**(c)** With no snakes, more **rats, lizards and weaver birds** survive at first, so they increase; they then eat more maize, grass, grasshoppers and caterpillars, which decrease. The hawk loses one food source but can eat more rats and lizards, so it may be little affected.
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
**Predicting changes in a web:** trace every arrow **into** and **out of** the organism removed. Its prey increase (fewer eaten); its predators may decrease or switch to other food; and the change passes on along the chain. Use "**may**" and give the reason, because webs are complex.
:::

## Energy flow

![Energy flows from the sun to producers (10 000 kJ), primary consumers (1000 kJ), secondary (100 kJ) and tertiary consumers (10 kJ); at each step most energy is lost as heat, in waste and in uneaten parts](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/energy-flow.svg)

Only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next. The rest is lost:

- as **heat** from **respiration** (most of the loss)
- in **movement** and keeping warm
- in parts that are **not eaten** (roots, bones, fur)
- in **faeces and urine**, which go to decomposers

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Grass traps 20 000 kJ of energy. The grasshoppers that eat it gain 2000 kJ, and the lizards that eat the grasshoppers gain 180 kJ. Calculate the efficiency of energy transfer from grasshoppers to lizards.**

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{energy passed on}}{\text{energy available}} \times 100 = \frac{180}{2000} \times 100 = 9\%$$
:::

### Why food chains are short

Because so much energy is lost at each step, after four or five levels there is too little left to support another population. That is why **top carnivores are few** and food chains rarely have more than five links.

### Why plant food feeds more people

A field of rice feeds people directly with the energy at the **producer** level. Feeding the rice to chickens and then eating the chickens passes the energy through one more level, losing about 90% of it. The same field feeds **many more people** as rice than as chicken.

## Ecological pyramids

![Three pyramids: a pyramid of numbers for grass, grasshoppers, frogs and a snake; a pyramid of numbers for one tree, many caterpillars and fewer birds, which is not a true pyramid; and a pyramid of energy, always upright](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/ecological-pyramids.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
| Pyramid | Each bar shows | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **numbers** | the **number** of organisms at each level | usually upright; can be **inverted** or spindle-shaped when one large producer (a tree) feeds many small consumers, or with parasites |
| **biomass** | the **dry mass** of organisms at each level | usually upright; can be inverted in the sea, where fast-reproducing phytoplankton have a small biomass at any moment |
| **energy** | the **energy** at each level over a period (kJ per m² per year) | **always upright**, because energy is lost at each step |
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Saying all pyramids are upright.** Pyramids of numbers and of biomass can be inverted. Only the **pyramid of energy** is **always** upright.
:::

## Decomposers and detritus

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (earthworms, termites, millipedes) feed on dead remains and wastes from **every** trophic level. They release the energy as heat by respiration and return **mineral salts** to the soil for producers. Without them, dead matter would pile up and nutrients would be locked away.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Termites in the savanna.** The large termite mounds of northern Sierra Leone are decomposer factories: termites feed on dead wood and grass, with microorganisms in their guts digesting the cellulose. They recycle nutrients from dead plants back into the soil, and they are themselves food for birds, lizards and even people at the start of the rains.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. In the chain maize → rat → snake → hawk, name the primary consumer and the trophic level of the snake."}
Primary consumer: **rat**. The snake is at the **third** trophic level (secondary consumer).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Why is the arrow in a food chain drawn from the food to the feeder?"}
It shows the **direction of energy flow**: energy passes from the food to the organism that eats it.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give three ways energy is lost between trophic levels."}
Any three: heat from respiration; movement; parts not eaten; faeces and urine.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Producers trap 50 000 kJ; primary consumers gain 4000 kJ. Calculate the efficiency."}
$\dfrac{4000}{50\,000} \times 100 = 8\%$.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Explain why a pyramid of numbers for a mango tree, caterpillars and birds is not pyramid-shaped."}
One large tree supports **many** caterpillars, so the producer bar is **narrower** than the primary consumer bar. Numbers ignore the **size** of organisms.
:::

## Key points

- **Food chain**: arrows show **energy flow**, from food to feeder.
- **Trophic levels**: producer, primary, secondary, tertiary consumer.
- **Food web** = interlinked chains; removing one organism affects many others.
- Only about **10%** of energy passes to the next level; the rest is lost, mainly as **heat from respiration**.
- Efficiency $= \dfrac{\text{energy passed on}}{\text{energy available}} \times 100$.
- Pyramids of **numbers and biomass** can be inverted; the pyramid of **energy** is always upright.
