Ice melts in a cold drink, water boils in a pot of rice, and naphthalene balls in a wardrobe slowly disappear without ever becoming liquid. Each is a change of state, and each involves energy. This lesson explains the changes of state, the heating curve, evaporation and boiling, and the substances that absorb or lose water from the air.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name and describe the changes of state, including sublimation
- interpret a heating curve and explain the flat sections using latent heat
- distinguish evaporation from boiling and state the factors that affect evaporation
- explain how pressure affects boiling point
- describe the diffusion of ammonia and hydrogen chloride
- define deliquescent, efflorescent and hygroscopic substances

## Changes of state

| Change | From → to | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| **Melting (fusion)** | solid → liquid | Absorbed |
| **Freezing (solidification)** | liquid → solid | Released |
| **Boiling / evaporation (vaporisation)** | liquid → gas | Absorbed |
| **Condensation (liquefaction)** | gas → liquid | Released |
| **Sublimation** | solid → gas directly (and back) | Absorbed |

Substances that **sublime** include **iodine**, **ammonium chloride**, **naphthalene** (mothballs) and **solid carbon dioxide** ("dry ice").

## The heating curve

![Heating curve of water: temperature rises to 0 °C, stays flat while ice melts, rises to 100 °C, stays flat while water boils, then rises again as steam](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/heating-curve.svg)

*Figure 1. Heating ice steadily until it becomes steam. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- On the **sloping parts**, the energy supplied raises the **kinetic energy** of the particles, so the temperature rises.
- On the **flat parts**, the temperature stays constant even though heat is still supplied. The energy is used to **overcome the forces between particles**, not to speed them up. This energy is called **latent heat**:
  - **latent heat of fusion** at the melting point
  - **latent heat of vaporisation** at the boiling point
- The boiling plateau is **longer** than the melting plateau: separating particles completely into a gas needs much more energy than loosening them into a liquid.

A **cooling curve** is the reverse: the temperature stays constant while the substance freezes, because latent heat is **released**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Reading a heating curve.** A pure solid is heated steadily. Its temperature stays at 80 °C from minute 3 to minute 7, then rises.

- The melting point is **80 °C**.
- Between minutes 3 and 7, the substance is a **mixture of solid and liquid**.
- The heat supplied is overcoming forces between particles (latent heat of fusion).
- An **impure** sample would melt over a **range** below 80 °C, with no sharp plateau.
:::

## Evaporation and boiling

| Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|
| Happens at **any temperature** | Happens at a **fixed temperature** (the boiling point) for a given pressure |
| Only at the **surface** | Throughout the liquid: **bubbles** form inside |
| **Slow** | **Fast** |
| Causes **cooling** (the fastest particles escape) | Temperature stays constant while it boils |

**Factors that increase the rate of evaporation:**

- higher **temperature**
- larger **surface area**
- **wind** (air movement), which removes the vapour
- **lower humidity** (drier air)
- **lower pressure**

**Evaporation causes cooling:** the particles with the most energy escape, so the average energy of those left falls. This is why sweating cools the body, and why water in a clay pot stays cool.

### Boiling point and pressure

A liquid boils when its **vapour pressure equals the external pressure**.

- **Lower pressure** (high mountains) → **lower** boiling point; food takes longer to cook.
- **Higher pressure** (pressure cooker) → **higher** boiling point; food cooks faster.
- **Impurities** (dissolved salt) **raise** the boiling point of water.

## Diffusion of gases: the ammonia–hydrogen chloride experiment

![A long glass tube with ammonia-soaked cotton at one end and hydrochloric acid-soaked cotton at the other; a white ring of ammonium chloride forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/diffusion-tube.svg)

*Figure 2. Ammonia diffuses faster than hydrogen chloride. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

The two gases diffuse along the tube and react where they meet:

$$\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + \text{HCl(g)} \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl(s)}$$

The white ring forms **nearer the hydrochloric acid end**, because ammonia molecules (M = 17) are **lighter** and diffuse **faster** than hydrogen chloride molecules (M = 36.5). The next topic gives the exact relationship (Graham's law).

## Substances and water vapour in the air

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliquescent** | Absorbs so much water vapour from the air that it **dissolves** and forms a solution | Sodium hydroxide, calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, iron(III) chloride |
| **Hygroscopic** | Absorbs water vapour from the air but does **not dissolve** (or, for liquids, becomes diluted) | Concentrated sulphuric acid, calcium oxide, anhydrous copper(II) sulphate |
| **Efflorescent** | **Loses** water of crystallisation to the air and becomes powdery | Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O), Glauber's salt (Na₂SO₄·10H₂O) |

Hygroscopic and deliquescent substances are used as **drying agents** (desiccants). For example, calcium chloride and silica gel keep tablets and electronic goods dry.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Table salt in Freetown often becomes damp and lumpy in the rainy season. Pure sodium chloride is not deliquescent, but the **magnesium chloride** and calcium chloride impurities in sea salt are, and they absorb moisture from the very humid air. Rice grains are added to salt shakers to absorb some of the moisture.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
On the flat part of a heating curve the substance **is still absorbing heat**. The temperature stops rising because the energy goes into breaking the forces between particles, not into increasing their kinetic energy.
:::

## Key points

- Changes of state: **melting, freezing, boiling, evaporation, condensation, sublimation**; melting, boiling and sublimation absorb energy.
- On a heating curve, **flat sections** are changes of state where **latent heat** overcomes the forces between particles.
- **Evaporation** happens at any temperature at the surface and causes cooling; **boiling** happens throughout the liquid at a fixed temperature.
- **Lower pressure lowers** the boiling point; impurities **raise** it.
- **Deliquescent** substances absorb water and dissolve; **hygroscopic** ones absorb water without dissolving; **efflorescent** ones lose water to the air.

## Quick check

1. Name two substances that sublime.
2. Why does the temperature stay constant while water boils?
3. Give two differences between evaporation and boiling.
4. Distinguish a deliquescent substance from an efflorescent one, with an example of each.

**Answers:**

1. Any two: iodine, ammonium chloride, naphthalene, solid carbon dioxide.
2. The heat supplied is used to overcome the forces between water molecules (latent heat of vaporisation), not to increase their kinetic energy.
3. Any two: evaporation occurs at any temperature, boiling at a fixed temperature; evaporation occurs at the surface, boiling throughout; evaporation is slow, boiling fast; evaporation causes cooling.
4. A deliquescent substance absorbs water from the air and dissolves (e.g. sodium hydroxide); an efflorescent substance loses water of crystallisation to the air (e.g. washing soda).
