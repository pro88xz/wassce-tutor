# Human reproduction: fertilisation, pregnancy, birth and reproductive health

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- describe the events from fertilisation to implantation
- explain the roles of the placenta, umbilical cord and foetal membranes
- describe the development and nourishment of the embryo and fetus
- explain the hormonal maintenance of pregnancy at syllabus level
- describe the stages of childbirth and the role of breastfeeding
- distinguish identical and fraternal twins
- explain responsible reproductive health and prevention of sexually transmitted infections

## Fertilisation and early development

During sexual intercourse, semen is deposited in the vagina. Sperm move through the cervix and uterus towards the oviduct. If an ovum is present, a sperm may reach and penetrate its protective coverings.

When one sperm successfully fuses with the ovum, the membranes change in ways that help prevent additional sperm from entering. The nuclei of the sperm and ovum fuse, restoring the diploid chromosome number. This forms a **zygote**.

The zygote undergoes repeated mitotic divisions called cleavage. It becomes a ball of cells and then a blastocyst. The blastocyst travels to the uterus and attaches to the endometrium. This process is called **implantation**.

The embryo begins to form specialised tissues and organs. During early development, cells differentiate and organise into body systems. After the early embryonic period, the developing human is commonly called a fetus. Growth and maturation continue until birth.

## The placenta and umbilical cord

The placenta is a temporary organ that develops in the uterus during pregnancy. It forms an exchange surface between the maternal and fetal circulations.

Its functions include:

1. transferring oxygen from the mother's blood to the fetus
2. transferring digested nutrients, water and mineral ions to the fetus
3. transferring carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes from the fetus to the mother
4. producing hormones that help maintain pregnancy
5. providing a selective barrier against some harmful substances, although it does not block every drug, pathogen or toxin

Maternal blood flows through spaces around the chorionic villi, while fetal blood flows through capillaries inside the villi. The two blood supplies normally remain separate, with substances passing across a thin exchange surface.

The **umbilical cord** connects the fetus to the placenta. It normally contains two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein:
- the umbilical vein carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus
- the two umbilical arteries carry blood containing carbon dioxide and other wastes from the fetus to the placenta

The names of these vessels are based on the direction in which blood travels relative to the fetal heart. Do not assume that every artery carries oxygenated blood or every vein carries deoxygenated blood.

## Foetal membranes and protection

The developing embryo is surrounded by membranes that support and protect it.

- **Amnion**: surrounds the embryo and encloses amniotic fluid.
- **Amniotic fluid**: cushions the developing fetus against mechanical shock, allows movement and helps maintain a relatively stable environment.
- **Chorion**: the outer foetal membrane; contributes to formation of the placenta.
- **Allantois**: contributes to structures associated with the umbilical cord and placental development in humans.

Amniotic fluid is not the fetus's food supply. Nutrients and oxygen are transferred through the placenta and umbilical cord.

## Hormonal support of pregnancy

After ovulation, the corpus luteum produces progesterone. If implantation occurs, the developing embryo produces **human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG)**. hCG helps maintain the corpus luteum during early pregnancy so that progesterone production continues.

Progesterone helps maintain the endometrium and reduces uterine contractions during early pregnancy. The placenta later becomes a major source of progesterone and oestrogen. These hormones support the uterine lining and the continuation of pregnancy.

Pregnancy involves several interacting hormones. Avoid the oversimplified claim that one hormone alone controls every stage.

## Birth (parturition)

**Parturition** is the process of childbirth. Near the end of pregnancy, hormonal and mechanical signals contribute to the onset of labour. The uterus contracts rhythmically, and the cervix gradually dilates to allow the baby to pass through the birth canal.

The main stages are:

1. **Dilation stage**: uterine contractions cause the cervix to soften, thin and widen. The amniotic sac may rupture, releasing amniotic fluid.
2. **Expulsion stage**: strong uterine contractions push the baby through the cervix and vagina.
3. **Afterbirth stage**: the placenta and remaining foetal membranes are expelled.

After birth, the umbilical cord is clamped and cut. The newborn must begin breathing independently and maintain its body temperature.

## Lactation and breastfeeding

**Lactation** is the production and release of milk by the mammary glands. The hormone prolactin stimulates milk production, while oxytocin causes contraction of cells around the milk-producing structures, helping milk to be released (the let-down reflex).

Breast milk provides water, nutrients and protective immune factors. Colostrum, the first milk produced after birth, is especially rich in antibodies and other protective substances. Breastfeeding can support infant nutrition and protection against some infections.

## Twins

Twins may develop in two main ways.

**Identical (monozygotic) twins** form when one fertilised egg divides into two embryos. They usually have the same genetic information and are generally the same sex, although rare biological exceptions occur.

**Fraternal (dizygotic) twins** form when two separate ova are released and fertilised by two different sperm cells. They are genetically similar to ordinary siblings and may be the same or different sexes.

| Identical twins | Fraternal twins |
|---|---|
| Develop from one zygote that divides | Develop from two separate zygotes |
| Usually have almost identical inherited genetic information | Share, on average, about half their segregating genes, like other siblings |
| Usually the same sex | May be the same or different sexes |
| May share some foetal membranes depending on when division occurs | Usually have separate chorions and amniotic sacs, though placentas may appear joined |

## Reproductive health and sexually transmitted infections

Reproductive health includes physical, emotional and social well-being in matters relating to the reproductive system. It includes accurate information, access to appropriate healthcare, prevention and treatment of infections, and respectful decision-making.

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are infections that can spread mainly through sexual contact. Examples include gonorrhoea, syphilis, chlamydia, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV) and HIV. Some STIs can also be transmitted through infected blood or from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, depending on the infection.

Prevention and risk reduction include:
- abstaining from sexual activity
- mutual monogamy where both partners have tested negative and remain faithful
- correct and consistent use of condoms, which reduces but does not eliminate the risk of all STIs
- testing and prompt treatment, including partner notification where appropriate
- vaccination where available, such as HPV and hepatitis B vaccination
- avoiding sharing needles or other equipment that may be contaminated with blood

Many STIs can have no obvious symptoms. A person should not assume that the absence of symptoms means there is no infection. Diagnosis, treatment and counselling should be provided by qualified health professionals.

## WASSCE examination focus

- Fertilisation usually occurs in the oviduct; implantation occurs in the endometrium of the uterus.
- The placenta is an exchange organ and an endocrine organ. Maternal and fetal blood normally do not mix directly.
- The umbilical vein carries blood from the placenta to the fetus; the umbilical arteries carry blood from the fetus to the placenta.
- Amniotic fluid cushions the fetus and allows movement; it is not the source of nutrients.
- hCG maintains the corpus luteum in early pregnancy; progesterone helps maintain the uterine lining.
- Prolactin stimulates milk production; oxytocin causes milk ejection.
- Identical twins arise from one zygote; fraternal twins arise from two separately fertilised ova.

**Answers**

1. A diploid zygote.
2. Fertilisation is the fusion of gamete nuclei; implantation is the attachment and embedding of the early embryo in the uterine lining.
3. It transfers oxygen and nutrients to the fetus, removes fetal carbon dioxide and wastes, and produces hormones that help maintain pregnancy.
4. From the placenta towards the fetus.
5. It cushions the fetus against shock and allows movement in a protective fluid environment.
