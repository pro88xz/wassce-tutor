**Pan-Africanism** is the idea and movement that all people of African descent, in Africa and in the **diaspora**, share a common history and destiny and should **unite** to end racial oppression and colonial rule and to build a free, united Africa. It began among Black thinkers in the **Americas, the Caribbean and Britain**, and grew through a series of **Pan-African Congresses**: **London (1900)**, **Paris (1919)** and, most importantly, **Manchester (1945)**, where future leaders such as **Kwame Nkrumah** and **Jomo Kenyatta** demanded **independence**. After Ghana's independence it moved to Africa with the **All-African Peoples' Conference in Accra (1958)** and led to the **Organisation of African Unity (1963)**. This lesson explains the **meaning, origins and growth of Pan-Africanism**.

## Meaning and aims

:::callout{type=definition}
**Pan-Africanism** is the belief that people of African origin everywhere share common interests and should **unite** for their **freedom, dignity and progress**. Politically, it aimed at the **end of colonial rule** and the **unity of Africa**.
:::

**Aims of Pan-Africanism:**

1. To **restore the dignity** of Africans and fight **racism** and racial discrimination.
2. To **end colonial rule** and win **independence** for all African countries.
3. To promote the **unity** of Africans at home and abroad.
4. To promote **pride in African history and culture**.
5. To achieve **economic and social progress** for Africans.

## Origins: why Pan-Africanism began in the diaspora

1. **The Atlantic slave trade and slavery** scattered Africans across the Americas, where they suffered **racial oppression**.
2. **Racist ideas** that Africans were inferior provoked educated Black people to defend African dignity.
3. The **colonisation of Africa** after 1880 made the struggle international.
4. Black people in the Americas and Britain had **education and freedom of expression** to organise.
5. **Back-to-Africa** ideas and the founding of **Sierra Leone** and **Liberia** connected the diaspora with Africa.

## Leading Pan-Africanists

| Person | Background | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Edward Wilmot Blyden** (1832–1912) | born in the Danish West Indies; lived in **Liberia** and **Sierra Leone** | wrote about the **"African personality"** and the value of African culture; often called a **father of Pan-Africanism** |
| **Henry Sylvester Williams** (1869–1911) | Trinidadian lawyer in London | organised the **first Pan-African Conference, London, 1900** |
| **W. E. B. Du Bois** (1868–1963) | African American scholar | led the **Pan-African Congresses of 1919–1927**; co-chaired Manchester (1945); died in **Ghana** |
| **Marcus Garvey** (1887–1940) | Jamaican | founded the **Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA)** in 1914; "**Africa for the Africans**"; the **Black Star Line** shipping company |
| **George Padmore** (1903–1959) | Trinidadian | organiser of the **Manchester Congress**; later adviser to Nkrumah |
| **Kwame Nkrumah** (1909–1972) | Gold Coast | joint secretary at Manchester; made **Ghana** the centre of Pan-Africanism; called for a **United States of Africa** |

| Meeting | Place and date | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Pan-African Conference** | **London, 1900** | first use of "Pan-African" for a meeting; protested against racism and colonial abuses |
| **First Pan-African Congress** | **Paris, 1919** | led by Du Bois during the peace conference; asked for African rights under the League of Nations |
| **Congresses of 1921, 1923, 1927** | London, Brussels, Paris, Lisbon, New York | kept the movement alive; mostly diaspora members; moderate demands |
| **Fifth Pan-African Congress** | **Manchester, October 1945** | **turning point**: Africans dominated; demanded **independence**; attended by Nkrumah, Kenyatta, Padmore, Du Bois, Wallace-Johnson and others |
| **All-African Peoples' Conference** | **Accra, December 1958** | first major Pan-African meeting held **in Africa**; brought nationalist leaders together, including **Patrice Lumumba** |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why the Manchester Congress (1945) was a turning point**

1. For the first time, **Africans from Africa**, not the diaspora, were the majority.
2. It demanded **complete independence**, not just reforms.
3. It supported **mass action**, including strikes and boycotts, if colonial powers refused.
4. Its delegates, such as **Nkrumah** and **Kenyatta**, went home to **lead independence movements**.
5. It moved the centre of Pan-Africanism from the diaspora **to Africa**.
:::

## Pan-Africanism after independence

After Ghana's independence in **1957**, Nkrumah declared that "**the independence of Ghana is meaningless unless it is linked up with the total liberation of the African continent**". Ghana:

1. hosted the **Conference of Independent African States** (**April 1958**) and the **All-African Peoples' Conference** (**December 1958**);
2. supported **liberation movements** with money and training;
3. formed the **Ghana–Guinea Union (1958)**, later joined by **Mali (1961)**, as a step towards African unity.

Leaders disagreed over **how** to unite Africa:

| Casablanca Group (1961) | Monrovia Group (1961) |
|---|---|
| **radical**: Ghana, Guinea, Mali, Morocco, Egypt and others | **moderate**: Nigeria, Liberia, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, Sierra Leone and others |
| wanted **immediate political union** (a United States of Africa) | wanted **gradual cooperation** and respect for each state's **sovereignty** |

The two groups came together to form the **Organisation of African Unity** in **1963**, studied in the next lesson.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone has a special place in Pan-African history. **Freetown** was founded as a home for freed Africans from the diaspora; **Edward Blyden** lived and taught there; **Fourah Bay College** educated Africans from all over West Africa; and **I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson** attended the Manchester Congress. Sierra Leone joined the **Monrovia Group** and was a **founding member of the OAU** in 1963.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not confuse **Pan-Africanism** with **nationalism**. Nationalism aimed at the independence of **one country**; Pan-Africanism aimed at the **liberation and unity of all Africans** everywhere. The two supported each other.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Edward Wilmot Blyden** (1832–1912): a scholar born in the Danish West Indies who lived in Liberia and Sierra Leone and wrote about the dignity of Africans.
- **Henry Sylvester Williams** (1869–1911): the Trinidadian lawyer in London who organised the first Pan-African Conference in London in 1900.
- **W. E. B. Du Bois**: the African American scholar who organised Pan-African congresses from 1919 onwards.
- **Marcus Garvey**: the Jamaican leader who called on people of African descent to take pride in Africa and look to it as their home.
- **Manchester Congress** (1945): the Pan-African Congress, attended by future leaders such as Nkrumah, that turned the movement towards independence.
:::

## Key points

- **Pan-Africanism**: unity of all people of African descent against **racism and colonialism**.
- It began in the **diaspora** because of slavery, racism and colonisation.
- Leaders: **Blyden, Sylvester Williams, Du Bois, Garvey, Padmore, Nkrumah**.
- Meetings: **London 1900**, **Paris 1919**, **Manchester 1945** (turning point), **Accra 1958**.
- After 1957 Ghana led the movement; **Casablanca** and **Monrovia** groups joined in the **OAU (1963)**.
