Rub a plastic comb on dry hair and it picks up scraps of paper. Take off a nylon shirt in the harmattan and it crackles. Lightning flashes across the sky in the rainy season. All are static electricity: charges that build up and stay put — until they suddenly jump.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain charging by friction in terms of electron transfer
- state the law of electrostatics and distinguish conductors from insulators
- describe charging by contact and by induction
- describe the gold-leaf electroscope and its uses

## Charge and electrons

All matter is made of atoms containing positive **protons** (in the nucleus) and negative **electrons** (around it). A neutral object has equal numbers of each.

:::callout{type=definition}
An object becomes **charged** when it **gains or loses electrons**. Gaining electrons makes it **negative**; losing electrons makes it **positive**. Protons do not move: only **electrons** are transferred.
:::

The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb (C)**. One electron carries $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

## Charging by friction (rubbing)

When two different insulators are rubbed together, electrons move from one to the other:

| Rubbed together | Becomes negative | Becomes positive |
|---|---|---|
| polythene rod and wool | polythene (gains electrons) | wool |
| glass (or Perspex) rod and silk | silk | glass (loses electrons) |
| plastic comb and dry hair | comb | hair |

The two materials always gain **equal and opposite** charges.

## The law of electrostatics

:::callout{type=definition}
**Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.**
:::

A charged object also attracts small **uncharged** objects (such as scraps of paper) by inducing opposite charges on their near side.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Attraction does not prove that an object is charged** — a charged rod attracts neutral paper too. **Repulsion is the only sure test**: if two objects repel, both must be charged with the same sign.
:::

## Conductors and insulators

| Conductors | Insulators |
|---|---|
| allow charge to flow through them easily (they have free electrons) | do not allow charge to flow |
| metals (copper, aluminium), graphite, the human body, the Earth, water with impurities | plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, silk, dry air |

A conductor can be charged by rubbing only if it is held by an insulating handle; otherwise the charge flows away through the hand to the Earth (**earthing**).

## Charging by contact and by induction

**By contact (conduction):** touching a charged rod to a neutral conductor shares the charge; the conductor gets the **same sign** of charge as the rod.

**By induction** (charging without touching), for example to make a metal sphere **positive** using a **negative** rod:

1. Bring the negative rod **near** (not touching) the insulated sphere. Electrons in the sphere are repelled to the far side; the near side becomes positive.
2. **Earth** the sphere (touch it with a finger) while the rod is still near. The repelled electrons flow away to the Earth.
3. **Remove the earth connection first**, then remove the rod.
4. The sphere is left with a **positive** charge — **opposite** to the rod.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How many electrons must be removed from a neutral object to give it a charge of +3.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ C?**

$$n = \dfrac{Q}{e} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-18}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 20\ \text{electrons}$$
:::

## The gold-leaf electroscope

![A gold-leaf electroscope: a metal cap on a metal rod passing through an insulating plug into a case; a thin gold leaf hangs from the rod and rises when the instrument is charged](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/gold-leaf-electroscope.svg)

*Figure 1. The gold-leaf electroscope. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

When the cap is charged, the charge spreads over the rod and the leaf. The rod and leaf carry the **same** charge, so they **repel** and the leaf **diverges** (rises).

**Uses:**

1. **To detect charge**: the leaf rises if an object brought near the cap is charged.
2. **To find the sign of a charge**: charge the electroscope with a **known** sign (say positive). Bring the test object near the cap:
   - if the leaf **diverges further**, the object has the **same** charge;
   - if the leaf **collapses**, the object has the **opposite** charge **or** is uncharged — so only an **increase** in divergence is a reliable test.
3. **To compare conductors and insulators**: touch the cap of a charged electroscope with the material; a conductor discharges it quickly (leaf falls), an insulator does not.

## Key points

- Charging means gaining or losing **electrons**; protons do not move.
- Like charges repel, unlike attract; repulsion is the only sure test of charge.
- Conductors let charge flow; insulators do not.
- Induction gives a charge **opposite** to the inducing charge: bring near, earth, remove earth, remove rod.
- Gold-leaf electroscope: detects charge, tests its sign (increase in divergence), compares conductors.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**Explain charging by friction** in terms of electrons, **describe charging by induction step by step** (the order "remove the earth, then the rod" matters), and **describe the gold-leaf electroscope** — how it detects charge and how to find the sign of an unknown charge. :::

## Quick check

1. A polythene rod rubbed with wool becomes negative. Explain why.
2. What is the only sure test that a body is charged?
3. What sign of charge does a sphere get when charged by induction using a positive rod?

**Answers:** 1. Electrons are transferred from the wool to the polythene. 2. Repulsion (by a body of known charge). 3. Negative (opposite to the inducing rod).
