Chemists do not guess; they test ideas by careful experiment, measure accurately in agreed units and work safely with substances that can burn, poison or corrode. This lesson covers the scientific method, the units and apparatus used in chemistry, and the safety rules every WASSCE candidate must know for practicals.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the steps of the scientific method and identify variables in an experiment
- state SI units used in chemistry and convert between common units
- name common laboratory apparatus and their uses
- interpret hazard symbols and state laboratory safety rules
- distinguish the luminous and non-luminous Bunsen flames

## The scientific method

| Step | What happens | Example: rusting of roofing sheets |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | Notice something | Roofs near the sea in Freetown rust faster than roofs in Makeni |
| **Problem / question** | Ask why | Does salt speed up rusting? |
| **Hypothesis** | A testable explanation | "Salt water makes iron rust faster than pure water" |
| **Experiment** | Test the hypothesis fairly | Place iron nails in pure water and in salt water for a week |
| **Results** | Record observations and measurements | The nail in salt water is much more rusted |
| **Conclusion** | Accept or reject the hypothesis | Salt speeds up rusting; the hypothesis is supported |
| **Theory / law** | An explanation tested many times | Rusting needs water and oxygen; salt is an electrolyte that speeds it |

### Variables

- **Independent variable:** what you change on purpose (salt or no salt).
- **Dependent variable:** what you measure (amount of rust).
- **Controlled variables:** what you keep the same for a fair test (type and size of nail, volume of water, time, temperature).

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **hypothesis** is not a guess about the answer to a sum. It is a **testable** statement that predicts what will happen and can be shown to be right or wrong by experiment.
:::

## Measurement and units

| Quantity | SI unit | Symbol | Common units in chemistry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass | kilogram | kg | g (1 kg = 1000 g) |
| Length | metre | m | cm, nm |
| Time | second | s | min |
| Temperature | kelvin | K | °C |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol | |
| Electric current | ampere | A | |
| Volume | cubic metre | m³ | dm³ (litre) and cm³ |

**Useful conversions:**

- 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 litre
- temperature in K = temperature in °C + 273

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Converting units.**

1. 250 cm³ in dm³: $250 \div 1000 = 0.250$ dm³.
2. 27 °C in kelvin: $27 + 273 = 300$ K.
3. 2.5 kg in grams: $2.5 \times 1000 = 2500$ g.
:::

**Accuracy and precision:** read a measuring cylinder or burette at the **bottom of the meniscus**, with your eye level with it. A burette reads to **0.05 cm³**, a measuring cylinder only to about **0.5 cm³**, so use the burette or pipette when volume must be exact.

## Common laboratory apparatus

![Common laboratory apparatus: beaker, conical flask, round-bottomed flask, test tube, measuring cylinder, burette, pipette, funnel and Bunsen burner](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/lab-apparatus.svg)

*Figure 1. Apparatus every candidate should recognise. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Apparatus | Use |
|---|---|
| **Beaker** | Holding, mixing and heating liquids (rough volumes only) |
| **Conical flask** | Titrations and mixing; its shape reduces splashing |
| **Round-bottomed flask** | Heating liquids evenly, as in distillation |
| **Test tube / boiling tube** | Small-scale reactions and heating |
| **Measuring cylinder** | Measuring approximate volumes of liquids |
| **Burette** | Delivering accurately measured, variable volumes (titration) |
| **Pipette** | Delivering one fixed, accurate volume (e.g. 25.0 cm³) |
| **Filter funnel** | Filtration, with filter paper |
| **Evaporating dish** | Evaporating a solution to recover the solute |
| **Bunsen burner** | Heating |
| **Thermometer** | Measuring temperature |
| **Desiccator** | Keeping substances dry |

### The Bunsen flame

| Luminous (yellow) flame | Non-luminous (blue) flame |
|---|---|
| Air hole **closed** | Air hole **open** |
| Incomplete combustion; sooty | Complete combustion; clean |
| Cooler; wavy and unsteady | **Hotter**; steady |
| Used only as a safety flame, when the burner is not heating | Used for **heating** |

## Laboratory safety

### Hazard symbols

| Hazard | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Flammable** | Catches fire easily | Ethanol, petrol, kerosene |
| **Corrosive** | Destroys skin and materials | Concentrated acids, sodium hydroxide |
| **Toxic** | Can kill if swallowed, inhaled or absorbed | Mercury, lead compounds, chlorine |
| **Oxidising** | Provides oxygen; makes fires fiercer | Potassium manganate(VII), hydrogen peroxide |
| **Harmful / irritant** | Causes illness or irritation | Dilute acids, ammonia |
| **Explosive** | May explode on heating or shock | Some nitrates and peroxides |
| **Environmental hazard** | Harms living things in water and soil | Copper(II) sulphate, mercury |

### Safety rules

1. Wear a **lab coat** and **safety goggles**; tie back long hair.
2. **Never eat, drink or taste** anything in the laboratory.
3. Read labels and follow instructions; ask before mixing chemicals.
4. Point test tubes **away from yourself and others** when heating.
5. **Add acid to water**, never water to concentrated acid.
6. Use a **fume cupboard** for poisonous gases (chlorine, hydrogen sulphide).
7. Smell gases by **wafting**, never by sniffing directly.
8. Keep flammable liquids **away from flames**.
9. Report **all accidents and breakages** at once; clear spills safely.
10. Wash your hands after practical work.

### First aid

| Accident | Action |
|---|---|
| Acid or alkali on the skin | Wash with **plenty of cold running water** for several minutes |
| Chemical in the eye | Rinse with **running water** at once (eye-wash bottle); get help |
| Minor burn | Cool under cold water for at least 10 minutes |
| Swallowed chemical | Rinse the mouth; do **not** induce vomiting; get medical help |
| Clothing on fire | Smother with a **fire blanket**; stop, drop and roll |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Many school laboratories in Sierra Leone have no piped water, so keep a **bucket of clean water** and a bottle for rinsing eyes ready before any practical with acids or alkalis. A tin of **dry sand** is a cheap way to put out small chemical fires.
:::

## Key points

- Scientific method: **observation → problem → hypothesis → experiment → results → conclusion → theory**.
- A fair test changes **one independent variable**, measures **one dependent variable** and **controls** the rest.
- Know the **SI units**; 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³; K = °C + 273.
- Use the **burette and pipette** for accurate volumes; heat with the **non-luminous** flame.
- Know the **hazard symbols** and safety rules: goggles, acid to water, waft gases, wash spills with water.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect questions on the **steps of the scientific method**, on **naming apparatus and their uses** (especially burette, pipette and the condenser), on **hazard symbols**, and on **safety precautions** in practical work. Practical papers reward accurate readings with the right units.
:::

## Quick check

1. In a test of whether salt speeds up rusting, what is the dependent variable?
2. Convert 0.5 dm³ to cm³.
3. Why is the non-luminous flame used for heating?
4. What should you do if dilute acid splashes on your skin?

**Answers:**

1. The amount of rust formed.
2. $0.5 \times 1000 = 500$ cm³.
3. It is hotter and does not leave soot.
4. Wash it at once with plenty of cold running water.
