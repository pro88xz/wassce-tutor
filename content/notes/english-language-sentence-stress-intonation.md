Read this sentence aloud five times, each time stressing the word in capitals: *"**MY** sister bought the red dress."* / *"My **SISTER** bought the red dress."* / *"My sister **BOUGHT** the red dress."* / *"My sister bought the **RED** dress."* / *"My sister bought the red **DRESS**."* The words never change, but each version answers a different question. That is **emphatic stress**, and it is one of the most reliable question types in WASSCE Oral English. This lesson explains sentence stress, emphatic stress and intonation, and shows you exactly how to answer the questions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- tell content words, which are normally stressed, from function words, which are normally weak
- use emphatic stress to work out which question a sentence answers
- recognise falling and rising intonation and when each is used
- read the meaning of question tags from their intonation

## Normal sentence stress

:::callout{type=definition}
In a normal sentence, the **content words** carry stress and the **function words** are weak.

| Content words (usually stressed) | Function words (usually unstressed) |
|---|---|
| nouns: *sister, dress, market* | articles: *a, an, the* |
| main verbs: *bought, run, decide* | auxiliary verbs: *is, was, have, can* |
| adjectives: *red, tall, happy* | prepositions: *in, at, of, to* |
| adverbs: *quickly, never, here* | pronouns: *I, he, it, them* |
| question words: *who, what, where* | conjunctions: *and, but, or* |
:::

> *She **WENT** to the **MAR**ket to **BUY** some **FISH**.*

English speakers squeeze the weak words between the stressed ones, which gives English its **rhythm**. West African English often gives every syllable nearly equal weight, which is why stress-timed English can sound fast and "swallowed" to Sierra Leonean ears.

## Emphatic (contrastive) stress

:::callout{type=definition}
**Emphatic stress** moves the strongest stress onto **one** word to make it stand out, usually to **contrast** it with something else or to **correct** someone. The stressed word is the **new or important information**: the part that answers the question.
:::

### The method

Find the stressed word, then ask: *what question would make **that** word the answer?* The correct question is usually one that gets **that** detail **wrong**, so the stressed word corrects it.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Sentence:** *My sister bought the **RED** dress.*

**Which question does it answer?**

- A. Did your brother buy the red dress?
- B. Did your sister sell the red dress?
- C. Did your sister buy the blue dress?
- D. Did your sister buy the red shoes?

**Answer: C.** The stress on **RED** corrects the colour: *not the blue one, the red one*.

Now match every version:

| Stressed word | Corrects | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| **MY** sister… | whose sister | *Did **Ama's** sister buy the red dress?* |
| My **SISTER**… | who bought it | *Did your **brother** buy the red dress?* |
| …**BOUGHT**… | what she did | *Did your sister **sell** the red dress?* |
| …the **RED** dress | which colour | *Did your sister buy the **blue** dress?* |
| …the red **DRESS** | which item | *Did your sister buy the red **shoes**?* |
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Choosing the question that mentions the stressed word.** The right question usually contains a **different** word in that position, the one the stress corrects. If *SISTER* is stressed, the question asks about *brother* (or someone else), not about *sister*.
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
WAEC also asks it the other way round: *"Which word should be stressed to answer the question 'Did Musa go to Bo on Monday?' with 'No, Musa went to Kenema on Monday'?"* Find the detail that **changes** between question and answer (*Bo* → *Kenema*), and stress it: *Musa went to **KENEMA** on Monday.*
:::

## Intonation: the music of the sentence

:::callout{type=definition}
**Intonation** is the rise and fall of the voice across a sentence. English uses two main tunes:

- **Falling** ↘: the voice goes down at the end. It sounds **complete and certain**.
- **Rising** ↗: the voice goes up at the end. It sounds **incomplete, uncertain or questioning**.
:::

| Falling tune ↘ | Rising tune ↗ |
|---|---|
| **statements**: *I live in Makeni.* ↘ | **yes/no questions**: *Do you live in Makeni?* ↗ |
| **commands**: *Close the door.* ↘ | **unfinished statements**: *When I reached the junction…* ↗ |
| **wh- questions**: *Where do you live?* ↘ | **polite requests**: *Could you help me?* ↗ |
| **exclamations**: *What a beautiful day!* ↘ | **items in a list** (all but the last): *rice ↗, oil ↗, onions ↗ and pepper ↘* |
| the **last item** in a list | **echo questions** (surprise): *You failed?* ↗ |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Wh- questions do not rise.** *"Where are you going?"* has a **falling** tune in standard English, because the question word already makes it a question. Students often choose "rising" because it is a question; only **yes/no** questions normally rise.
:::

## Question tags

A question tag is the short question at the end of a statement: *"You are coming, **aren't you**?"* Its intonation changes its meaning:

| Tag tune | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Falling** ↘ | the speaker is **sure** and expects **agreement** | *It's hot today, isn't it?* ↘ (I'm sure; agree with me.) |
| **Rising** ↗ | the speaker is **not sure** and is really **asking** | *You've paid the fees, haven't you?* ↗ (Have you? I'm not certain.) |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leonean English often uses a single tag for everything: *"You are coming, not so?"* or *"…isn't it?"* after any statement. In standard English the tag must **match** the auxiliary and subject: *"You are coming, **aren't you**?"*, *"She can swim, **can't she**?"*, *"They went home, **didn't they**?"*. Matching tags are tested in the grammar section, and their intonation in Oral English.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. 'The PRINCIPAL called the meeting.' Which question does it answer? A. Did the principal cancel the meeting? B. Did the bursar call the meeting? C. Did the principal call the party? D. Did the principal call the meeting yesterday?"}
**B.** The stress on **PRINCIPAL** corrects **who** called the meeting: not the bursar, the principal.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Which word should be stressed: 'Did Fatu walk to school?' — 'No, she RODE to school.'"}
**RODE**: it corrects the action (*walk* → *rode*).
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Rising or falling? (a) What time is it? (b) Is it raining? (c) Please sit down."}
(a) **Falling**: a wh- question. (b) **Rising**: a yes/no question. (c) **Falling**: a command.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. 'You've finished your homework, haven't you?' said with a falling tag. What does the speaker mean?"}
The speaker is **sure** the homework is finished and expects the listener to **agree**.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Mark the tunes: 'I bought rice, oil, onions and pepper.'"}
*rice ↗, oil ↗, onions ↗ and pepper ↘*: rising on each item, falling on the last to show the list is complete.
:::

## Key points

- **Content words** are stressed; **function words** are weak.
- **Emphatic stress** marks new or contrasting information. To find the question, ask what the stressed word **corrects**.
- **Falling** tune: statements, commands, **wh- questions**, exclamations, the last item in a list.
- **Rising** tune: **yes/no** questions, unfinished statements, polite requests, list items, echo questions.
- **Question tags**: falling = sure, expecting agreement; rising = a real question.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**Emphatic stress** questions appear every year: you are given a sentence with one word in capitals and asked which question it answers, or which word to stress to answer a given question. **Intonation** questions ask whether a sentence has a rising or falling tune. The wh-question rule and the "what does it correct?" method answer most of them.
:::
