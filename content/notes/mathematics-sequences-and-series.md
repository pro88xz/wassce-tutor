# Sequences and series: patterns, nth terms and sums

## Learning objectives
You should be able to distinguish a sequence from a series; identify arithmetic and geometric patterns; find the nth term; calculate sums; and solve simple applications.

## Sequences and series
A sequence is an ordered list of terms that follows a rule. A series is the sum of terms of a sequence. For example, 3, 7, 11, 15 is a sequence, while (3+7+11+15) is the corresponding finite series. Identifying the pattern is essential before selecting a formula.

## Arithmetic sequences
An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference between consecutive terms. This difference is called the common difference, (d). If the first term is (a), the nth term is:

(T_n=a+(n-1)d).

For 5, 8, 11, 14, ... the first term is 5 and (d=3). The 10th term is (5+(10-1)3=32).

The sum of the first (n) terms is:

(S_n=\frac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d])

or, when the last term (l) is known,

(S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)).

For the first ten terms of 5, 8, 11, ... the sum is (10/2(5+32)=185).

## Geometric sequences
A geometric sequence has a constant ratio between consecutive non-zero terms. The ratio is (r). The nth term is:

(T_n=ar^{n-1}).

For 2, 6, 18, 54, ... the common ratio is 3. The fifth term is (2(3^4)=162).

For (r\ne1), the sum of the first (n) terms is:

(S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}).

An equivalent form is (a(r^n-1)/(r-1)). Both are correct when applied consistently. If (r=1), every term equals (a), so (S_n=na).

## Recognising the pattern
Calculate consecutive differences first. If they are constant, the sequence is arithmetic. If the ratios are constant, it is geometric. A sequence can be neither. For example, square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16 have differences 3, 5, 7, so they are not arithmetic; their ratios are not constant either.

## Applications and common errors
Arithmetic sequences can model a fixed weekly increase or regularly spaced seating. Geometric sequences can model repeated percentage growth or repeated scaling. These models only apply when the stated rate or difference remains constant.

Use (n-1) in the nth-term formula because the first term has zero steps from itself. In geometric questions, keep the common ratio and powers accurate. A series asks for a sum; a term question asks for one position in the sequence.
