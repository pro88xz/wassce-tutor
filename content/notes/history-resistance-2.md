**Resistance to British colonial rule in West Africa** came from coastal traders who defended their **commercial independence**, such as **Jaja of Opobo** (deported **1887**) and **Nana Olomu** of the Itsekiri (defeated **1894**); from kingdoms that fought conquest, such as **Benin (1897)**, **Asante** in the **Yaa Asantewaa War (1900)** and the **Sokoto Caliphate (1903)**; and from risings against colonial taxes, such as **Bai Bureh's Hut Tax War (1898)** in Sierra Leone. Most resistance failed because of **superior British weapons** and **African disunity**, but it showed Africans' determination to keep their freedom and later inspired **nationalists**. This lesson explains these examples of **African resistance to British rule**.

## Jaja of Opobo

**Jaja (about 1821–1891)** was born in **Igboland**, sold as a boy into slavery in **Bonny**, and rose through his ability in the **palm-oil trade** to become head of the **Anna Pepple House**, one of Bonny's great trading houses. After a civil war with a rival house, he left Bonny in **1869** and founded the new state of **Opobo**, which controlled the **palm-oil markets** of the interior.

**Causes of conflict with the British:**

1. Jaja held a **monopoly** of the palm-oil trade with the hinterland and would not let British traders deal directly with producers.
2. He **charged duties** on British traders and sold oil directly to Britain, cutting out Liverpool merchants on the coast.
3. After the **Berlin Conference**, Britain declared the **Oil Rivers Protectorate (1885)** and demanded **free trade**.
4. Jaja insisted that his treaty with Britain (1884) had **excluded free trade**.

**Course and result:** In **1887** the British consul **Harry Johnston** invited Jaja aboard a ship for talks, **promised him safe conduct**, then **arrested** him and sent him for trial in **Accra**. He was **deported to the West Indies**. In **1891** he was allowed to return but **died on the way home**. Opobo lost its control of the trade.

## Nana Olomu and the fall of Benin

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figure"}
**Nana Olomu** was the **Itsekiri** "governor of the Benin River" (appointed **1884**) and a rich trader who controlled the palm-oil trade of the area.
:::

Like Jaja, he blocked British traders from reaching producers. In **1894** British forces attacked his fortified town of **Ebrohimi**, which fell after fierce fighting. Nana escaped but **surrendered**, and was **deported**, first to Calabar and then to the Gold Coast; he was allowed home in 1906 and died in **1916**.

In **1897** a British party led by **James Phillips** tried to visit **Benin City** during a sacred festival against the Oba's wishes, and most of its members were killed. Britain sent a **punitive expedition**, captured Benin City in **February 1897**, burned much of it, **looted the Benin Bronzes**, and exiled **Oba Ovonramwen** to **Calabar**.

## Asante and the Yaa Asantewaa War (1900)

Asante had fought several wars with the British in the nineteenth century. In **1896** the British occupied **Kumasi** without a fight and **exiled the Asantehene Prempeh I** (first to Elmina and **Sierra Leone**, later to the **Seychelles**).

**Causes of the 1900 war:**

1. The **exile of Prempeh I** and other chiefs.
2. British demands for **payment of a war indemnity** and taxes.
3. **Forced labour** for building and carrying.
4. The British Governor **Frederic Hodgson** demanded, in **March 1900**, that the **Golden Stool**, the sacred symbol of the Asante nation, be handed over so that he could sit on it.

**Course:** **Yaa Asantewaa**, the **queen mother of Ejisu**, called the Asante to fight. Asante forces **besieged the British fort at Kumasi** for months. British reinforcements relieved the fort in **July 1900** and defeated the Asante by early **1901**.

**Results:** Yaa Asantewaa and other leaders were **exiled to the Seychelles**, where she died in **1921**; Asante was **annexed** to the Gold Coast (from **1902**); but the **Golden Stool was never captured**, and the British never again demanded it. Prempeh I returned in **1924**.

## Bai Bureh and the Hut Tax War (1898)

After the **Sierra Leone Protectorate** was declared in **1896**, Governor **Frederic Cardew** introduced a **hut tax** from **January 1898**: **5 shillings** a year on smaller houses and **10 shillings** on larger houses, collected by chiefs.

**Causes of the war:**

1. Chiefs and people saw the tax as a sign of **lost independence** and of being treated as owners of no land.
2. **Brutal collection** by the **Frontier Police**, who flogged and humiliated chiefs who could not pay.
3. Anger at **restrictions on chiefs' powers**, the **Protectorate Ordinance** and the loss of control over land and justice.
4. **Hostility** to Krio traders and missionaries, seen as part of the colonial system.

**Bai Bureh**, chief of **Kasseh** near **Port Loko**, led the northern **Temne** rising from **February 1898**. Using **guerrilla tactics**, stockades and ambushes along narrow forest paths, his fighters held off British forces for most of the year. In **April 1898** a separate, more violent **Mende rising** broke out in the south. Bai Bureh was **captured in November 1898** and **exiled to the Gold Coast**; he returned in **1905** and died in **1908**. The full story is studied in the topic on the Protectorate and the Hut Tax War.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Bai Bureh is one of **Sierra Leone's national heroes**. Although his resistance failed, the **Chalmers Commission** of inquiry criticised the way the tax was imposed and collected. His image has appeared on Sierra Leone's **leone banknotes**, and he is remembered as a symbol of courage against colonial injustice.
:::

## The fall of the Sokoto Caliphate (1903)

**Lugard's** forces captured **Kano** and **Sokoto** in **1903**. **Sultan Attahiru I** fled east with many followers and was killed at **Burmi** in **July 1903**. The British appointed a new Sultan and kept the emirate system for **indirect rule**.

## Why resistance to the British failed

1. **Superior weapons**: Maxim guns, artillery and rifles.
2. **Disunity**: neighbouring states did not unite; some helped the British.
3. **African troops** in British service, such as the **West African Frontier Force** and the **West India Regiment**.
4. **Trickery and diplomacy**, as in the arrest of Jaja.
5. **British economic power**: blockades and control of trade.

## Importance of the resistance

1. It showed Africans' **love of freedom** and rejection of foreign rule.
2. It made the British **change some policies**, for example over the Golden Stool and the way taxes were collected.
3. Heroes such as **Bai Bureh**, **Yaa Asantewaa** and **Jaja** later **inspired nationalist movements**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Jaja and Nana were not mainly resisting for religion or land: they resisted to protect their **commercial monopoly** as **middlemen** in the palm-oil trade. Explain the **economic** nature of their resistance.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Jaja of Opobo**: the trader-king who founded Opobo in 1869 and defended his palm-oil monopoly until he was arrested and deported by the British in 1887.
- **Oba Ovonramwen**: the king of Benin exiled by the British after their punitive expedition captured Benin City in 1897.
:::

## Key points

- **Jaja of Opobo** (founded Opobo **1869**): defended his palm-oil **monopoly**; arrested by **Consul Johnston** in **1887** and deported.
- **Nana Olomu**: Itsekiri trader; **Ebrohimi** fell in **1894**.
- **Benin 1897**: punitive expedition; **Oba Ovonramwen** exiled; bronzes looted.
- **Yaa Asantewaa War (1900)**: caused by Prempeh's exile and **Hodgson's demand for the Golden Stool**.
- **Hut Tax War (1898)**: **Bai Bureh** of Kasseh against **Cardew's hut tax**.
- **Sokoto** fell in **1903**; **Attahiru I** killed at Burmi.
