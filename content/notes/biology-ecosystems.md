:::callout{type=definition}
An **ecosystem** is a community of living things together with its non-living environment, all working as one system.
:::

A mangrove swamp, an inland valley swamp, a stretch of the Moa River and a patch of Gola rainforest are each an ecosystem. Inside every ecosystem two great processes run all the time: **energy flows through** it, from the sun to producers to consumers, and **materials cycle round** it, from the air and soil into living things and back again. This lesson describes the parts of an ecosystem, the carbon, nitrogen and water cycles, how organisms live together, and how ecosystems change over time through succession.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name the components of an ecosystem and the role of each
- describe the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle and the water cycle
- explain the role of decomposers
- describe the types of association between organisms: predation, parasitism, mutualism, commensalism, competition
- describe ecological succession

## The components of an ecosystem

| Component | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **producers** (autotrophs) | make food by photosynthesis; trap the sun's energy | grass, trees, rice, algae, phytoplankton |
| **consumers** (heterotrophs) | eat other organisms | herbivores (grasshopper), carnivores (hawk), omnivores (human) |
| **decomposers** | break down dead remains and wastes, releasing minerals | bacteria and fungi |
| **abiotic environment** | supplies light, water, air, minerals, a place to live | sunlight, soil, water, CO₂, O₂ |

:::callout{type=tip}
**Energy flows; matter cycles.** Energy enters as light and leaves as heat, so it must keep coming from the sun. Carbon, nitrogen and water go round and round, used again and again. Decomposers make the cycling possible.
:::

## The carbon cycle

![The carbon cycle: CO₂ in the air is taken in by green plants in photosynthesis; carbon passes to animals by feeding; plants, animals and decomposers return CO₂ by respiration; dead remains can become fossil fuels; combustion releases CO₂](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/carbon-cycle.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
**Carbon cycle:** the movement of carbon round an ecosystem: taken out of the air as CO₂, built into living things, and returned to the air.

**Photosynthesis:** green plants and algae use light to make food from CO₂ and water, removing CO₂ from the air.

**Respiration:** plants, animals and decomposers break down food for energy and give out CO₂, returning it to the air.

**Combustion:** burning wood, charcoal and fossil fuels releases CO₂ into the air.

**Decay:** decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead remains and waste, releasing CO₂ as they respire.

Carbon passes from plants to animals by **feeding**, and to decomposers through **death and waste**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Saying plants only photosynthesise.** Plants also **respire** all the time, day and night. In bright light, photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so plants take in more CO₂ than they give out; in darkness they only give it out.
:::

## The nitrogen cycle

Living things need nitrogen to make **proteins** and DNA. The air is 78% nitrogen, but plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly. They absorb **nitrates** from the soil.

![The nitrogen cycle: nitrogen gas is fixed by bacteria in legume root nodules and by lightning; plants absorb nitrates; animals eat plant proteins; decomposers make ammonium compounds; nitrifying bacteria make nitrites and then nitrates; denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen gas to the air](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/nitrogen-cycle.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
| Process | What happens | Organisms |
|---|---|---|
| **nitrogen fixation** | N₂ from the air → nitrogen compounds | *Rhizobium* in the root nodules of **legumes** (groundnut, beans, cowpea); free-living *Azotobacter*; also lightning |
| **decomposition (putrefaction)** | proteins in dead remains and wastes → ammonium compounds | bacteria and fungi |
| **nitrification** | ammonium → nitrites → nitrates | *Nitrosomonas* (to nitrite), *Nitrobacter* (to nitrate) |
| **absorption** | nitrates taken up by roots to make proteins | green plants |
| **denitrification** | nitrates → N₂ gas, returned to the air | denitrifying bacteria, especially in waterlogged soil |
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Why farmers rotate groundnuts with rice and maize.** Groundnuts and cowpeas are legumes. Their root nodules hold *Rhizobium* bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air. When the crop is harvested and its roots decay, the soil gains nitrogen compounds, so the next cereal crop grows better with less fertiliser. This is the nitrogen cycle working for the farmer.
:::

## The water cycle

Water evaporates from the sea, rivers and land, and **transpires** from leaves. The vapour rises, cools and **condenses** into clouds, and falls as **precipitation** (rain). Rain runs off into rivers, soaks into the soil and rocks, is taken up by plants, and drunk by animals, which return it by respiration, sweating and excretion. Forests matter here: cutting them reduces transpiration and the moisture that becomes rain, and increases run-off and flooding.

## How organisms live together

:::callout{type=definition}
| Association | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **predation** | a predator kills and eats its prey | hawk and rat; lion and antelope |
| **parasitism** | a parasite lives on or in a host and harms it | tapeworm in humans; *Plasmodium* in humans; dodder on plants |
| **mutualism (symbiosis)** | both partners benefit | *Rhizobium* in legume roots; lichens (fungus + alga); bacteria in the rumen of cattle |
| **commensalism** | one benefits, the other is unaffected | epiphytes (orchids, ferns) growing on tree branches; remora fish on sharks |
| **competition** | organisms need the same limited resource | weeds and rice competing for light, water and nutrients |
| **saprophytism** | feeding on dead organic matter | mushrooms, bread mould, many bacteria |
:::

**Competition** can be **intraspecific** (between members of the **same** species, such as rice plants planted too close together) or **interspecific** (between **different** species, such as rice and weeds).

## Ecological succession

:::callout{type=definition}
**Succession** is the gradual, orderly change in the community of an area over time.
:::

- **Primary succession** starts on bare ground with no soil: bare rock, new sand, a cooled lava flow. Pioneers such as **lichens and mosses** break down rock and add humus; then grasses, shrubs and finally trees.
- **Secondary succession** starts where a community was removed but soil remains: an abandoned farm, burnt bush.

The final, stable community is the **climax community** (in much of southern Sierra Leone, tropical rainforest).

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Bush fallow is secondary succession.** After a farmer harvests upland rice and leaves the land, weeds and grasses come first, then shrubs and fast-growing trees, and after many years the land returns towards forest. Traditional fallows of 10 years or more let the soil recover; today, with more people and more demand for land, fallows are often only 3 to 5 years, the soil does not recover, and yields fall.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. State the role of decomposers in an ecosystem."}
They break down dead remains and wastes, releasing **mineral salts** (and CO₂) back into the soil and air, so that **nutrients are recycled**.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Name two processes that add CO₂ to the air and one that removes it."}
Add: respiration, combustion, decay (any two). Remove: photosynthesis.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Explain the role of Rhizobium in the nitrogen cycle."}
*Rhizobium* lives in the root nodules of legumes and **fixes** nitrogen gas from the air into nitrogen compounds the plant can use, so the soil and plant gain nitrogen.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Why do waterlogged soils tend to lose nitrogen?"}
Waterlogged soils lack oxygen, which favours **denitrifying bacteria**; they convert nitrates to nitrogen gas, which escapes to the air.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Distinguish mutualism from parasitism, with one example of each."}
In **mutualism** both partners benefit (*Rhizobium* and legumes). In **parasitism** the parasite benefits and the host is harmed (tapeworm in humans).
:::

## Key points

- Ecosystem = **producers + consumers + decomposers + abiotic environment**.
- **Energy flows** through; **matter cycles**.
- **Carbon cycle**: photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration, combustion and decay return it.
- **Nitrogen cycle**: fixation (*Rhizobium*, lightning), decomposition, nitrification (*Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrobacter*), absorption, denitrification.
- Associations: **predation, parasitism, mutualism, commensalism, competition, saprophytism**.
- **Succession** leads to a **climax community**; bush fallow is secondary succession.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE often asks you to draw or describe the carbon or nitrogen cycle, to name the bacteria at each step of the nitrogen cycle, to explain the role of decomposers, and to define and give examples of ecological associations. Succession appears less often but is worth knowing. Diagrams of cycles must show the direction of each process with labelled arrows.
:::
