:::callout{type=definition}
**Nationalism in West Africa** was the movement of Africans to **end colonial rule** and win **self-government and independence** for their countries.
:::

It grew from early protests by the **educated elite** before the First World War, through the **National Congress of British West Africa (NCBWA)** of **1920**, to the **mass political parties** that followed the **Second World War**. Its main causes were **Western education**, the **African press**, **colonial grievances** such as racial discrimination and economic exploitation, the effects of the **two World Wars**, the **Atlantic Charter (1941)** and the **United Nations**, **Pan-Africanism**, and the example of **India's independence (1947)**. This lesson explains the **causes and growth of nationalism in West Africa**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define nationalism in the colonial context
- explain the causes of nationalism in West Africa
- describe the early phase of nationalism and the NCBWA

- describe the role of the press, youth movements and trade unions

## Meaning

:::callout{type=definition}
**Nationalism** is a feeling of loyalty and pride in one's nation and the desire for it to be **free and self-governing**. In colonial West Africa it meant the **struggle against colonial rule** for **equal rights, self-government and independence**.
:::

Historians often describe two phases:

| Phase | Period | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Early (moderate) nationalism** | about **1900–1945** | led by the **educated elite** (lawyers, doctors, clergy, journalists); asked for **reforms** and representation **within** the colonial system, not immediate independence |
| **Mass (radical) nationalism** | **after 1945** | **mass parties** with support from workers, farmers, ex-servicemen, women and youth; demanded **self-government now** and independence |

## Causes of nationalism in West Africa

*Figure 1. Why nationalism grew in West Africa. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Western education** | produced an **educated elite** who learned European ideas of **liberty, democracy and equality** and wanted them applied to Africa; many studied in Britain and the USA |
| **Racial discrimination** | educated Africans were **kept out of senior posts** in the civil service and paid less than Europeans with the same qualifications |
| **Economic grievances** | low prices for **cash crops** fixed by European firms and marketing boards; **expatriate firms** dominated trade; heavy taxes; forced labour |
| **The African press** | newspapers spread nationalist ideas and criticised colonial rule |
| **The First World War** | Africans fought for Britain and France and expected rewards; the war spread ideas of **self-determination** |
| **The Second World War** | ex-servicemen gained experience and confidence; the **Atlantic Charter (1941)** promised the right of peoples to choose their government; Britain and France were **weakened** |
| **Outside influences** | **Pan-Africanism** (Du Bois, Garvey, the Manchester Congress of 1945); **India and Pakistan's independence (1947)**; the **United Nations (1945)**; the USA and USSR opposed colonial empires |
| **Organisations** | **youth movements**, **trade unions**, ex-servicemen's unions and **political parties** organised people |
| **Colonial constitutions** | elections to Legislative Councils, even for few seats, encouraged political parties |

## Early nationalism and the NCBWA

Early nationalists included **J. E. Casely Hayford** (Gold Coast), **Herbert Macaulay** (Nigeria), and Sierra Leoneans such as **Dr H. C. Bankole-Bright** and **F. W. Dove**.

:::callout{type=definition}
The **National Congress of British West Africa (NCBWA)** was founded in **Accra in March 1920** by educated Africans from the **Gold Coast, Nigeria, Sierra Leone and The Gambia**, led by **J. E. Casely Hayford**. It was the first organisation to unite nationalists across all four British West African colonies.
:::

**Demands of the NCBWA:**

1. **Elected African members** in the Legislative Councils, with half the members elected.
2. **Separation of the judiciary** from the executive.
3. **Africans in senior posts** in the civil service, and an end to racial discrimination.
4. A **West African university**.
5. **Municipal self-government** for the main towns.
6. An end to the **expatriation of profits** and unfair treatment of African traders.

**Achievements:** The NCBWA's petition to London in **1920–21** was rejected, but the new constitutions of **Nigeria (1922)**, **Sierra Leone (1924)** and the **Gold Coast (1925)** introduced the **elective principle**, giving a few elected African members in the Legislative Councils. The NCBWA declined after Casely Hayford's death in **1930**.

| **Accra Evening News** | Accra (1948) | **Kwame Nkrumah** |
| **African Standard** | Freetown | **I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson** |

**Youth movements** in the 1930s, such as the **Nigerian Youth Movement (1934)** and the **West African Youth League**, founded by **I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson** of Sierra Leone, were more radical than the NCBWA and brought in workers and young people.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Isaac Theophilus Akunna Wallace-Johnson** of Sierra Leone was a journalist and trade unionist who founded the **West African Youth League** in the Gold Coast (**1935**) and brought it to Sierra Leone in **1938**. He organised workers, attacked colonial injustice in his newspaper, and was **detained during the Second World War**. He later attended the **Manchester Pan-African Congress (1945)**.
:::

## Why nationalism became a mass movement after 1945

1. **Ex-servicemen** came home with grievances and new confidence.
2. **Economic hardship**: inflation, shortages and low produce prices led to protests, such as the **Accra riots of 1948**.
3. **Mass parties** led by radical leaders appealed to **workers, farmers, market women and youth**, for example **Nkrumah's Convention People's Party (1949)** and **Azikiwe's NCNC (1944)**.
4. The **Atlantic Charter**, the **UN** and **Indian independence** made colonial rule look outdated.
5. **Britain's Labour government** after 1945 accepted gradual self-government.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Early nationalists of the NCBWA did **not** demand immediate independence. They asked for **reforms and representation** within the British Empire. The demand for **"self-government now"** came after the Second World War, especially from Nkrumah's CPP.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **J. E. Casely Hayford**: the Gold Coast lawyer who led the National Congress of British West Africa, founded in Accra in 1920.
- **Elective principle**: the right of Africans to elect some members of a colony's Legislative Council, won in Nigeria (1922), Sierra Leone (1924) and the Gold Coast (1925).
- **I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson**: the Sierra Leonean nationalist and trade unionist who led the West African Youth League.
:::

## Key points

- **Nationalism** = the struggle against colonial rule for **self-government and independence**.
- Causes: **Western education, discrimination, economic grievances, the press, the World Wars, outside influences, organisations**.
- **NCBWA (Accra, 1920)**, led by **Casely Hayford**, united the four British colonies; led to the **elective principle** (Nigeria 1922, Sierra Leone 1924, Gold Coast 1925).
- The **press** (West African Pilot, Evening News) and **youth movements** (Wallace-Johnson's West African Youth League) spread ideas.
- After **1945**, nationalism became a **mass movement** with radical parties.
