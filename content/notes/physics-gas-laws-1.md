Pump air into a football and it gets firmer; heat an aerosol can and it may explode; squeeze a sealed syringe and the air inside resists more and more. Gases obey three simple laws linking their pressure, volume and temperature.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state Boyle's law, Charles's law and the pressure law
- explain absolute zero and why the Kelvin scale is used in gas calculations
- solve problems using $pV = \text{constant}$, $V/T = \text{constant}$ and $p/T = \text{constant}$
- interpret the graphs of the gas laws

## The variables

A fixed mass of gas is described by its **pressure** $p$, **volume** $V$ and **absolute temperature** $T$ (in kelvin). Each gas law keeps one variable constant and links the other two.

## Boyle's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Boyle's law:** the **volume** of a fixed mass of gas is **inversely proportional** to its **pressure**, provided the **temperature remains constant**.

$$pV = \text{constant} \qquad p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A bicycle pump contains 200 cm³ of air at atmospheric pressure (100 kPa). The outlet is blocked and the air is compressed slowly (constant temperature) to 80 cm³. Find the new pressure.**

$$p_2 = \dfrac{p_1V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{100 \times 200}{80} = 250\ \text{kPa}$$
:::

**Kinetic explanation:** halving the volume crowds the same number of molecules into half the space, so they strike each unit area of the walls twice as often; the pressure doubles.

## Charles's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Charles's law:** the **volume** of a fixed mass of gas is **directly proportional** to its **absolute temperature**, provided the **pressure remains constant**.

$$\dfrac{V}{T} = \text{constant} \qquad \dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A balloon holds 3.0 litres of air at 27 °C. What is its volume at 77 °C, at the same pressure?**

Convert to kelvin: $T_1 = 300$ K, $T_2 = 350$ K.

$$V_2 = V_1\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 3.0 \times \dfrac{350}{300} = 3.5\ \text{litres}$$
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Always use kelvin** in gas law calculations. Using Celsius gives nonsense: doubling from 20 °C to 40 °C does **not** double the volume, because the absolute temperature only rises from 293 K to 313 K.
:::

## The pressure law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Pressure law (Gay-Lussac's law):** the **pressure** of a fixed mass of gas is **directly proportional** to its **absolute temperature**, provided the **volume remains constant**.

$$\dfrac{p}{T} = \text{constant} \qquad \dfrac{p_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2}{T_2}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A car tyre has a pressure of 200 kPa at 27 °C in the morning. After a long drive in the sun its temperature is 57 °C. Find the new pressure (volume constant).**

$$p_2 = 200 \times \dfrac{330}{300} = 220\ \text{kPa}$$
:::

## Absolute zero

![Three graphs for a fixed mass of gas: pressure against volume is a curve; pressure against one over volume is a straight line through the origin; volume against temperature in Celsius is a straight line that meets the temperature axis at minus 273 degrees Celsius](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/gas-law-graphs.svg)

*Figure 1. Graphs of the gas laws. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

If the volume–temperature (or pressure–temperature) graph of any gas is **extended backwards**, it meets the temperature axis at about **−273 °C**. At this temperature the volume (or pressure) would be zero.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Absolute zero** (0 K, −273 °C) is the lowest possible temperature, at which the molecules of a substance have their **minimum** kinetic energy. The **Kelvin scale** starts at absolute zero: $T = \theta + 273$.
:::

## Graphs to recognise

| Law | Graph | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Boyle | $p$ against $V$ | curve (hyperbola) |
| Boyle | $p$ against $\dfrac{1}{V}$ | straight line through the origin |
| Boyle | $pV$ against $p$ | horizontal line |
| Charles | $V$ against $T$ (K) | straight line through the origin |
| Charles | $V$ against θ (°C) | straight line meeting the θ-axis at −273 °C |
| Pressure law | $p$ against $T$ (K) | straight line through the origin |

## Key points

- Boyle (constant $T$): $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$.
- Charles (constant $p$): $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$.
- Pressure law (constant $V$): $p_1/T_1 = p_2/T_2$.
- Use **kelvin**: $T = \theta + 273$. Absolute zero is −273 °C.

## Quick check

1. Which quantity is kept constant in Boyle's law?
2. A gas at 100 kPa occupies 6 litres. What volume does it occupy at 300 kPa (same temperature)?
3. Convert −73 °C to kelvin.

**Answers:** 1. Temperature (and the mass of gas). 2. 2 litres. 3. 200 K.
