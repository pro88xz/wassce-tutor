# Physical quantities and units: measurement and SI conventions

## Learning objectives
You should be able to distinguish base and derived quantities; state common SI units; use prefixes and scientific notation; distinguish scalars from vectors; and report measurements with appropriate units.

## Physical quantities
A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as a numerical value and a unit. The International System of Units (SI) provides a consistent framework for measurement. The seven SI base quantities are length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), thermodynamic temperature (kelvin, K), amount of substance (mole, mol) and luminous intensity (candela, cd).

Derived quantities are formed from base quantities. Area is measured in square metres (m²), volume in cubic metres (m³), velocity in metres per second (m/s), acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²), force in newtons (N) and energy in joules (J). Since (1\,N=1\,kg\,m\,s^{-2}), the newton is a derived unit.

## Scalars and vectors
A scalar has magnitude only; examples include mass, time, distance, speed, energy and temperature. A vector has both magnitude and direction; examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration and force. Direction matters when vectors are added or when signs are used in equations. A speed of 10 m/s does not specify direction, whereas a velocity of 10 m/s east does.

## Prefixes and scientific notation
SI prefixes represent powers of ten. Common examples are kilo (k), (10^3); centi (c), (10^{-2}); milli (m), (10^{-3}); micro (µ), (10^{-6}); and mega (M), (10^6). Thus 2.5 km = 2500 m, while 35 cm = 0.35 m. Take care: upper- and lower-case symbols can represent different quantities.

Scientific notation writes a number as (A\times10^n), where (1\leq |A|<10) and (n) is an integer. For example, 0.00042 m is (4.2\times10^{-4}\,m). It is useful for very large or very small measurements and reduces errors when multiplying or dividing.

## Measurement quality
A measuring instrument has a resolution, the smallest change it can display or reliably distinguish. Accuracy describes closeness to the accepted value; precision concerns the consistency or repeatability of readings. A reading should include its unit and a sensible number of significant figures. Do not report more precision than the instrument supports.

Errors may be systematic, shifting readings in a consistent direction, or random, causing readings to vary. Repeating measurements and taking a mean can reduce the effect of random variation, but it does not automatically remove a systematic error. Zero error should be checked where relevant.

## WASSCE examination focus
Memorise base quantities and units, practise converting prefixes, and know common derived units. Show unit conversions before substitution. Distinguish accuracy from precision and scalar from vector; these terms are not interchangeable.

## Check your understanding
1. State the SI unit of mass and time.
2. Is force a base or derived quantity?
3. Convert 4.2 km to metres.
4. Classify displacement and speed as scalar or vector.
5. Express 0.0000063 s in scientific notation.

## Answers for self-assessment
1. Kilogram (kg) and second (s).
2. Derived quantity.
3. 4200 m.
4. Displacement is a vector; speed is a scalar.
5. (6.3\times10^{-6}\,s).
