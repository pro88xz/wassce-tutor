:::callout{type=definition}
A **computer** is an electronic device that accepts data as input, processes it according to a set of stored instructions (a program), stores the data and results, and produces information as output.
:::

The smartphone in your pocket can do far more than the first electronic computers, which filled a large hall, weighed about 27 tonnes and used about 150 kilowatts of electricity. This lesson traces the **history of computers**: from counting aids such as the abacus, through the mechanical calculators of Pascal, Leibniz and Babbage, to the **five generations of computers** built on vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits, microprocessors and artificial intelligence.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name early counting and calculating devices, who invented them and what they did
- explain why Charles Babbage is called the father of the computer, and the work of Ada Lovelace
- state the main technology, features and examples of each generation of computers
- describe how computers changed from one generation to the next

## Early counting and calculating devices

Long before electricity, people built tools to count and calculate faster and with fewer mistakes.

| Device | Inventor | Date | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abacus** | used in ancient Mesopotamia and China | thousands of years ago | a frame of beads on rods for adding and subtracting |
| **Napier's bones** | John Napier (Scotland) | 1617 | numbered rods that turned multiplication into addition |
| **Slide rule** | William Oughtred (England) | about 1622 | sliding scales for multiplying and dividing |
| **Pascaline** | Blaise Pascal (France) | 1642 | the first mechanical adding machine, worked by gears |
| **Stepped Reckoner** | Gottfried Leibniz (Germany) | 1673 | a mechanical calculator that could also multiply and divide |
| **Jacquard loom** | Joseph-Marie Jacquard (France) | 1804 | a weaving loom controlled by **punched cards**, the first machine "programmed" by cards |
| **Difference Engine** | Charles Babbage (England) | 1822 | a mechanical machine designed to calculate and print mathematical tables |
| **Analytical Engine** | Charles Babbage | 1837 | a design for a general-purpose mechanical computer |
| **Tabulating machine** | Herman Hollerith (USA) | 1890 | counted the US census from punched cards using electricity |

:::callout{type=definition}
**Abacus:** the earliest known calculating aid: a frame of beads that slide on rods, used for adding and subtracting.

**Punched card:** a stiff card with holes punched in set places to stand for data or instructions; punched cards controlled Jacquard's loom, fed Babbage's design and Hollerith's tabulator, and were used with computers until the 1970s.

**Analytical Engine:** Charles Babbage's 1837 design for a general-purpose mechanical computer, with a mill to calculate, a store to hold numbers, and input and output on punched cards.
:::

Hollerith's company later became part of **IBM** (International Business Machines), which grew into one of the largest computer makers in the world.

## Charles Babbage, the father of the computer

**Charles Babbage** (1791–1871), an English mathematician, is called the **father of the computer** because his **Analytical Engine** had the same main parts as a modern computer:

- an **input** of instructions and numbers on punched cards
- a **mill** that did the calculations, like today's central processing unit (CPU)
- a **store** that held numbers, like today's memory
- an **output** that printed the results

**Ada Lovelace** (1815–1852) studied the Analytical Engine and in 1843 published notes that included a step-by-step method for the engine to calculate a series of numbers. This is seen as the first computer program, so she is called the **first computer programmer**. The programming language Ada is named after her.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Calling Babbage's engines electronic.** They were **mechanical**, made of gears and levers, and neither was finished in his lifetime. The first **electronic** general-purpose computer was ENIAC, completed in the United States in 1946.
:::

## Turing and von Neumann

Two scientists shaped how every computer since works:

- **Alan Turing** (England) described in 1936 a "universal machine" that could carry out any calculation that can be written as steps. During the Second World War he helped break German secret codes. He is often called the father of computer science and of artificial intelligence.
- **John von Neumann** (Hungary, later USA) set out in 1945 the **stored-program concept**: the program and the data are both kept in the computer's memory. Almost every computer today follows this design, called the **von Neumann architecture**.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Stored-program concept:** the idea, set out by John von Neumann in 1945, that a computer keeps its program in memory alongside its data, so it can be given a new task by loading a new program instead of being rewired.
:::

## The five generations of computers

:::callout{type=definition}
**Generation of computers:** a stage in the development of computers marked by a major change in the technology used to build them, such as the change from vacuum tubes to transistors.
:::

Third generation, 1964 to 1971: integrated circuits, for example the IBM System/360. Fourth generation, 1971 to today: microprocessors, for example the IBM PC, laptops and smartphones. Fifth generation: artificial intelligence](https://wasscetutor.com/img/ict/computer-generations.svg)

*Figure 1. The five generations of computers and the technology that marks each. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Generation | Period (about) | Main technology | Programming | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 1940s–1956 | vacuum tubes | machine language (0s and 1s) | ENIAC, EDSAC, UNIVAC I |
| Second | 1956–1963 | transistors | assembly language, FORTRAN, COBOL | IBM 1401, IBM 7090 |
| Third | 1964–1971 | integrated circuits (ICs) | high-level languages such as BASIC; operating systems | IBM System/360, PDP-8 |
| Fourth | 1971–today | microprocessors (VLSI chips) | high-level languages, graphical user interfaces | IBM PC, Apple Macintosh, laptops, smartphones |
| Fifth | today and the future | artificial intelligence, parallel processing | natural language (speech) | voice assistants, chatbots, robots |

Textbooks give slightly different years for each generation; the technology is what matters.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Vacuum tube:** a glass tube, also called a valve, that switches and controls electric current; the main part of first-generation computers.

**Transistor:** a small semiconductor switch that replaced the vacuum tube in second-generation computers.

**Integrated circuit (IC):** a single silicon chip that carries many transistors and other components; the basis of third-generation computers.

**Microprocessor:** a single chip that contains the whole central processing unit; the basis of fourth-generation computers.

**Very large scale integration (VLSI):** the making of chips that each carry hundreds of thousands to billions of transistors, which made the microprocessor possible.

**Artificial intelligence (AI):** the ability of a computer to do tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as understanding speech, recognising images and learning from experience.
:::

### First generation (1940s–1956): vacuum tubes

- built with thousands of **vacuum tubes**; memory on magnetic drums
- very large (filled whole rooms), heavy and expensive
- produced a lot of heat, used much electricity and broke down often when tubes burnt out
- programmed in **machine language**; input on punched cards and paper tape
- examples: **ENIAC** (1946), EDSAC (1949) and **UNIVAC I** (1951), the first computer sold for business use in the United States

### Second generation (1956–1963): transistors

- the **transistor** was invented in 1947 at Bell Laboratories by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley
- smaller, faster, cheaper, more reliable and cooler than vacuum-tube computers
- magnetic core memory; magnetic tape and disks for storage
- programmed in **assembly language** and the first high-level languages, **FORTRAN** (1957) and **COBOL** (1959)
- examples: IBM 1401, IBM 7090

### Third generation (1964–1971): integrated circuits

- the **integrated circuit** was invented by Jack Kilby (1958) and Robert Noyce (1959)
- smaller, faster and more reliable again; keyboards and monitors replaced punched cards
- **operating systems** let a computer run several programs at the same time
- high-level languages such as **BASIC** (1964)
- examples: IBM System/360 (1964), the PDP-8 minicomputer

### Fourth generation (1971–today): microprocessors

- the **microprocessor** puts the whole CPU on one chip; the first was the **Intel 4004** (1971)
- very large scale integration (**VLSI**): millions, now billions, of transistors on a chip
- the **personal computer**: Apple II (1977), **IBM PC** (1981), Apple Macintosh (1984) with a mouse and a graphical user interface; then laptops, tablets and smartphones
- networks and the **Internet** connect computers around the world

### Fifth generation (today and the future): artificial intelligence

- computers that **learn**, **reason** and understand **natural language** and images
- **parallel processing**: many processors working on one task at the same time
- examples: voice assistants, chatbots, translation apps, face recognition, robots and self-driving car research

## How computers changed from one generation to the next

Each new generation made computers:

- **smaller** and lighter
- **faster** and more **powerful**
- **cheaper** to buy and to run
- more **reliable**, with less heat and lower power use
- able to **store** much more
- **easier to use**, from switches and punched cards to touch screens and speech

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Fourth-generation computers all around you.** When someone pays with mobile money, buys electricity units for a prepaid meter or checks a WASSCE result online, microprocessors are at work: in the phone, in the shop's machine and in the servers behind them.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Why is Charles Babbage called the father of the computer?"}
Because his **Analytical Engine** (1837) was designed with the main parts of a modern computer: **input** on punched cards, a **mill** for calculating (like the CPU), a **store** for numbers (like memory) and an **output** printer.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. State the main technology used in each of the first four generations of computers."}
First: **vacuum tubes**. Second: **transistors**. Third: **integrated circuits**. Fourth: **microprocessors** (VLSI chips).
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give three disadvantages of first-generation computers."}
Any three: very large and heavy; very expensive; produced much heat and needed air conditioning; used a lot of electricity; broke down often; could only be programmed in machine language.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Who set out the stored-program concept, and what does it mean?"}
**John von Neumann**, in 1945. The **program and the data are both kept in the computer's memory**, so the computer can be given a new task by loading a new program instead of being rewired. Almost every computer today follows this design.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. State the feature that marks the fifth generation of computers."}
**Artificial intelligence**: computers that can learn, reason and understand natural language, often using **parallel processing**.
:::

## Key points

- A **computer** accepts data, processes it under stored instructions, stores it and outputs information.
- Early devices: **abacus**, **Napier's bones** (1617), **slide rule**, **Pascaline** (1642), **Stepped Reckoner** (1673), **Jacquard loom** (1804, punched cards), **Babbage's engines**, **Hollerith's tabulator** (1890).
- **Charles Babbage** is the father of the computer; **Ada Lovelace** is the first programmer.
- Generations: **vacuum tubes** (1st), **transistors** (2nd), **integrated circuits** (3rd), **microprocessors** (4th), **artificial intelligence** (5th).
- Each generation: smaller, faster, cheaper, more reliable, less heat and power.
