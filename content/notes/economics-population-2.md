Is a large population a blessing or a burden? Thomas Malthus warned in 1798 that people would always outgrow their food. Other economists answer that it depends on how many people a country's land, capital and skills can support. This lesson covers the population theories WASSCE tests and what they mean for Sierra Leone.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state and criticise the Malthusian theory of population
- explain the optimum population theory, underpopulation and overpopulation
- describe the stages of the demographic transition
- discuss the economic effects of rapid population growth
- suggest population policies for a developing country

## The Malthusian theory

Thomas Robert Malthus, an English economist, published his theory in 1798.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Malthus's theory:** population tends to grow in a **geometric progression** (1, 2, 4, 8, 16…), doubling about every 25 years, while food supply grows only in an **arithmetic progression** (1, 2, 3, 4, 5…). Population therefore tends to outrun the food supply.
:::

![Graph of population growing geometrically to 16 while food supply grows arithmetically to 5 over four generations, opening a widening gap](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/malthus.svg)

*Figure 1. Malthus: population outruns food. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Malthus said population would be kept in line with food by **checks**:

- **positive checks**, which raise the death rate: famine, disease, war
- **preventive checks**, which lower the birth rate: late marriage and "moral restraint" (self-control)

**Criticisms of the theory:**

1. **Food production has grown much faster** than Malthus expected, thanks to new farming methods, fertilisers, irrigation and improved seeds.
2. **Birth rates fall as incomes rise**: in rich countries families have become smaller.
3. He **ignored international trade**: a country can import food it does not grow.
4. He gave no evidence that population doubles every 25 years everywhere.
5. Population is also a source of **labour and ideas** that raise output.

Even so, the theory remains a warning for poor countries where food output grows more slowly than population.

## The optimum population theory

:::callout{type=definition}
The **optimum population** is the size of population which, with the existing land, capital and technology, gives the **highest output (income) per head**.
:::

![Graph of output per head against population size: output per head rises to a peak at the optimum population and then falls; the region to the left is underpopulation and to the right overpopulation](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/optimum-population.svg)

*Figure 2. The optimum population. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **Underpopulation**: too few people to use the country's resources fully. More people would **raise** output per head (through division of labour and larger markets).
- **Overpopulation**: too many people for the resources. More people would **lower** output per head (diminishing returns to fixed land and capital).

The optimum is **not fixed**: new resources, more capital or better technology raise it.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Overpopulation does not mean "a large population". A large country with plenty of capital and technology may be at or below its optimum; a small country with few resources may be overpopulated. It depends on **output per head**.
:::

## The demographic transition

Countries tend to pass through four stages as they develop:

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. High stationary | High | High | Stable or slow growth |
| 2. Early expanding | High | Falling fast | Rapid growth |
| 3. Late expanding | Falling | Low | Growth slows |
| 4. Low stationary | Low | Low | Stable, ageing |

Sierra Leone and most of West Africa are in **stage 2 or early stage 3**: death rates have fallen with better health care, but birth rates remain high, so population grows quickly.

## Effects of rapid population growth

| Possible benefits | Possible problems |
|---|---|
| A larger labour force | Unemployment if jobs do not grow as fast |
| A bigger market for goods | Pressure on food supply and land |
| More people to develop resources | Heavy spending on schools and health; a high dependency ratio |
| A young, adaptable workforce | Low saving and investment per head |
| | Overcrowding, slums and environmental damage |
| | Faster rural–urban migration |

## Population policy

A government can try to bring population growth into line with economic growth:

1. **Family planning** services and information.
2. **Education of girls**, which delays marriage and lowers birth rates.
3. **Better health care**, which lowers infant deaths so families need not have many children.
4. **Raising incomes** and providing **old-age security**, so parents depend less on children.
5. **Faster economic growth**: more jobs, more food production and industry to support the people.
6. **Encouraging a better distribution** of population through rural development.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's young population can be an advantage, a "demographic dividend", if the many young people entering the labour force find productive work. That depends on education, skills training and enough investment in farming, industry and services to create jobs.
:::

## Key points

- **Malthus**: population grows geometrically, food arithmetically; positive and preventive checks. Criticised because food output and trade have grown, and birth rates fall with income.
- **Optimum population** gives the highest output per head; below it is **underpopulation**, above it **overpopulation**.
- The **demographic transition** has four stages; West Africa is mostly in the rapid-growth stage.
- Rapid growth brings a larger labour force and market, but also unemployment and pressure on services.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE often asks you to **state the Malthusian theory and give its weaknesses**, to **explain optimum population with a diagram**, to **distinguish underpopulation from overpopulation**, and to discuss the **economic effects of a rapidly growing population** and suitable **population policies**. Label the optimum diagram's axes "output per head" and "population".
:::

## Quick check

1. According to Malthus, how do population and food supply grow?
2. Define the optimum population.
3. Name one positive check and one preventive check.
4. Why is the optimum population not fixed?

**Answers:** 1. Population geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8…), food arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4…). 2. The population that gives the highest output per head with the existing resources and technology. 3. Positive: famine, disease or war. Preventive: late marriage or moral restraint. 4. Because more capital, new resources or better technology raise the population at which output per head is greatest.
