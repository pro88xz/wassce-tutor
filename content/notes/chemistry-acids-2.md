"How acidic?" is answered by a single number: **pH**. Farmers use it to decide whether to lime their fields, doctors use it in blood tests, and brewers and fish farmers watch it daily. This lesson explains the pH scale, how to calculate pH for strong acids and alkalis, how indicators work, and the uses of neutralisation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the pH scale and give examples of acidic, neutral and alkaline substances
- define pH and calculate the pH of strong acids and alkalis
- use the ionic product of water
- describe common indicators and their colours
- write the ionic equation for neutralisation and give its everyday applications

## The pH scale

![The pH scale from 0 to 14, coloured like universal indicator, with examples from stomach acid and lime juice through pure water to soap, ammonia and sodium hydroxide](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/ph-scale.svg)

*Figure 1. The pH scale with everyday examples. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- **pH below 7:** acidic. The lower the pH, the more acidic.
- **pH 7:** neutral (pure water at 25 °C).
- **pH above 7:** alkaline. The higher the pH, the more alkaline.

## Defining and calculating pH

:::callout{type=definition}
**pH** is the negative logarithm (base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/dm³:

$$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$$
:::

Each step of 1 on the pH scale is a **tenfold** change in [H⁺]. An acid of pH 2 has **ten times** the hydrogen ion concentration of an acid of pH 3.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**pH of strong acids** (fully ionised).

1. 0.01 mol/dm³ HCl: [H⁺] = 0.01 = 10⁻² mol/dm³, so pH = **2**.
2. 0.001 mol/dm³ HNO₃: [H⁺] = 10⁻³, so pH = **3**.
3. 0.05 mol/dm³ H₂SO₄ (dibasic: 2H⁺ per molecule): [H⁺] = 2 × 0.05 = 0.1 = 10⁻¹, so pH = **1**.
:::

### The ionic product of water

Pure water ionises very slightly: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻.

$$K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ at 25 °C}$$

So in pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ mol/dm³ and pH = 7. Also

$$\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14, \qquad \text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**pH of an alkali.** Find the pH of 0.01 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide.

- [OH⁻] = 0.01 = 10⁻² mol/dm³, so pOH = 2.
- pH = 14 − 2 = **12**.

Alternatively, [H⁺] = 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 10⁻² = 10⁻¹², so pH = 12.
:::

## Indicators

An **indicator** is a substance that changes colour depending on whether the solution is acidic or alkaline.

| Indicator | In acid | In alkali | Changes around pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Litmus** | Red | Blue | 5–8 |
| **Methyl orange** | Red | Yellow | 3.1–4.4 (orange at end point) |
| **Phenolphthalein** | Colourless | Pink | 8.3–10 |
| **Universal indicator** | Red → orange → yellow | Green (neutral) → blue → purple | Whole range 1–14 |

**Universal indicator** is a mixture of indicators that shows a range of colours, so it gives the **approximate pH**. A **pH meter** gives a more accurate reading.

**Natural indicators:** extracts of **hibiscus (sorrel) flowers**, red cabbage and some fruit skins also change colour with pH. Hibiscus extract is red in acid and turns green-yellow in alkali.

## Neutralisation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Neutralisation** is the reaction between an acid and a base to form a **salt and water only**. It is **exothermic** (gives out heat). The ionic equation for any acid and alkali is:

$$\text{H}^+\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^-\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
:::

### Applications of neutralisation

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| **Indigestion** | Antacids such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate neutralise excess stomach acid |
| **Acidic soils** | Farmers add **lime** (CaO, Ca(OH)₂) or powdered limestone (CaCO₃) to raise soil pH for crops |
| **Insect stings** | Bee and ant stings (acidic) are treated with a weak alkali such as baking soda; wasp stings (alkaline) with vinegar |
| **Tooth decay** | Bacteria make acids that dissolve enamel; alkaline toothpaste neutralises them |
| **Industrial waste** | Acid waste is neutralised with lime before discharge |
| **Acid spills** | Neutralised with sodium hydrogencarbonate |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Much of the farmland in Sierra Leone's upland areas is **acidic**, because heavy rainfall washes basic minerals out of the soil. Agricultural officers test the soil pH, and where it is low they advise farmers to apply **lime** before planting crops such as groundnuts and maize that grow poorly in acid soil.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A solution of pH 2 is not "twice as acidic" as one of pH 4. Each pH unit is a **factor of 10**, so it has **100 times** the hydrogen ion concentration.
:::

## Key points

- **pH < 7** acidic, **7** neutral, **> 7** alkaline; each unit is a **tenfold** change in [H⁺].
- $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$; for strong acids, [H⁺] = concentration × basicity.
- $K_w = 10^{-14}$; **pH + pOH = 14**.
- **Litmus** red/blue; **methyl orange** red/yellow; **phenolphthalein** colourless/pink; **universal indicator** gives the approximate pH.
- **Neutralisation:** H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, exothermic; used for **indigestion, acid soils, stings and waste**.

## Quick check

1. Find the pH of 0.001 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid.
2. Find the pH of 0.1 mol/dm³ potassium hydroxide.
3. What colour is phenolphthalein in (a) acid, (b) alkali?
4. Why do farmers add lime to some soils?

**Answers:**

1. [H⁺] = 10⁻³, so pH = 3.
2. pOH = 1, so pH = 13.
3. (a) Colourless; (b) pink.
4. To neutralise soil acidity and raise the pH so crops grow well.
