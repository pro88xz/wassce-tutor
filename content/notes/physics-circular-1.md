A body moving in a circle at steady speed is accelerating, even though its speed never changes. That surprising fact explains why you slide across the seat when a vehicle turns, how a spin dryer works and what holds the Moon in its orbit.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define angular displacement, angular velocity, period and frequency
- relate linear speed to angular velocity, $v = \omega r$
- explain why a body in uniform circular motion is accelerating
- calculate centripetal acceleration and centripetal force

## Describing circular motion

:::callout{type=definition}
**Uniform circular motion** is motion in a circle at **constant speed**.
:::

| Quantity | Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Angular displacement | θ | angle turned through | radian (rad) |
| Angular velocity | ω | rate of change of angle, $\omega = \dfrac{\theta}{t}$ | rad s⁻¹ |
| Period | $T$ | time for one complete revolution | s |
| Frequency | $f$ | revolutions per second, $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ | Hz |

One complete revolution is $2\pi$ radians (360°), so

$$\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$$

:::callout{type=definition}
One **radian** is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. $2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360°$, so $1\ \text{rad} \approx 57.3°$.
:::

## Linear speed and angular velocity

In one revolution a body travels the circumference $2\pi r$ in time $T$:

$$v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \omega r$$

Points further from the centre move faster for the same ω: the outside of a turning wheel moves faster than the hub.

## Why circular motion is accelerated

![A body moving round a circle of radius r: its velocity points along the tangent while the centripetal force and acceleration point towards the centre O](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/circular-motion.svg)

*Figure 1. Velocity is along the tangent; acceleration and force point to the centre. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Velocity is a vector. Although the **speed** is constant, the **direction** of motion changes continuously, so the **velocity** changes. A changing velocity means an **acceleration**. This acceleration is directed **towards the centre** of the circle and is called **centripetal** ("centre-seeking") acceleration:

$$a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

By Newton's second law, a resultant force must cause it. This **centripetal force** also acts towards the centre:

$$F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$$

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Centripetal force is **not a new kind of force**. It is the name for whatever **resultant** force points to the centre: tension in a string, friction between tyres and road, gravity on a satellite, or the electric force on an electron in an atom. If that force disappears, the body moves off along the **tangent** in a straight line (Newton's first law), not outwards along the radius.
:::

## Worked examples

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**1. A stone of mass 0.2 kg is whirled in a horizontal circle of radius 0.5 m at 3 revolutions per second. Find (a) the angular velocity, (b) the linear speed, (c) the tension in the string.**

(a) $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 3 = 18.85$ rad s⁻¹.

(b) $v = \omega r = 18.85 \times 0.5 = 9.42$ m s⁻¹.

(c) $T = m\omega^2 r = 0.2 \times 18.85^2 \times 0.5 = 35.5$ N.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**2. A car of mass 800 kg takes a bend of radius 50 m on a level road at 15 m s⁻¹. Find the friction force needed. If the maximum friction is 4000 N, what is the greatest safe speed?**

$$F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{800 \times 15^2}{50} = 3600\ \text{N}$$

Greatest speed: $4000 = \dfrac{800v^2}{50} \Rightarrow v^2 = 250 \Rightarrow v = 15.8$ m s⁻¹.

Above this the tyres cannot supply enough centripetal force, and the car skids outwards along a tangent.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
A spin dryer or the spinning drum of a washing machine uses this idea: the drum wall pushes the clothes towards the centre, but the water passes through the holes, which supply no force. With nothing to keep it moving in a circle, the water flies off along tangents and leaves the clothes.
:::

## Applications

- **Vehicles on bends**: friction provides the centripetal force; roads are **banked** to help.
- **Spin dryers and centrifuges**: separate water from clothes, cream from milk, blood cells from plasma.
- **Satellites and the Moon**: gravity provides the centripetal force.
- **Cyclists lean inwards** when turning.
- **"Wall of death"** riders and fairground rides.

## Key points

- Uniform circular motion: constant speed, changing velocity, so it is **accelerated**.
- $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$, $v = \omega r$.
- Centripetal acceleration $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$, towards the centre.
- Centripetal force $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, provided by tension, friction, gravity or another real force.
- If the force stops, the body moves off along the tangent.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **explain why a body moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating**, to **define angular velocity** and the **radian**, and to calculate **centripetal force or tension** for a stone on a string or a car on a bend. Objective items often ask for the **direction** of centripetal acceleration (towards the centre) or what happens when a string breaks (moves along the tangent).
:::

## Quick check

1. In which direction does the centripetal acceleration act?
2. A wheel makes 120 revolutions per minute. Find its angular velocity.
3. What provides the centripetal force for a car turning on a flat road?

**Answers:** 1. Towards the centre of the circle. 2. $f = 2$ Hz, $\omega = 2\pi \times 2 = 12.6$ rad s⁻¹. 3. Friction between the tyres and the road.
