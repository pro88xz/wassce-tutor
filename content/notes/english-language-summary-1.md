The summary carries **30 marks** in Paper 2, more than the comprehension. It is also the most predictable question in the paper: you are given a passage and asked to state a fixed number of points, one sentence each. Students rarely lose summary marks because the skill is hard. They lose them because they summarise **the wrong things**: background, examples, stories, or points of the wrong kind. This lesson teaches you to find exactly the points the question wants, and nothing else, using a complete passage.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- read a summary question and say exactly what it asks for
- tell a main point from an example, illustration, statistic or repetition
- find hidden points and split sentences that hold two points
- build a checked list of points before writing a word of your answer

## What the question fixes

A typical question reads:

> *In **six** sentences, **one for each**, summarise the **reasons** given in the passage for the **decline of farming** among young people.*

| The question fixes | Here | So you must |
|---|---|---|
| **How many** points | six | find exactly six |
| **What kind** of point | reasons | give reasons, not effects or solutions |
| **About what** | the decline of farming among young people | ignore points about anything else |
| **The form** | six sentences, one for each | write six separate sentences (next lesson) |

Sometimes the question also **limits where you look**: *"according to paragraphs 3 to 5"*, *"in the last two paragraphs"*. A good point from outside the limit earns **nothing**.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **summary point** is one **main idea** in the passage that answers the question. It is **not** an example, a story, a statistic, a name, a quotation, or the same idea said again in other words.
:::

## The keep-or-cut test

| Keep: main points | Cut: supporting material |
|---|---|
| a general statement that answers the question | *for example*, *for instance*, *such as*, *like* |
| a reason, effect, step, quality, problem or measure | stories about individuals |
| | figures, dates, percentages |
| | names of people and places used as illustrations |
| | quotations and dialogue |
| | the same idea repeated for emphasis |

:::callout{type=tip}
Ask of every sentence: **"Is this the idea, or is this showing me the idea?"** The idea stays. Whatever shows it (an example, a story, a number) goes.
:::

## A complete passage

Read the passage below. The question is printed after it.

::::model{label="Practice passage" title="Why young people are leaving the farms"}
In villages across Sierra Leone, the people bending over the rice fields are growing older every year. Ask the young where they are, and you will hear the same answer: in town.

Many young people now believe that farming is work for the poor and the uneducated. A boy who finishes secondary school in Moyamba would rather sell phone credit on a Freetown street corner than plant rice on his father's land, because the street, at least, looks like progress.

Another cause is the work itself. Most farmers still clear their land with the hoe and the cutlass their grandparents used. With such tools a family can manage only a small plot, and the labour is so hard that few young people are willing to do it for long.

Even a good harvest does not guarantee an income. The roads that link many farming villages to the markets are so poor that, in the rainy season, lorries cannot pass for weeks. Tomatoes, peppers and bananas rot by the roadside while traders in Bo and Kenema complain of shortages. Farmers who do reach the market often find that middlemen have fixed the prices so low that the journey was hardly worth making.

Money to start or grow a farm is also hard to find. Banks rarely lend to farmers, who have no salary to show and whose land may not be registered in their name. A young person who wants to buy seed, fertiliser or a power tiller has nowhere to turn.

Finally, village life offers young people little besides work. With no electricity, few schools beyond the primary level and nowhere to meet friends in the evening, the town, with all its problems, simply seems more alive.
::::

**Question:** *In six sentences, one for each, summarise the reasons the writer gives for young people leaving farming.*

## Finding the points, paragraph by paragraph

:::callout{type=worked-example}
| Paragraph | What it says | Keep or cut? | Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Farmers are getting older; the young are in town. | **Cut**: background, introduces the topic | — |
| 2 | Young people see farming as work for the poor and uneducated. The Moyamba boy selling credit. | **Keep** the attitude; **cut** the boy (example) | **1.** Young people see farming as low-status work. |
| 3 | Old tools: only small plots, and very hard labour. | **Keep**: but it holds **two** ideas | **2.** Farmers use outdated tools, so farms stay small. **3.** The work is too hard. |
| 4 | Bad roads: produce rots. Middlemen fix low prices. | **Keep** both; **cut** the list of crops and the Bo/Kenema traders | **4.** Bad roads stop produce reaching markets. **5.** Middlemen pay farmers very low prices. |
| 5 | Banks will not lend; no capital. | **Keep**; **cut** the list of items (seed, fertiliser, tiller) | **6.** Farmers cannot get loans or capital. |
| 6 | Village life is dull: no electricity, few schools, nowhere to meet. | **Keep** | **7.** Village life lacks amenities and social life. |
:::

We now have **seven** possible points for a question that asks for **six**. Look again:

- Points 2 and 3 come from one paragraph. Are they two reasons or one? The writer gives **two** separate consequences of the old tools: small plots (*so there is little money*) and hard labour (*so the young don't want to do it*). A careful candidate could merge them into one point about poor tools making farming hard and unrewarding.
- Points 4 and 5 are clearly two different problems: transport and price-fixing.

**Decision:** merge 2 and 3 (they share one cause, the tools), keeping six points:

1. Young people regard farming as work for the poor and uneducated.
2. Outdated tools make farming exhausting and limit farms to small plots.
3. Poor roads prevent produce from reaching markets before it spoils.
4. Middlemen force farmers to sell at very low prices.
5. Farmers cannot get loans to start or expand their farms.
6. Villages lack the amenities and social life that attract the young to towns.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Keeping the illustration instead of the point.** *"A boy from Moyamba would rather sell phone credit in Freetown"* is the **example**. The **point** is the attitude it shows: young people regard farming as low-status work.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Including background as a point.** *"Farmers in Sierra Leone are getting older"* is true, and it is in the passage, but it is not a **reason** for the young leaving. It is the situation the passage explains.
:::

## When you have too many or too few points

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Too many** points | two of them are the same idea, or one is an example | merge the repeats; cut the examples |
| **Too few** points | one sentence holds two points, or a point is hidden in a long sentence | split compound sentences; reread paragraphs you skimmed |

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. The question asks for 'the advantages of the new hospital'. The passage also describes its problems. Do you include them?"}
**No.** Only advantages. Problems are the wrong kind of point and earn nothing.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Keep or cut: 'In 2019, for example, the river flooded twice and destroyed forty houses.'"}
**Cut.** It is an example with a date and a statistic. If the passage's general point is that flooding destroys homes, that is the point to keep.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Find two points in: 'Plastic waste blocks the drains, and when the drains are blocked the streets flood and mosquitoes breed in the standing water.'"}
1. Plastic waste blocks drains and causes **flooding**.
2. Standing water **breeds mosquitoes**.

(If the question asks for the **effects of plastic waste**, both are effects.)
:::

:::reveal{title="4. The question says 'according to paragraph 4'. Your best point is in paragraph 5. Can you use it?"}
**No.** A point outside the stated paragraphs earns nothing, however good it is.
:::

## Key points

- The summary is worth **30 marks** and asks for a **fixed number** of points of a **fixed kind**.
- Underline the **number**, the **kind of point**, the **topic** and any **limit** on paragraphs.
- **Keep main ideas; cut** examples, stories, figures, names, quotations, repetition and background.
- **List, count and check** your points before writing.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Marks are given for each **correct, relevant point** and for **expression**. A point of the wrong kind, a point from outside the stated paragraphs, or an example in place of a point earns nothing. The next lesson shows how to turn your list into sentences that earn every mark.
:::
