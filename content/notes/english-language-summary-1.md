The summary carries **30 marks** in Paper 2, more than the comprehension. It is also the most predictable question in the paper: you are given a passage and asked to state a fixed number of points, usually one sentence each. Students lose marks not because summary is hard, but because they summarise the wrong things. This lesson shows you how to find exactly the points the question asks for.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- read a summary question and identify exactly what it asks for
- find the relevant points in a passage and ignore the rest
- tell a main point from an example, illustration or repetition
- prepare a list of points before writing

## What the summary question asks

A typical question reads:

> *In **six** sentences, **one for each**, summarise the **reasons** the writer gives for the decline of farming in the village.*

Three things are fixed by the question:

1. **How many points**: here, six.
2. **What kind of point**: here, *reasons* for the decline. Not effects, not solutions, not background.
3. **The form**: here, six separate sentences, one point each.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **summary point** is one main idea from the passage that answers the question. It is **not** an example, a story, a statistic, a quotation or a repeated idea.
:::

## Step 1: underline the instruction words

Before you read the passage, underline in the question:

- the **number**
- the **topic word**: *reasons*, *problems*, *advantages*, *effects*, *steps*, *qualities*, *measures*
- any **limit**: *according to the writer*, *in paragraphs 3 to 5*, *in the last two paragraphs*

If the question says *"paragraphs 2 to 4"*, points from paragraph 1 or 5 earn nothing, however good they are.

## Step 2: find the points

Read the relevant part of the passage and mark every sentence that answers the topic word. Then sort what you marked.

| Keep (main points) | Cut (supporting material) |
|---|---|
| a general statement that answers the question | examples: *"for instance…"*, *"such as…"* |
| a reason, effect, step or quality | stories and anecdotes |
| | figures, dates, names of people |
| | quotations and dialogue |
| | the same idea said again in other words |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Passage (extract):** *"Young people are leaving the farms. Many of them believe that farming is for the poor and the uneducated; a boy who finishes school in Moyamba would rather sell phone credit in Freetown than plant rice at home. Another cause is the lack of tools. Most farmers still use the hoe and cutlass their grandparents used, so a family can clear only a small plot each season. Even when the harvest is good, the road to the market is so bad that much of the produce rots before it can be sold."*

**Question:** In three sentences, one for each, state the reasons for the decline of farming.

**Points found:**

1. Young people regard farming as work for the poor and uneducated. *(The Moyamba boy selling phone credit is an **example**: cut it.)*
2. Farmers lack modern tools, so they can farm only small plots.
3. Poor roads stop farmers getting their produce to market before it spoils.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Including the example instead of the point.** *"A boy from Moyamba would rather sell phone credit"* is the illustration. The **point** is the attitude it illustrates: young people see farming as work for the poor.
:::

## Step 3: list, then count

On rough paper, list the points in a few words each:

- youths see farming as low-status
- no modern tools: small farms
- bad roads: produce rots

Count them against the number in the question. If you have **more**, two of your points are probably the same idea, or one is an example. If you have **fewer**, read the passage again: a point may be hidden in a long sentence, or one sentence may hold two points.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Suppose the question asks for **problems facing traders at a big market such as Dove Cut or Big Market in Freetown**, and the passage mentions crowding, theft, rain damage, high stall fees and the council's new market hours. Make sure the *new market hours* are described in the passage **as a problem**. If the writer calls them an improvement, they are not one of your points.
:::

## Key points

- The summary is worth **30 marks** and asks for a **fixed number** of points of a **fixed kind**.
- Underline the **number**, the **topic word** and any **limit** on which paragraphs to use.
- Keep **main points**; cut **examples, stories, figures, quotations and repetition**.
- **List and count** your points before you write a single sentence.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Marks are given for each **correct point** and for **expression**. A point outside the question's limits, or of the wrong kind (an effect when reasons were asked), earns nothing. The next lesson shows how to write each point so it earns the full mark.
:::

## Quick check

1. The question asks for *"the advantages of the new hospital"*. The passage also lists its problems. Do you include them?
2. Is *"For example, in 2019 the river flooded twice"* a point or support?
3. You found eight points for a six-point question. What should you check?
4. The question says *"according to paragraphs 3 and 4"*. Can you use a strong point from paragraph 5?

**Answers:** 1. No: only advantages. 2. Support (an example with a date). 3. Whether some are repeats or examples of another point. 4. No.
