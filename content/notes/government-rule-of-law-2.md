If the same person made the laws, enforced them and judged those accused of breaking them, nothing would stop that person from becoming a tyrant. To prevent this, modern constitutions divide the work of government among three separate organs, and then give each the power to check the others. This lesson explains the **separation of powers**, **checks and balances**, and how they work in Sierra Leone.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the theory of separation of powers and its origin
- state the merits and limitations of separation of powers
- explain checks and balances with examples from Sierra Leone
- distinguish separation of powers from fusion of powers

## The separation of powers

:::callout{type=definition}
**Separation of powers** is the principle that the three functions of government (**legislative, executive and judicial**) should be performed by **separate organs**, staffed by **different people**, so that no one person or body holds all power.
:::

The idea is most associated with the French writer **Baron de Montesquieu** in *The Spirit of the Laws* (**1748**). He argued that "when the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person ... there can be no liberty", and that there is no liberty if the judicial power is not separated from the other two. The English philosopher **John Locke** had earlier argued for separating legislative and executive power.

The three elements of the doctrine:

1. **Separation of functions:** each organ does its own work.
2. **Separation of personnel:** no one should be a member of more than one organ.
3. **Separation of powers (independence):** no organ should control the others.

### Merits

- **Prevents tyranny and dictatorship** by stopping concentration of power.
- **Protects individual liberty** and rights.
- **Promotes the rule of law** and an independent judiciary.
- **Efficiency through specialisation**: each organ concentrates on its own work.
- **Accountability**: each organ can be held responsible for its own actions.

### Limitations: why complete separation is impossible

- The **executive makes laws** through delegated legislation and initiates most bills.
- The **legislature performs executive and judicial acts**, such as approving appointments and impeaching the President.
- The **head of state appoints judges** and grants pardons.
- In **parliamentary systems**, ministers sit in the legislature: there is **fusion of powers**.
- Complete separation would cause **deadlock**, because government needs the organs to cooperate.
- **Military regimes** combine executive and legislative powers in a ruling council.

## Checks and balances

:::callout{type=definition}
**Checks and balances** is the system by which each organ of government has some power to **limit (check) the actions of the others**, so that power is balanced and none can abuse it.
:::

![A triangle linking the executive, legislature and judiciary, with labelled checks: Parliament approves the budget and ministers and can impeach the President; the President can refuse assent and appoints judges with Parliament's approval; the courts can declare actions unconstitutional](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/checks-balances.svg)

*Figure 1. Checks and balances among the three organs. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Organ | Checks it exercises (Sierra Leone) |
|---|---|
| **Executive on the legislature** | The President may **refuse assent** to bills, returning them with reasons; initiates most legislation |
| **Executive on the judiciary** | Appoints judges (on JLSC advice, with Parliament's approval); grants pardons |
| **Legislature on the executive** | Approves ministers and other appointments, the **budget** and loans; questions ministers; can **remove the President** for gross misconduct |
| **Legislature on the judiciary** | Approves judicial appointments; takes part in the removal of judges; can change laws the courts interpret |
| **Judiciary on the executive** | Can declare executive actions **unconstitutional or unlawful** |
| **Judiciary on the legislature** | Can declare Acts of Parliament **void** if they conflict with the Constitution |

## Separation of powers versus fusion of powers

| Separation (presidential) | Fusion (parliamentary) |
|---|---|
| Ministers are not members of the legislature | Ministers are members of parliament |
| Executive is elected separately | Executive comes from the majority party in parliament |
| Fixed terms; removal by impeachment | Government can be removed by a vote of no confidence |
| Example: USA, Sierra Leone | Example: Britain |

:::callout{type=local-example}
The 1991 Constitution of Sierra Leone puts the **Executive (Chapter V)**, the **Legislature (Chapter VI)** and the **Judiciary (Chapter VII)** in separate chapters. An MP appointed as a minister must **give up the parliamentary seat**, keeping personnel separate. Parliament vets and approves the President's nominees, and the **Supreme Court** can rule on whether actions of government are consistent with the Constitution.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**"Separation of powers is more theoretical than real." Discuss.** Give the theory, then show the overlaps:

- **Theory:** three organs, separate functions and personnel (Montesquieu).
- **Overlap 1:** the executive legislates through regulations and initiates bills.
- **Overlap 2:** the President appoints judges and pardons convicts, a quasi-judicial power.
- **Overlap 3:** Parliament approves appointments and can remove the President, which is executive and quasi-judicial work.
- **Conclusion:** the organs are separate but **interdependent**; checks and balances make the overlap useful rather than dangerous.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Separation of powers and checks and balances are **not the same thing**. Separation **divides** power among the organs; checks and balances **link** them so each can limit the others. A good answer explains both and shows how they work together.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Separation of personnel**: the rule that no one should be a member of more than one organ of government.
- **Judicial review**: the power of the courts to declare executive actions or Acts of Parliament void when they conflict with the Constitution.
- **Assent**: the President's formal approval that turns a bill into law; the President may refuse assent and return the bill with reasons.
:::

## Key points

- **Separation of powers** divides government into **legislative, executive and judicial** organs with separate **functions and personnel** (**Montesquieu, 1748**).
- It **prevents tyranny** and **protects liberty**, but **complete separation is impossible**.
- **Checks and balances** let each organ limit the others: **assent, approval of appointments and budget, impeachment, judicial review**.
- Presidential systems show **separation**; parliamentary systems show **fusion**.

1. Who wrote *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748)?
2. State two merits of separation of powers.
3. Give one way the legislature checks the executive in Sierra Leone.
4. What is fusion of powers?

**Answers:**

1. Baron de Montesquieu.
2. Any two: prevents tyranny; protects liberty and rights; promotes the rule of law and judicial independence; efficiency through specialisation; accountability.
3. Any one: approving ministers and other appointments; approving the budget and loans; questioning ministers; removing the President for gross misconduct.
