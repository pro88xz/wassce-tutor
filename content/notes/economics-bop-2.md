How many leones does a dollar cost today, and why does the answer keep changing? The exchange rate affects the price of every imported bag of rice and litre of fuel. This lesson explains how exchange rates are set, what happens when the leone falls, and the international institutions that help countries with payments problems.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the exchange rate and convert between currencies
- distinguish fixed, floating and managed exchange rate systems
- explain how a floating exchange rate is determined
- distinguish devaluation from depreciation, and explain their effects
- describe the functions of the IMF and the World Bank

## What is an exchange rate?

:::callout{type=definition}
The **exchange rate** is the **price of one currency in terms of another**: for example, the number of leones needed to buy one US dollar.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Suppose the exchange rate is NLe 23 = US\$1.**

- A trader needs \$2,000 to import a container of goods: $2000 \times 23 = $ **NLe 46,000**.
- A tourist changes \$150: $150 \times 23 = $ **NLe 3,450**.
- If the rate moves to NLe 25 = \$1, the same \$2,000 import costs $2000 \times 25 = $ NLe 50,000: the leone has **depreciated** and imports are dearer.
:::

## Exchange rate systems

| System | How the rate is set | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | The government or central bank fixes the rate and defends it by buying and selling currency | Certainty for traders and investors | Needs large reserves; can become overvalued |
| **Floating (flexible)** | Set freely by **demand and supply** in the foreign exchange market | Adjusts automatically; no need for large reserves | Uncertainty; can swing sharply |
| **Managed (dirty) float** | Mostly market-determined, but the central bank intervenes to smooth sharp movements | Combines flexibility with some stability | Requires reserves and judgement |

Sierra Leone operates a **managed floating** exchange rate: the leone's value is set mainly by the market, with the Bank of Sierra Leone intervening at times.

## How a floating rate is determined

The **demand for US dollars** (in leones) comes from **importers**, people paying for travel and education abroad, firms repaying foreign loans and investors sending money out. The **supply of dollars** comes from **exporters**, foreign investors, tourists, remittances and aid.

![Graph of the market for US dollars in leones: the demand curve for dollars and the supply curve of dollars cross at rate R1; when importers demand more dollars, demand shifts right to D2, the rate rises to R2 leones per dollar and the leone depreciates](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/exchange-rate.svg)

*Figure 1. An increase in the demand for dollars makes the leone depreciate. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**The leone tends to depreciate (more leones per dollar) when:** imports rise; export earnings fall (for example when iron ore prices drop); inflation in Sierra Leone is higher than abroad; investors move money out; or remittances and aid fall.

## Devaluation, depreciation, revaluation, appreciation

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Devaluation** | A **deliberate official reduction** in the value of a currency under a **fixed** exchange rate |
| **Depreciation** | A **fall** in the value of a currency caused by **market forces** under a floating rate |
| **Revaluation** | A deliberate official increase in the value of a fixed currency |
| **Appreciation** | A rise in the value of a currency through market forces |

### Effects of devaluation or depreciation

- **Exports become cheaper** to foreign buyers, so they may rise.
- **Imports become dearer** at home, so they may fall.
- The **balance of payments may improve**, but only if the demand for exports and imports is **elastic** enough.
- **Imported inflation**: the prices of fuel, food and machinery rise at home.
- The **burden of foreign debt** (in leones) rises.
- Local industries using imported inputs face **higher costs**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why elasticity matters.** Sierra Leone's main imports (fuel, rice, medicines) are necessities with **inelastic** demand, so a depreciation makes them dearer without cutting them much: the import bill in leones **rises**. Its main exports (minerals) are priced in dollars on world markets, so a weaker leone does not make them much cheaper to foreign buyers. This is why depreciation alone often fails to cure the deficit in primary-producing countries.
:::

## International financial institutions

### The International Monetary Fund (IMF)

Set up in 1944 (Bretton Woods conference); Sierra Leone is a member.

**Functions:** promoting exchange rate stability and international monetary cooperation; giving **short- and medium-term loans** to members with balance of payments problems; advising governments on economic policy, often with conditions (reforms); providing technical assistance and training; monitoring the world economy.

### The World Bank (IBRD and IDA)

Also set up in 1944.

**Functions:** giving **long-term loans** and grants for **development** projects (roads, power, schools, health, agriculture); offering low-interest credits to the poorest countries through the **International Development Association (IDA)**; giving technical advice; encouraging private investment in developing countries.

| | IMF | World Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim | Stability of exchange rates and the balance of payments | Long-term economic development |
| Lending | Short to medium term | Long term |
| Typical use | Financing a payments deficit | Financing projects and programmes |

Other institutions include the **African Development Bank (AfDB)** and the **ECOWAS Bank for Investment and Development (EBID)**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Devaluation** is an official decision under a fixed rate; **depreciation** happens through the market under a floating rate. Using them as if they mean the same thing loses marks in definition questions.
:::

## Key points

- The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in another.
- Systems: **fixed, floating, managed**; Sierra Leone uses a **managed float**.
- A floating rate is set by the **demand for and supply of foreign currency**.
- **Devaluation** (official) and **depreciation** (market) make exports cheaper and imports dearer, but cause **imported inflation**, and work only with **elastic** demand.
- The **IMF** helps with payments problems; the **World Bank** finances development.

## Quick check

1. At NLe 24 = \$1, how many leones does \$500 cost?
2. What is the difference between devaluation and depreciation?
3. Give two reasons why the leone may depreciate.
4. State one function of the IMF and one of the World Bank.

**Answers:** 1. $500 \times 24 = $ NLe 12,000. 2. Devaluation is an official cut in a fixed exchange rate; depreciation is a fall in value caused by market forces. 3. Any two: rising imports, falling export earnings, high inflation at home, capital outflows, falling remittances or aid. 4. IMF: loans for balance of payments problems, exchange rate stability, policy advice. World Bank: long-term development loans and grants, technical advice.
