:::callout{type=definition}
The **abolition of the slave trade** was the movement, mainly in **Britain**, Europe and the Americas, that ended the legal Atlantic slave trade in the early nineteenth century, beginning with the **British Act of 1807**.
:::

It was driven by **humanitarian and religious** campaigners such as **Granville Sharp, Thomas Clarkson, William Wilberforce** and the African writer **Olaudah Equiano**, by changing **economic** interests during the **Industrial Revolution**, and by **political** ideas of liberty and the **Haitian Revolution**. Britain then used its navy, based in **Freetown**, to enforce abolition. This lesson explains the reasons for abolition and how it was carried out.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the humanitarian and religious reasons for abolition
- explain the economic reasons for abolition
- explain the political factors that encouraged abolition
- describe the main stages of the abolition of the slave trade and of slavery
- explain how Britain enforced abolition and the role of Freetown

## Reasons for abolition

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Humanitarian and religious** | **Quakers** and **evangelical Christians** condemned slavery as cruel and sinful; campaigners such as **Granville Sharp**, **Thomas Clarkson** and **William Wilberforce** organised petitions and campaigns |
| **African voices** | formerly enslaved writers such as **Olaudah Equiano** (whose *Interesting Narrative*, 1789, described the Middle Passage) and **Ottobah Cugoano** exposed its horrors |
| **Economic** | the **Industrial Revolution** made factories, raw materials (palm oil, cotton) and **markets** more important; some argued that free labour and "legitimate" trade would be more profitable than slavery |
| **Political** | ideas of **liberty and equality** from the American and **French Revolutions**; the **Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)**, in which enslaved people won independence, showed the danger and cost of slavery |
| **Resistance** | revolts and resistance by enslaved people made slavery costly and dangerous |
| **Legal** | the **Somerset case (1772)**, in which Lord Mansfield ruled that an enslaved man could not be forcibly removed from England |

## Stages of abolition

![A vertical timeline: Somerset case 1772; Province of Freedom founded in Sierra Leone 1787; Britain abolishes the slave trade 1807; Sierra Leone becomes a Crown Colony 1808; Slavery Abolition Act 1833; slavery abolished in the USA 1865 and in Brazil 1888](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/abolition-timeline.svg)

*Figure 1. Milestones in the abolition of the slave trade and slavery. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1787** | the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade is founded in London; settlers land at the **Province of Freedom** in Sierra Leone |
| **1807** | the **Abolition of the Slave Trade Act**: British subjects may no longer take part in the slave trade |
| **1808** | the **United States** bans the import of slaves; Sierra Leone becomes a **Crown Colony** |
| **1815** | the **Congress of Vienna** condemns the slave trade |
| **1833** | the **Slavery Abolition Act** ends slavery in most of the British Empire (from 1834) |
| **1865** | slavery abolished in the **United States** |
| **1888** | slavery abolished in **Brazil** |

## Enforcing abolition

Abolition laws meant little unless they were enforced. Britain:

1. Created the **West Africa Squadron** of the Royal Navy (from **1808**) to patrol the coast and capture slave ships.
2. Made **Freetown** the squadron's base and landed the people freed from captured ships there.
3. Set up a **Vice-Admiralty Court** and, from **1819**, **Courts of Mixed Commission** in Freetown, with other countries, to try captured slave ships.
4. Signed **treaties** with other European countries and with **African rulers** to end the trade.
5. Later used force against slave-trading ports, such as the attack on **Lagos (1851)**.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Tens of thousands of **"Liberated Africans" (recaptives)**, freed from slave ships by the Royal Navy, were landed in **Freetown** between 1808 and the 1860s. They came from many parts of West Africa, settled in villages around the peninsula such as **Regent, Wellington, Hastings and Waterloo**, and together with earlier settlers formed the **Krio** people of Sierra Leone.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The **1807 Act** abolished the **slave trade** for British subjects; it did **not** free people who were already enslaved. **Slavery** itself in the British colonies was abolished only by the **1833 Act**.
:::

## Key points

- Reasons: **humanitarian and religious** campaigns, **African voices**, **economic change**, **revolutionary ideas and Haiti**, **resistance**, the **Somerset case**.
- **1807**: British slave trade abolished; **1833**: slavery abolished in most British colonies.
- Britain enforced abolition with the **West Africa Squadron** based at **Freetown**, **courts** and **treaties**.
- **Liberated Africans** landed at Freetown became part of the **Krio** people.

## Quick check

1. Name two British abolitionists.
2. Who was Olaudah Equiano?
3. Give two economic reasons for abolition.
4. What did the 1807 Act do?
5. Why was Freetown important in enforcing abolition?

**Answers:**

1. Any two: Granville Sharp; Thomas Clarkson; William Wilberforce.
2. A formerly enslaved African whose autobiography of 1789 described the horrors of the slave trade and supported abolition.
3. Any two: the Industrial Revolution increased the need for raw materials and markets; legitimate trade seemed more profitable; free labour was thought more efficient than slave labour.
4. It made it illegal for British subjects to take part in the slave trade.
5. It was the base of the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, the place where captured slave ships were tried and where liberated Africans were landed.
