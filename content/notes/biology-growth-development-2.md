# Growth and development: germination, animal development and measurement

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- describe the stages and conditions of seed germination
- distinguish epigeal from hypogeal germination
- explain major stages of growth and development in animals
- identify internal and external factors that affect growth
- describe practical methods of measuring growth
- calculate and interpret simple growth rates

## Seed germination

**Germination** is the resumption of growth of a viable seed embryo under suitable conditions. It begins when the seed absorbs water and ends, in the usual school-level description, when the young seedling becomes established and begins independent growth.

### Conditions required

- **Water:** softens the seed coat, activates enzymes and allows stored food to be mobilised and transported to the embryo.
- **Oxygen:** is required for aerobic respiration, which supplies energy for growth.
- **Suitable temperature:** allows enzymes and metabolic processes to function at an appropriate rate.
- **Viable embryo:** the embryo must be alive and capable of growth. Some seeds also require particular light or dormancy-breaking conditions.

Soil is not an essential condition for germination itself: seeds can germinate on a suitable moist support if water, oxygen and temperature are adequate. Light is required by some seeds but not all.

### Main events during germination

1. The dry seed absorbs water by **imbibition** and swells.
2. The seed coat may split as the seed expands.
3. Enzymes become active and stored food reserves are broken down into soluble substances that the embryo can use.
4. The **radicle** emerges first and develops into the primary root.
5. The **plumule** develops into the shoot.
6. The young seedling develops leaves and begins photosynthesis, becoming increasingly independent of the food stored in the seed.

In a cereal grain such as maize, the endosperm stores food. The scutellum, a specialised cotyledon, helps transfer digested food to the embryo. In a bean seed, food is stored mainly in the cotyledons.

### Epigeal and hypogeal germination

In **epigeal germination**, elongation of the hypocotyl carries the cotyledons above the soil surface. Bean is a common example.

In **hypogeal germination**, the cotyledons remain below the soil while the epicotyl elongates and carries the plumule upwards. Pea and maize are commonly used examples of hypogeal germination in school biology.

The distinction concerns the position of the cotyledons during germination, not whether the seed germinates above or below ground.

## Growth and development in animals

Animal development begins with a fertilised egg, or zygote. The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis to form an embryo. Cells differentiate and tissues and organs develop. After birth or hatching, the young animal grows and passes through further developmental stages until it reaches reproductive maturity.

The sequence varies among animal groups.

### Direct development

In direct development, the young animal resembles a smaller version of the adult and does not pass through a distinct larval stage. Mammals, birds and many reptiles show direct development, although the young still undergo substantial growth and maturation.

### Development with metamorphosis

**Metamorphosis** is a marked change in body form during development.

A frog has an aquatic tadpole stage with a tail and gills. During metamorphosis, limbs develop, lungs become functional, the tail is resorbed and the body changes towards the adult form.

In insects with complete metamorphosis, the stages are egg → larva → pupa → adult. A butterfly is an example. The larva is structurally different from the adult, and major reorganisation occurs in the pupal stage.

In insects with incomplete metamorphosis, the stages are egg → nymph → adult. The nymph resembles a smaller, immature adult and develops through successive moults. Grasshoppers and cockroaches are examples.

Growth in many insects occurs in steps because the rigid external exoskeleton must be shed during **moulting** before the body can expand. The new covering hardens after the moult.

## Factors affecting growth

Growth depends on the interaction of genetic, nutritional, hormonal and environmental factors.

### Internal factors

- **Genes:** inherited information influences potential body size, growth pattern and developmental timing.
- **Hormones:** growth hormone from the pituitary gland promotes growth in many tissues; thyroid hormones support normal development and metabolism; sex hormones contribute to the growth spurt and maturation at puberty.
- **Health and metabolism:** disease, inherited disorders and disturbances in hormone production can affect normal growth.

### External factors

- **Nutrition:** proteins supply amino acids for tissue building; carbohydrates and fats provide energy; vitamins and mineral salts are needed for normal metabolism, bone formation and other functions.
- **Water:** is needed for metabolic reactions, transport and maintenance of cell structure.
- **Temperature:** affects enzyme activity and metabolic rate. Very high or low temperatures can slow or damage biological processes.
- **Light:** affects photosynthesis and can influence plant development, including flowering in some species.
- **Oxygen:** supports aerobic respiration and energy release.
- **Space and competition:** overcrowding may reduce access to light, water, nutrients, food or territory.
- **Disease and parasites:** may damage tissues, reduce food intake or divert energy away from growth.

A shortage of one essential factor can limit growth even when other factors are abundant. For example, adding fertiliser will not correct severe water shortage.

## Measuring growth

Growth should be measured using a suitable feature and at consistent intervals.

Common measurements include:

| Measurement | Suitable example | Important point |
|---|---|---|
| Length or height | Seedling shoot, animal body length | Use the same reference points and instrument |
| Fresh mass | Plant or animal specimen | Includes water, so it can change rapidly with hydration |
| Dry mass | Plant tissue | Water is removed to constant mass; useful for comparing accumulated organic material |
| Cell number | Microorganisms or dividing tissue | Requires an appropriate counting method |
| Volume or girth | Fruit, stem or developing organ | Measure at the same position each time |

### A simple seedling investigation

To investigate growth in seedlings:

1. Select healthy seeds of the same species and similar size.
2. Germinate them under the same conditions.
3. Place seedlings in labelled groups and provide equal water, light and suitable temperature.
4. Measure shoot length or another chosen feature at the same time each day.
5. Record results in a table and calculate the mean for each day.
6. Plot mean measurement against time.
7. Keep other conditions constant so that the factor being investigated can be evaluated fairly.

Use enough seedlings to reduce the effect of individual differences. A ruler should be read at eye level to avoid parallax error. If destructive dry-mass measurements are made, use separate groups of seedlings for each sampling time because the same seedling cannot be dried and measured repeatedly.

### Calculating growth rate

The average growth rate over a period is:

**average growth rate = change in measurement ÷ time taken**

For example, if the mean shoot length increases from 2 cm to 8 cm over 6 days:

Change in length = 8 − 2 = 6 cm

Average growth rate = 6 cm ÷ 6 days = **1 cm per day**.

The units must match the measurements and time used. A growth rate calculated over a whole week is an average; it does not show whether growth was equally fast on every day.

## Quick check

1. Define germination.
2. State three conditions generally required for seed germination and explain the role of oxygen.
3. Which part of the embryo develops into the root?
4. Distinguish epigeal from hypogeal germination.
5. Give the stages of complete metamorphosis in an insect.
6. State two internal and three external factors that affect growth.
7. A seedling grows from 3.5 cm to 9.5 cm in 4 days. Calculate its average growth rate.
8. Why is dry mass often more useful than fresh mass when comparing plant growth?

**Answers**

1. The resumption of growth of a viable seed embryo under suitable conditions.
2. Water, oxygen and a suitable temperature. Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which supplies energy for growth.
3. The radicle.
4. In epigeal germination, cotyledons are carried above the soil; in hypogeal germination, they remain below the soil.
5. Egg → larva → pupa → adult.
6. Internal: genes and hormones. External: nutrition, water, temperature, light, oxygen, disease or competition (any three).
7. Change = 9.5 − 3.5 = 6 cm; average rate = 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5 cm per day**.
8. Dry mass excludes variable water content and therefore gives a better indication of accumulated organic material.
