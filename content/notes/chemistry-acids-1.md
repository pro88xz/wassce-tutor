Lime juice, vinegar and the sting of an ant are acidic; wood ash, soap and baking soda are alkaline. Acids and bases are among the most common chemicals in the home, the farm and industry. This lesson defines them in three ways, describes their properties and explains the difference between a **strong** acid and a **concentrated** one, a distinction many candidates miss.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define acids and bases using the Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis theories
- identify conjugate acid–base pairs
- describe the properties and reactions of acids and of alkalis
- define basicity and give examples of mono-, di- and tribasic acids
- distinguish strong from weak, and concentrated from dilute, acids and bases
- describe amphoteric oxides and hydroxides

## Three definitions

| Theory | Acid | Base | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arrhenius** | Produces **H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions** in water as the only positive ions | Produces **OH⁻ ions** in water | HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻; NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ |
| **Brønsted–Lowry** | A **proton (H⁺) donor** | A **proton acceptor** | NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ |
| **Lewis** | An **electron-pair acceptor** | An **electron-pair donor** | NH₃ (base) donates its lone pair to BF₃ (acid) |

The Arrhenius theory works only in water. Brønsted–Lowry explains why ammonia is a base: it **accepts a proton**.

### Conjugate acid–base pairs

When an acid donates a proton, what remains is its **conjugate base**; when a base accepts a proton, it becomes its **conjugate acid**.

$$\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$$

- HCl (acid) and Cl⁻ (its conjugate base)
- H₂O (base) and H₃O⁺ (its conjugate acid)

Water can act as **both** an acid and a base: it is **amphiprotic**.

## Properties of acids

1. **Sour taste** (never taste laboratory chemicals).
2. Turn **blue litmus red**.
3. React with **reactive metals** to give a salt and **hydrogen**: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
4. React with **carbonates and hydrogencarbonates** to give a salt, water and **carbon(IV) oxide** (effervescence): CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
5. React with **bases** to give a salt and water only (**neutralisation**): CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
6. Their aqueous solutions **conduct electricity**, because they contain ions.

**Acids need water.** Dry hydrogen chloride gas, or HCl dissolved in methylbenzene, does not turn dry blue litmus red and does not react with magnesium, because it does not ionise to form H⁺ ions without water.

### Basicity

:::callout{type=definition}
The **basicity** of an acid is the **number of replaceable hydrogen ions** that one molecule of the acid can produce.
:::

| Basicity | Acid | Ionisation |
|---|---|---|
| Monobasic | Hydrochloric acid HCl, nitric acid HNO₃, ethanoic acid CH₃COOH | HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ |
| Dibasic | Sulphuric acid H₂SO₄, carbonic acid H₂CO₃ | H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ |
| Tribasic | Phosphoric(V) acid H₃PO₄ | H₃PO₄ → 3H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ |

Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, has four hydrogen atoms but is **monobasic**: only the hydrogen of the –COOH group is replaceable.

## Bases and alkalis

:::callout{type=definition}
A **base** is a substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only (metal oxides and hydroxides). An **alkali** is a base that **dissolves in water** to give OH⁻ ions.
:::

All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. Copper(II) oxide is a base but does not dissolve in water; sodium hydroxide is a base **and** an alkali.

**Common alkalis:** sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide (lime water) and aqueous ammonia.

**Properties of alkalis:**

1. Bitter taste and a **soapy feel**.
2. Turn **red litmus blue**.
3. **Neutralise acids**: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
4. React with **ammonium salts** on warming to give **ammonia**: NH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + NH₃
5. **Precipitate** insoluble metal hydroxides from solutions of metal salts: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) (blue)

## Strength and concentration

| Strong | Weak |
|---|---|
| **Completely ionised** in water | **Partly ionised**: an equilibrium |
| Acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃ | Acids: ethanoic acid (vinegar), carbonic acid, citric acid |
| Bases: NaOH, KOH | Bases: aqueous ammonia |

Hydrochloric acid ionises **completely**:

$$\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{H}^+\text{(aq)} + \text{Cl}^-\text{(aq)}$$

Ethanoic acid ionises only **partly**; the ⇌ sign shows an equilibrium:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+$$

**Concentration** is a different idea. It is how much acid is dissolved per dm³.

- A **concentrated** solution has a lot of solute per dm³; a **dilute** one has little.
- You can have a **dilute strong** acid (0.01 mol/dm³ HCl) or a **concentrated weak** acid (glacial ethanoic acid).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Comparing equal concentrations of HCl and CH₃COOH (both 0.1 mol/dm³).**

| Test | 0.1 mol/dm³ HCl | 0.1 mol/dm³ CH₃COOH |
|---|---|---|
| pH | About 1 | About 3 |
| Electrical conductivity | High | Low |
| Rate of reaction with magnesium | Fast, vigorous fizzing | Slow |
| Volume of H₂ with excess Mg | **Same** | **Same** (all the acid eventually reacts) |

The strong acid has more H⁺ ions **at any moment**, so it reacts faster. Both contain the same total amount of acid, so they give the same total volume of hydrogen.
:::

## Amphoteric oxides and hydroxides

:::callout{type=definition}
An **amphoteric** oxide or hydroxide reacts with **both acids and alkalis** to form salts.
:::

Examples: zinc oxide, aluminium oxide, lead(II) oxide and their hydroxides.

$$\text{ZnO} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

$$\text{ZnO} + 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{Zn(OH)}_4$$

The second product is sodium zincate.

**Classes of oxides:**

- **acidic oxides** (non-metal oxides): CO₂, SO₂, P₄O₁₀
- **basic oxides** (metal oxides): Na₂O, CaO, CuO
- **amphoteric oxides**: ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO
- **neutral oxides**: CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O

:::callout{type=local-example}
Acids and bases are all around Sierra Leonean homes:

- **Lime and lemon juice** contain citric acid, and **sour palm wine** contains ethanoic acid.
- **Ant and bee stings** contain methanoic acid.
- **Wood ash**, used in traditional soap making, is alkaline because it contains potassium carbonate.
- **Lime (calcium hydroxide)** is used to whitewash walls and to reduce the acidity of farm soil.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Strong" does not mean "concentrated". **Strong** describes how completely an acid **ionises**; **concentrated** describes how much acid is **dissolved** per dm³.
:::

## Key points

- **Arrhenius:** acids give H⁺, bases give OH⁻ in water. **Brønsted–Lowry:** acids donate protons, bases accept them. **Lewis:** acids accept electron pairs.
- Acids turn blue litmus red and react with **metals (H₂), carbonates (CO₂) and bases (salt + water)**; they need **water** to show acidity.
- **Basicity** = number of replaceable H⁺ per molecule: HCl 1, H₂SO₄ 2, H₃PO₄ 3.
- An **alkali** is a **soluble base**.
- **Strong** = fully ionised; **weak** = partly ionised. **Concentration** is a separate idea.
- **Amphoteric** oxides (ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO) react with both acids and alkalis.

## Quick check

1. Identify the conjugate base of H₂SO₄ in H₂SO₄ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻.
2. What is the basicity of ethanoic acid? Explain.
3. Distinguish a dilute strong acid from a concentrated weak acid, with an example of each.
4. Write an equation to show that aluminium oxide is amphoteric (reaction with acid).

**Answers:**

1. HSO₄⁻.
2. One: only the hydrogen of the –COOH group ionises.
3. A dilute strong acid is fully ionised but has little acid per dm³ (0.01 mol/dm³ HCl); a concentrated weak acid has a lot of acid per dm³ but is only partly ionised (glacial ethanoic acid).
4. Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (it also reacts with NaOH to form sodium aluminate).
