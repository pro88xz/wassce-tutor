When hydrogen burns in chlorine, one volume of each gas gives exactly two volumes of hydrogen chloride. Why such neat numbers? Because equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. This lesson covers the laws that describe gases in reactions and mixtures: Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes, Avogadro's law, Graham's law of diffusion and Dalton's law of partial pressures.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes and use it to calculate reacting gas volumes
- state Avogadro's law and use it to explain combining volumes
- state Graham's law and compare rates of diffusion of gases
- state Dalton's law of partial pressures and correct for water vapour
- list the assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases

## Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes

:::callout{type=definition}
**Gay-Lussac's law:** when gases react, the **volumes** of the reacting gases and of any gaseous products are in a **simple whole-number ratio**, provided they are measured at the **same temperature and pressure**.
:::

| Reaction | Volume ratio |
|---|---|
| H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g) | 1 : 1 : 2 |
| N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) | 1 : 3 : 2 |
| 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g) | 2 : 1 : 2 |

The ratio of volumes is the same as the ratio of **moles** in the balanced equation.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**20 cm³ of methane is exploded with 60 cm³ of oxygen. What is the volume and composition of the gas left at room temperature?**

$$\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

- 20 cm³ CH₄ reacts with $2 \times 20 = 40$ cm³ O₂.
- Oxygen left over: $60 - 40 = 20$ cm³.
- CO₂ formed: 20 cm³.
- Water is a **liquid** at room temperature, so it has almost no volume.

Gas left: **40 cm³**, made of 20 cm³ CO₂ and 20 cm³ O₂. Passing it through sodium hydroxide solution would absorb the CO₂, leaving 20 cm³ of oxygen.
:::

## Avogadro's law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Avogadro's law:** **equal volumes of all gases**, at the same temperature and pressure, contain **equal numbers of molecules**.
:::

Avogadro's law explains Gay-Lussac's results. If 1 volume of hydrogen and 1 volume of chlorine give **2** volumes of hydrogen chloride, then 1 molecule of each gives **2** molecules of HCl. This is only possible if hydrogen and chlorine are **diatomic** (H₂ and Cl₂).

It also leads to the **molar volume**: one mole of any gas occupies **22.4 dm³ at s.t.p.** (about 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure).

## Graham's law of diffusion

:::callout{type=definition}
**Graham's law:** at constant temperature and pressure, the **rate of diffusion** of a gas is **inversely proportional to the square root of its density** (or of its relative molecular mass).

$$\dfrac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_2}{M_1}}$$
:::

Lighter gases diffuse **faster**. Because **time** is the inverse of rate, the time taken for equal volumes to diffuse follows

$$\dfrac{t_1}{t_2} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_1}{M_2}}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**1. Compare the rates of diffusion of hydrogen (M = 2) and oxygen (M = 32).**

$$\dfrac{r_{\text{H}_2}}{r_{\text{O}_2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{32}{2}} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$

Hydrogen diffuses **4 times** as fast as oxygen.

**2. Gas X diffuses twice as fast as sulphur(IV) oxide (M = 64). Find the relative molecular mass of X.**

$$2 = \sqrt{\dfrac{64}{M_X}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4 = \dfrac{64}{M_X} \quad\Rightarrow\quad M_X = 16$$

X could be methane, CH₄.
:::

The ammonia–hydrogen chloride tube experiment shows Graham's law: ammonia (M = 17) travels further than hydrogen chloride (M = 36.5) before they meet.

## Dalton's law of partial pressures

:::callout{type=definition}
**Dalton's law of partial pressures:** the **total pressure** of a mixture of gases that do not react is the **sum of the partial pressures** of the gases in it. The partial pressure of a gas is the pressure it would exert if it filled the container alone.

$$P_{\text{total}} = P_A + P_B + P_C + \cdots$$
:::

**Collecting a gas over water.** The gas in the jar is mixed with water vapour, so

$$P_{\text{dry gas}} = P_{\text{total}} - \text{s.v.p. of water}$$

where s.v.p. is the saturated vapour pressure of water at that temperature.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Oxygen is collected over water at 27 °C and a total pressure of 760 mmHg. The s.v.p. of water at 27 °C is 27 mmHg. What is the pressure of the dry oxygen?**

$$P_{\text{O}_2} = 760 - 27 = 733 \text{ mmHg}$$

This corrected pressure is used in the general gas equation to find the volume of dry oxygen at s.t.p.
:::

Each gas's partial pressure is also proportional to its share of the molecules. Air is about 78% nitrogen, so at 760 mmHg the partial pressure of nitrogen is about $0.78 \times 760 \approx 593$ mmHg.

## The kinetic theory of gases

1. A gas consists of a large number of tiny particles in **constant, random, straight-line motion**.
2. The **volume of the particles** is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
3. There are **no forces of attraction** between the particles.
4. Collisions are **perfectly elastic**.
5. The **average kinetic energy** of the particles is proportional to the **kelvin temperature**.

Real gases depart from this ideal at **high pressure** and **low temperature**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In Graham's law the **lighter** gas has the **larger rate** but the **shorter time**. Put the masses the right way round: rate ratio uses $\sqrt{M_2/M_1}$, time ratio uses $\sqrt{M_1/M_2}$.
:::

## Key points

- **Gay-Lussac:** reacting gas volumes are in simple whole-number ratios, equal to the mole ratios in the equation.
- **Avogadro:** equal volumes of gases at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules; molar volume = **22.4 dm³ at s.t.p.**
- **Graham:** rate ∝ $1/\sqrt{M}$; H₂ diffuses 4 times as fast as O₂.
- **Dalton:** total pressure = sum of partial pressures; dry gas pressure = total − s.v.p. of water.
- The kinetic theory of gases assumes **negligible particle volume, no attractions, elastic collisions**.

## Quick check

1. What volume of oxygen is needed to burn 30 cm³ of carbon(II) oxide? (2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂)
2. State Avogadro's law.
3. How many times faster does helium (M = 4) diffuse than sulphur(IV) oxide (M = 64)?
4. Hydrogen is collected over water at a total pressure of 750 mmHg; the s.v.p. of water is 18 mmHg. Find the pressure of the dry hydrogen.

**Answers:**

1. 15 cm³ (half the volume of CO).
2. Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
3. $\sqrt{64/4} = \sqrt{16} = 4$ times faster.
4. $750 - 18 = 732$ mmHg.
