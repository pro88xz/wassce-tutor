**Indirect rule** was the British system of colonial administration in which the colonial government ruled the people **through their own traditional rulers and institutions**, under the supervision of British officials. It is linked above all with **Frederick Lugard**, who developed it in **Northern Nigeria** after 1900 and described it in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922). Indirect rule **worked well in Northern Nigeria**, **partly worked in the West**, and **failed in Eastern Nigeria**, where the British created **warrant chiefs**. This lesson explains the **features, reasons, advantages and disadvantages of indirect rule** and why its success varied.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define indirect rule and describe its main features
- explain why the British adopted indirect rule
- explain why indirect rule succeeded in Northern Nigeria
- explain why it partly succeeded in Western Nigeria and failed in Eastern Nigeria
- describe indirect rule in Sierra Leone
- state the advantages and disadvantages of indirect rule

## Meaning and features

:::callout{type=definition}
**Indirect rule** is a system of colonial government in which the colonial power rules **through existing traditional rulers**, such as emirs, obas and chiefs, who keep their positions and govern their people by **local law and custom**, under the control and supervision of **colonial officials**.
:::

**Main features:**

1. **Traditional rulers** kept their titles and ruled their people.
2. A **Native Authority** (the chief and his council) was the local government unit.
3. **Native Treasuries** collected **taxes** and paid chiefs' salaries and local services.
4. **Native Courts** judged cases by **customary or Islamic law**, except serious crimes.
5. A British **Resident** or **District Commissioner** supervised and **advised** the rulers, and could remove them.
6. Chiefs could **not** raise armies, make treaties or act against colonial policy.

## Why the British used indirect rule

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Shortage of British staff** | there were too few British officials to govern huge territories directly |
| **Lack of money** | it was **cheap**: local taxes paid for local administration |
| **Existing systems** | centralised states, such as the **emirates of Northern Nigeria**, already had rulers, courts and taxes |
| **Language and communication** | traditional rulers spoke to the people in their own language |
| **Fewer revolts** | people were more likely to obey their own rulers than foreigners |
| **Climate and disease** | Europeans feared malaria and other diseases in the interior |
| **Lugard's ideas** | he believed Africans should develop "along their own lines" under British guidance |

### Why it succeeded in Northern Nigeria

1. The **Sokoto Caliphate** had a **centralised** system: emirs with authority, officials, courts and **an organised tax system**.
2. People were used to **obeying the emirs**, whose authority was backed by **Islam**.
3. **Islamic law and courts** already existed.
4. **Lugard** conquered the north (**Kano and Sokoto, 1903**) and then kept the emirs in office under **Residents**.
5. The emirs **cooperated** because the system protected their position.

### Why it partly worked in Western Nigeria

1. **Yoruba obas** were not absolute rulers; they were checked by **councils of chiefs** and by custom (for example, the **Oyo Mesi** in Old Oyo).
2. The British treated obas like emirs and **made them too powerful**, which angered chiefs and the educated elite.
3. **Direct taxation**, introduced in the west from **1918**, caused unrest, such as the **Egba (Adubi) rising of 1918** in Abeokuta.
4. **Educated Africans** in Lagos and other towns resented being ruled through traditional rulers.

### Why it failed in Eastern Nigeria

1. Most **Igbo**, **Ibibio** and other peoples had **no kings**: they were governed by **elders, title holders, age grades and village assemblies** (often called **acephalous** or "stateless" societies).
2. The British created **warrant chiefs**, given a warrant of authority by the colonial government, who had **no traditional standing**.
3. Many warrant chiefs were **corrupt and oppressive**.
4. The introduction of **direct taxation (1928)** and rumours that **women** would be counted for tax led to the **Aba Women's War of 1929**, in which many women were killed.
5. After 1929 the British **reformed** the system, using village councils and other bodies.

## Indirect rule in Sierra Leone

After the **Protectorate** was declared in **1896**, the British ruled the hinterland through **paramount chiefs** under **District Commissioners**. The **Hut Tax** of 1898 was collected through chiefs. In **1937** the **Native Administration** system was introduced, with **chiefdom treasuries** and native courts. The **Colony** (Freetown and the peninsula) was governed **directly** as a Crown Colony.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **chiefdom** system in Sierra Leone today, with **paramount chiefs** elected by **Tribal Authorities** and chiefdom councils, grew out of colonial indirect rule. This is why chiefs still play an important part in local government, land matters and customary law in the provinces.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of indirect rule

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **cheap** to run | made some chiefs **autocratic and corrupt** |
| **preserved** African customs and institutions | **excluded the educated elite** from government |
| fewer **revolts** | **slowed modernisation** and Western education, especially in Northern Nigeria |
| trained some rulers in **local administration** | encouraged **ethnic and regional divisions** |
| used people's **own language and law** | failed where there were **no centralised rulers** (warrant chiefs) |
| kept **continuity** of local leadership | chiefs became **agents of the colonial government** and lost respect |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Indirect rule did **not** give real power to African rulers. Chiefs ruled **under British control**: the Resident or District Commissioner could **depose** them, and they could not oppose colonial policy. In an essay, show that indirect rule was a method of **colonial control**, not self-government.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Lord Lugard**: the British administrator whose name is linked with indirect rule, first applied in Northern Nigeria.
- **Native Authority**: under indirect rule, the traditional ruler and council that governed local people under British supervision.
- **Warrant chiefs**: chiefs appointed by the British in south-eastern Nigeria, where there were no traditional kings.
:::

## Key points

- **Indirect rule** = ruling **through traditional rulers** under British supervision; linked with **Lugard** and Northern Nigeria.
- Features: **Native Authorities, Native Treasuries, Native Courts**, Residents or District Commissioners.
- Reasons: **few staff, little money, existing systems**, language, fewer revolts.
- **North:** succeeded; **West:** partly; **East:** failed (**warrant chiefs**, **Aba Women's War 1929**).
- Sierra Leone: **paramount chiefs** in the Protectorate; **Native Administration 1937**.

## Quick check

1. Define indirect rule.
2. Who is most closely associated with indirect rule in Nigeria?
3. Give three reasons the British adopted indirect rule.
4. Why did indirect rule succeed in Northern Nigeria?
5. Who were warrant chiefs?
6. What happened in 1929 in Eastern Nigeria?

**Answers:**

1. A system of colonial government in which the colonial power ruled through existing traditional rulers under the supervision of colonial officials.
2. Frederick (later Lord) Lugard.
3. Any three: too few British officials; lack of money; existing centralised systems; language; to reduce revolts; disease; Lugard's ideas.
4. The emirates already had centralised rulers, officials, Islamic courts and a tax system, and people obeyed the emirs.
5. Men given warrants of authority by the British to act as chiefs in Eastern Nigeria, where there had been no kings.
6. The Aba Women's War: women protested against warrant chiefs and feared taxation of women, and many were killed.
