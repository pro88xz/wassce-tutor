"It is 32 degrees today" — but 32 degrees of what, measured how? A thermometer only works because some property of a substance changes steadily with how hot it is. This lesson explains temperature, the scales we use and how a thermometer is made.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish temperature from heat
- explain thermometric properties and fixed points
- convert between the Celsius and Kelvin scales
- describe the liquid-in-glass and clinical thermometers and compare mercury with alcohol

## Temperature and heat are different

:::callout{type=definition}
**Temperature** is the **degree of hotness or coldness** of a body. It determines the direction in which heat flows: from a body at higher temperature to one at lower temperature.

**Heat** is a form of **energy** that is transferred from a hotter body to a colder one because of their temperature difference.
:::

| Temperature | Heat |
|---|---|
| degree of hotness | a form of energy |
| measured with a thermometer | measured with a calorimeter (by its effects) |
| unit: kelvin (K) or °C | unit: joule (J) |
| does not depend on the amount of substance | depends on mass, material and temperature change |

A bucket of warm water can contain more heat energy than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup is at a higher temperature.

## Thermometric properties

:::callout{type=definition}
A **thermometric property** is a physical property of a substance that **changes continuously and measurably** with temperature, so it can be used to measure temperature.
:::

| Thermometer | Thermometric property |
|---|---|
| Liquid-in-glass (mercury or alcohol) | change in **volume (length)** of a liquid column |
| Constant-volume gas thermometer | change in **pressure** of a gas at constant volume |
| Platinum resistance thermometer | change in **electrical resistance** of a metal wire |
| Thermocouple | **e.m.f.** produced at the junction of two different metals |
| Bimetallic thermometer | **bending** of a bimetallic strip |
| Pyrometer | colour or intensity of **radiation** from a hot body |

## Fixed points and temperature scales

![A mercury thermometer with a bulb and capillary; the lower fixed point is 0 degrees Celsius in pure melting ice and the upper fixed point is 100 degrees Celsius in steam](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/thermometer-fixed-points.svg)

*Figure 1. A liquid-in-glass thermometer and its fixed points. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Lower fixed point (ice point):** the temperature of **pure melting ice** at standard atmospheric pressure, **0 °C**.
- **Upper fixed point (steam point):** the temperature of **steam above pure water boiling** at standard atmospheric pressure, **100 °C**.
- The **fundamental interval** is the difference between them (100 divisions on the Celsius scale).
:::

### Celsius and Kelvin

The **Kelvin (absolute) scale** starts at **absolute zero**, the lowest possible temperature, at which particles have the least possible energy. A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C.

$$T\,(\text{K}) = \theta\,(°\text{C}) + 273$$

| | Celsius | Kelvin |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute zero | −273 °C | 0 K |
| Ice point | 0 °C | 273 K |
| Normal body temperature | 37 °C | 310 K |
| Steam point | 100 °C | 373 K |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Kelvin temperatures are written **without** the degree sign: 300 K, not 300 °K. And a temperature **difference** is the same number in both scales: a rise from 20 °C to 50 °C is a rise of 30 K.
:::

## The liquid-in-glass thermometer

It consists of a thin-walled **glass bulb** containing the liquid, joined to a fine **capillary tube** of uniform bore, sealed at the top, with a scale on the stem.

**Design features:**

- a **thin-walled bulb** so heat passes in quickly (quick response)
- a **narrow capillary** so a small expansion gives a long movement of the thread (sensitivity)
- a **uniform bore** so equal temperature changes give equal lengths (a linear scale)

### Mercury versus alcohol

| Mercury | Alcohol |
|---|---|
| opaque and shiny, easy to read | colourless (must be dyed) |
| does not wet glass | wets glass, so the reading can be slightly low |
| good conductor of heat: responds quickly | poor conductor |
| boils at 357 °C: good for high temperatures | boils at 78 °C: cannot measure boiling water |
| freezes at −39 °C: useless in very cold places | freezes at −115 °C: good for low temperatures |
| expansion is small but regular | expands about six times more (more sensitive) |
| poisonous | not poisonous |

## The clinical thermometer

A clinical thermometer measures human body temperature (normal about 37 °C).

- **Short range**, typically 35 °C to 43 °C, so the scale can be widely spaced and read to 0.1 °C.
- A **narrow constriction (kink)** just above the bulb stops the mercury from flowing back when the thermometer is removed from the mouth or armpit, so the reading can be taken later.
- It is reset by **shaking** the mercury back past the constriction.
- It must **not** be sterilised in boiling water: 100 °C is beyond its range and would break it.

:::callout{type=local-example}
At health centres across Sierra Leone, nurses now often use digital or infrared thermometers, which use a thermistor or the infrared radiation from the forehead. A fever of 38.5 °C or more is a common warning sign of malaria.
:::

## Key points

- Temperature = degree of hotness; heat = energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
- A thermometer uses a thermometric property that changes steadily with temperature.
- Ice point 0 °C, steam point 100 °C; $T(\text{K}) = \theta(°\text{C}) + 273$.
- Mercury suits high temperatures and is opaque; alcohol suits low temperatures and expands more.
- The clinical thermometer has a short range and a constriction.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **distinguish heat and temperature**, **define fixed points**, **state thermometric properties** of different thermometers, **compare mercury and alcohol** as thermometric liquids, and **explain the features of a clinical thermometer** (especially the constriction). Conversions between °C and K are frequent in objective questions.
:::

## Quick check

1. Convert 27 °C to kelvin.
2. What is the purpose of the constriction in a clinical thermometer?
3. Give one advantage of alcohol over mercury as a thermometric liquid.

**Answers:** 1. 300 K. 2. It stops the mercury flowing back into the bulb, so the reading stays after the thermometer is removed. 3. For example: it can measure very low temperatures (freezes at −115 °C), or it expands more (more sensitive), or it is not poisonous.
