In July 2022 a bottle of drink that cost Le 10,000 suddenly cost NLe 10, yet nobody was richer or poorer. A few years earlier, the same bottle had cost far less. Both changes are about the **value of money**: one only changed the unit, the other reduced what each leone could buy. This lesson covers legal tender and the kinds of money, the leone and its redenomination, and how the value of money changes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define legal tender and distinguish limited and unlimited legal tender
- describe the kinds of money: token, fiat, bank and near money
- describe the leone and explain the 2022 redenomination
- explain the value of money and calculate changes using a price index
- explain inflation and deflation and their effects on traders

## Legal tender and kinds of money

:::callout{type=definition}
**Legal tender** is money that **must be accepted by law** in payment of debts. In Sierra Leone the notes and coins issued by the **Bank of Sierra Leone** are legal tender.
:::

- **Unlimited legal tender**: must be accepted for **any amount**, as bank notes are.
- **Limited legal tender**: must be accepted only **up to a set amount**, as some coins are, so nobody can be forced to take a huge debt in small coins.

| Kind of money | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Currency** | Notes and coins in circulation |
| **Token money** | Money whose **face value is greater than the value of the material** it is made of (modern coins and notes) |
| **Fiat money** | Money that has value because the **government declares** it legal tender, not because it is backed by gold |
| **Bank (deposit) money** | Money held in bank accounts and moved by cheque, transfer or card |
| **Near money (quasi-money)** | Assets easily turned into money but **not themselves** a means of payment: savings and fixed deposits, treasury bills, bills of exchange, postal orders |
| **Electronic and mobile money** | Money held and moved electronically |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **cheque is not money**. It is an order to a bank to transfer **bank money**. It is not legal tender, so a seller may refuse it.
:::

## The leone

- The **leone** became Sierra Leone's currency in **1964**, replacing British West African currency. One leone is divided into **100 cents**.
- It is issued by the **Bank of Sierra Leone**.
- Over the years inflation raised prices so much that everyday payments needed thousands of leones.

### The 2022 redenomination

:::callout{type=definition}
**Redenomination** is changing the **unit** of a currency by removing zeros. Prices, wages, debts and savings are all converted at the same rate, so **purchasing power does not change**.
:::

![The 2022 redenomination: Le 1,000 became NLe 1, Le 50,000 became NLe 50 and Le 2,000,000 became NLe 2,000; prices, wages and savings were all divided by 1,000, so purchasing power was unchanged](https://wasscetutor.com/img/commerce/redenomination.svg)

*Figure 1. Three zeros were removed from the leone on 1 July 2022. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

On **1 July 2022** the Bank of Sierra Leone introduced the **new leone (NLe)**, with **1,000 old leones = 1 new leone**. New notes from NLe 1 to NLe 20 were issued, and old notes were withdrawn over a changeover period.

**Reasons for the redenomination:**

1. To make **prices and calculations simpler**, with fewer zeros.
2. To reduce the **cost and risk of carrying and handling** large bundles of notes.
3. To make **accounting, computer systems and ATMs** easier to run.
4. To **restore confidence** in the national currency.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Converting old to new leones** (divide by 1,000).

| Item | Old leones | New leones |
|---|---|---|
| A loaf of bread | Le 5,000 | NLe 5 |
| A bag of rice | Le 450,000 | NLe 450 |
| A monthly salary | Le 2,500,000 | NLe 2,500 |

The bread-to-salary ratio is unchanged: in both currencies the salary buys $2500 \div 5 = 500$ loaves. That is why redenomination does **not** change purchasing power. **Devaluation** is different: it lowers the leone's value against **foreign** currencies.
:::

## The value of money

:::callout{type=definition}
The **value of money** is its **purchasing power**: the quantity of goods and services one unit of money can buy. When prices rise, the value of money **falls**.
:::

Changes in prices are measured with a **price index** (the cost of a fixed basket of goods compared with a base year set at 100).

$$\text{value of money} = \dfrac{100}{\text{price index}} \times \text{value in base year}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The price index rises from 100 (base year) to 125.**

$$\text{value of NLe 1} = \dfrac{100}{125} \times 1 = \text{NLe } 0.80$$

One leone now buys only what 80 cents bought in the base year: its value has fallen by **20%**, although prices rose by 25%.

**A salary of NLe 2,000** in the base year is worth $\dfrac{100}{125} \times 2000 = $ NLe 1,600 in base-year prices. The worker is worse off unless the salary rises by at least 25%.
:::

## Inflation and deflation

| | Inflation | Deflation |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | A **persistent rise** in the general price level; value of money falls | A **persistent fall** in the general price level; value of money rises |
| **Gainers** | **Debtors** (repay in money worth less); traders holding stock; owners of property | **Creditors**, savers and people on fixed incomes |
| **Losers** | **Creditors**, savers and people on **fixed incomes** (pensioners, salary earners) | **Debtors**; businesses, as falling prices cut profits and production |
| **Effect on business** | Uncertainty; higher costs; exports less competitive | Falling sales and profits; unemployment |

**Causes of inflation:**

- **demand-pull**: too much money chasing too few goods
- **cost-push**: rising costs of fuel, wages and imported raw materials
- **excessive money supply**
- **imported inflation**: a weaker leone makes imports dearer
- **shortages of goods**

**Control of inflation:**

- **monetary policy**: the central bank raises interest rates and reduces credit
- **fiscal policy**: the government cuts spending and raises taxes
- **increasing production**, especially of food
- **price controls**
- **stabilising the exchange rate**

## Exchange rate

:::callout{type=definition}
The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**An importer in Freetown must pay US\$4,000 for goods. Suppose US\$1 = NLe 24.**

$$\text{cost in leones} = 4000 \times 24 = \text{NLe } 96{,}000$$

If the leone falls to US\$1 = NLe 25, the same goods cost $4000 \times 25 = $ NLe 100,000: **NLe 4,000 more**. This is how a weaker leone causes imported inflation.
:::

## Key points

- **Legal tender** must be accepted by law: notes are unlimited, some coins limited.
- Kinds of money: **token, fiat, bank, near and mobile money**; a cheque is **not** money.
- The **2022 redenomination** removed three zeros (Le 1,000 = NLe 1) and did **not** change purchasing power.
- **Value of money** = purchasing power; it falls when prices rise. Value = 100 ÷ price index.
- **Inflation** helps debtors and hurts creditors and fixed-income earners; **deflation** does the opposite.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **define legal tender** and **near money**, to **explain the value of money** and how inflation affects it, and to **state the effects of inflation on traders, creditors and debtors**. Questions on your own currency, and on redenomination versus devaluation, are increasingly common.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is legal tender?
2. Give two examples of near money.
3. The price index rises from 100 to 160. What is the value of NLe 1 in base-year terms?
4. Who gains from inflation: debtors or creditors? Explain.

**Answers:** 1. Money that must by law be accepted in payment of debts. 2. Any two: savings deposits, fixed deposits, treasury bills, bills of exchange, postal orders. 3. $\dfrac{100}{160} = $ NLe 0.625 (62.5 cents). 4. Debtors, because they repay loans with money that buys less than when they borrowed.
