The **consequences of the abolition of the slave trade** for West Africa included the growth of **"legitimate" trade** in **palm oil, palm kernels, groundnuts, timber and other produce**; the founding of settlements for freed people in **Sierra Leone (1787–1792)** and **Liberia (1822)**; the rise of **Christian missions and Western education**, centred on places such as **Fourah Bay College (1827)**; new **African merchant and educated elites**; and growing **European involvement inland**, which led towards **colonial rule**. This lesson explains these consequences and their importance.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and growth of legitimate trade
- explain the effects of legitimate trade on West African states and peoples
- describe the founding of Sierra Leone and Liberia as homes for freed people
- explain the spread of Christian missions and Western education
- explain how abolition contributed to European colonisation

## Legitimate trade

:::callout{type=definition}
**Legitimate trade** was the trade in **agricultural and forest produce**, instead of slaves, that Europeans encouraged after abolition. Its main products were **palm oil and palm kernels**, **groundnuts**, **timber**, **gum**, **cotton** and later **cocoa** and **coffee**.
:::

**Why it grew:**

1. **European industry** needed **palm oil** for soap, candles and machine lubricants, and **groundnut oil** for food and soap.
2. Abolition closed the slave trade, so traders looked for new goods.
3. **Missionaries and abolitionists** promoted it as a "civilising" alternative.

**Effects of legitimate trade:**

| Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **New producers** | ordinary farmers and families could produce and sell palm oil and groundnuts, not only rulers |
| **New merchant class** | African traders grew rich, for example in the **Niger Delta** and among the **Krio** of Sierra Leone |
| **Decline of some rulers' power** | kings who depended on the slave trade lost revenue |
| **Continued domestic slavery** | slaves were sometimes used to produce palm oil and groundnuts |
| **Rivalry and conflict** | competition for control of trade routes led to wars and to European intervention |
| **European interest inland** | traders and governments wanted to reach producing areas directly |

## Homes for freed people

| Settlement | Founding | Settlers |
|---|---|---|
| **Sierra Leone** | **1787** (Province of Freedom); **1792** (Freetown, by Nova Scotian settlers); Crown Colony **1808** | Black Poor from London, **Nova Scotians**, **Jamaican Maroons (1800)**, and **Liberated Africans** |
| **Liberia** | **1822**, founded by the **American Colonization Society**; independent **1847** | freed African Americans, and later recaptives |

The founding of Freetown is studied in detail in the Sierra Leone topics.

## Missions and Western education

After abolition, **Christian missions** spread widely in West Africa:

- The **Church Missionary Society (CMS)**, **Methodists** and others worked from Freetown, the Gold Coast, Lagos and Calabar.
- **Fourah Bay College**, founded in Freetown in **1827**, trained teachers, clergy and later university graduates, and became known as the "Athens of West Africa".
- **Samuel Ajayi Crowther**, a Yoruba recaptive educated in Freetown and at Fourah Bay College, became the first African **Anglican bishop** in 1864.
- Missions brought **schools, hospitals, printing** and **written African languages**.

## A new educated elite

Mission education produced an **educated African elite** of teachers, clergy, lawyers, doctors and journalists, many of them **Krio** from Sierra Leone. Members of this elite later became leaders of **nationalism** and **Pan-Africanism**.

## Abolition and colonialism

Abolition contributed to colonialism because:

1. The **anti-slavery patrols and treaties** brought European navies and officials onto the coast and inland.
2. European powers used **"suppressing the slave trade"** as a reason to intervene in African states.
3. **Legitimate trade** made Europeans want direct control of producing areas and trade routes.
4. Missionaries and traders asked their governments for **protection**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Legitimate trade" did not mean that slavery disappeared at once in West Africa. **Domestic slavery** continued in many places, and enslaved people were sometimes used to produce the new export crops.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Samuel Ajayi Crowther**: a Yoruba recaptive educated in Freetown and at Fourah Bay College, who became the first African Anglican bishop.
- **Fourah Bay College**: the college founded in Freetown in 1827 by the Church Missionary Society, which trained West Africa's educated elite.
- **Liberia**: the settlement founded in 1822 for freed Black Americans, which became independent in 1847.
:::

## Key points

- **Legitimate trade**: palm oil, palm kernels, groundnuts, timber; driven by **European industry**.
- Effects: **new producers and merchants**, decline of slave-trading rulers, **conflict** and **European interest inland**.
- **Sierra Leone (1787/1792)** and **Liberia (1822, independent 1847)** became homes for freed people.
- **Missions and education** (CMS, **Fourah Bay College 1827**, **Ajayi Crowther**) created an **educated elite**.
- Abolition helped open the way to **colonial rule**.
