Every answer you write in a WASSCE Physics paper is a physical quantity: a number **and** a unit. "The speed is 20" earns nothing; "the speed is 20 m s⁻¹" earns the mark. This lesson gives you the language the rest of Physics is written in.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a physical quantity and distinguish fundamental from derived quantities
- state the seven SI base quantities with their units and symbols
- derive the SI units of common quantities from their defining equations
- use prefixes and standard form, and convert between units correctly

## What is a physical quantity?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **physical quantity** is any property of a body or a phenomenon that can be **measured** and expressed as a **number multiplied by a unit**.
:::

Length, mass, time, temperature, force and electric current are physical quantities. Beauty, kindness and taste are not: they cannot be measured on an agreed scale.

Every physical quantity has two parts:

$$\text{quantity} = \text{numerical value} \times \text{unit}$$

For example, in $m = 65\ \text{kg}$, the number is 65 and the unit is the kilogram. Change the unit and the number changes too: $65\ \text{kg} = 65\,000\ \text{g}$. The quantity itself has not changed.

## Fundamental and derived quantities

:::callout{type=definition}
**Fundamental (base) quantities** are quantities that are not defined in terms of other quantities. **Derived quantities** are quantities obtained by combining fundamental quantities through multiplication or division.
:::

The International System of Units (**SI**) uses seven fundamental quantities:

| Fundamental quantity | SI unit | Unit symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

The first three, **length, mass and time**, appear in almost every WASSCE question. Learn all seven: a common objective question simply asks which one of four quantities is fundamental.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Weight is not a fundamental quantity.** Weight is a force ($W = mg$), so it is derived. **Mass** is the fundamental one. Likewise, **temperature** is fundamental but **heat** (an energy) is derived.
:::

## Deriving SI units

The unit of a derived quantity comes straight from its defining equation. Replace each quantity by its SI unit and simplify.

| Derived quantity | Defining equation | SI unit | Special name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Area | length × breadth | m² | — |
| Volume | length × breadth × height | m³ | — |
| Density | mass ÷ volume | kg m⁻³ | — |
| Speed, velocity | distance ÷ time | m s⁻¹ | — |
| Acceleration | change in velocity ÷ time | m s⁻² | — |
| Force | mass × acceleration | kg m s⁻² | newton (N) |
| Momentum | mass × velocity | kg m s⁻¹ | (= N s) |
| Pressure | force ÷ area | N m⁻² = kg m⁻¹ s⁻² | pascal (Pa) |
| Work, energy | force × distance | N m = kg m² s⁻² | joule (J) |
| Power | work ÷ time | J s⁻¹ = kg m² s⁻³ | watt (W) |
| Frequency | 1 ÷ period | s⁻¹ | hertz (Hz) |
| Electric charge | current × time | A s | coulomb (C) |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Find the SI base units of pressure.**

$$\text{pressure} = \dfrac{\text{force}}{\text{area}} = \dfrac{\text{mass} \times \text{acceleration}}{\text{area}}$$

$$\text{unit} = \dfrac{\text{kg} \times \text{m s}^{-2}}{\text{m}^2} = \text{kg m}^{-1}\ \text{s}^{-2}$$

So $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N m}^{-2} = 1\ \text{kg m}^{-1}\ \text{s}^{-2}$.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Express the joule in base units.**

Work = force × distance, so $\text{J} = \text{N} \times \text{m} = (\text{kg m s}^{-2}) \times \text{m} = \text{kg m}^2\ \text{s}^{-2}$.

Check with kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$: $\text{kg} \times (\text{m s}^{-1})^2 = \text{kg m}^2\ \text{s}^{-2}$. The same unit, as it must be.
:::

## Prefixes: very large and very small numbers

Physics deals with the width of an atom and the distance to the Sun. Prefixes stop us writing long strings of zeros.

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| giga | G | $10^{9}$ | 1 GHz = $10^{9}$ Hz |
| mega | M | $10^{6}$ | 1 MW = $10^{6}$ W |
| kilo | k | $10^{3}$ | 1 km = 1000 m |
| centi | c | $10^{-2}$ | 1 cm = 0.01 m |
| milli | m | $10^{-3}$ | 1 mA = 0.001 A |
| micro | μ | $10^{-6}$ | 1 μF = $10^{-6}$ F |
| nano | n | $10^{-9}$ | 1 nm = $10^{-9}$ m |
| pico | p | $10^{-12}$ | 1 pF = $10^{-12}$ F |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Bumbuna hydroelectric station was built to deliver about **50 MW**: $50 \times 10^{6} = 5 \times 10^{7}$ W. A phone charger delivers about **10 W**. The station's output is roughly five million phone chargers running at once.
:::

## Standard form (scientific notation)

:::callout{type=definition}
A number is in **standard form** when it is written as $A \times 10^{n}$, where $1 \le A < 10$ and $n$ is a whole number.
:::

- $384\,000\,000\ \text{m} = 3.84 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$ (distance to the Moon)
- $0.000\,52\ \text{m} = 5.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}$

## Converting units without mistakes

Write the conversion as a multiplication by a fraction equal to 1, so the unwanted unit cancels.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Convert 72 km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹.**

$$72\ \dfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \dfrac{72\,000}{3600}\ \text{m s}^{-1} = 20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

Shortcut worth remembering: divide km h⁻¹ by **3.6** to get m s⁻¹.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A cube of side 2 cm has a mass of 21.6 g. Find its density in kg m⁻³.**

Volume $= (2\ \text{cm})^3 = 8\ \text{cm}^3 = 8 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3$ (because $1\ \text{cm}^3 = (10^{-2}\ \text{m})^3 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3$).

Mass $= 21.6\ \text{g} = 0.0216\ \text{kg}$.

$$\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{0.0216}{8 \times 10^{-6}} = 2700\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$$

That is the density of aluminium.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Converting **areas and volumes**: $1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm}$, but $1\ \text{m}^2 = 10\,000\ \text{cm}^2$ and $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$. Square or cube the conversion factor too.
:::

## Key points

- A physical quantity = **number × unit**; always write the unit.
- Seven SI base quantities: **length (m), mass (kg), time (s), current (A), temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), luminous intensity (cd)**.
- Derived units come from defining equations: N = kg m s⁻², J = kg m² s⁻², W = kg m² s⁻³, Pa = kg m⁻¹ s⁻².
- Prefixes: G $10^9$, M $10^6$, k $10^3$, c $10^{-2}$, m $10^{-3}$, μ $10^{-6}$, n $10^{-9}$, p $10^{-12}$.
- km h⁻¹ ÷ 3.6 = m s⁻¹.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Objective questions ask you to **pick the fundamental quantity** from a list, to **identify the unit of a derived quantity** (often in base units, e.g. "the unit of power is equivalent to kg m² s⁻³"), or to **convert** units. In theory papers, every final answer must carry the correct SI unit: a correct number with a wrong or missing unit usually loses the mark. Show the conversion line, especially for cm³ to m³ and km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹.
:::

## Quick check

1. Which of these is a fundamental quantity: force, weight, mass, pressure?
2. Express the watt in SI base units.
3. Convert 54 km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹.
4. Write 0.000 0067 m in standard form.

**Answers:** 1. Mass. 2. $\text{W} = \text{J s}^{-1} = \text{kg m}^2\ \text{s}^{-3}$. 3. $54 \div 3.6 = 15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. 4. $6.7 \times 10^{-6}$ m.
