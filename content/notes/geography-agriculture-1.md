The main **types of agriculture** are **subsistence farming**, where farmers grow food mainly for their own families, and **commercial farming**, where crops and animals are produced for sale. Within these, West Africa has several distinctive **farming systems**: **shifting cultivation**, **bush fallowing**, **mixed farming**, **plantation agriculture**, **peasant cash-crop farming**, **pastoral nomadism** and **irrigation farming**. This lesson describes each system, where it is practised, and its advantages and disadvantages.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish subsistence from commercial agriculture
- describe shifting cultivation and bush fallowing and explain their decline
- describe mixed farming and plantation agriculture
- describe peasant cash-crop farming and pastoral nomadism
- describe irrigation farming in West Africa
- give West African and Sierra Leone examples of each system

## Subsistence and commercial agriculture

| Subsistence agriculture | Commercial agriculture |
|---|---|
| produces mainly for the **family's own food** | produces for **sale** at home or abroad |
| small farms, often under 2 hectares | larger farms or plantations |
| simple tools: hoe, cutlass, axe | machines, fertilisers, improved seeds |
| family labour | hired labour |
| many crops grown together | often one main crop (**monoculture**) |
| low yields | higher yields |

## Farming systems of West Africa

### Shifting cultivation

The farmer clears a patch of forest or bush by **slash and burn**, farms it for 2–3 years until the soil is exhausted, then **moves to a new area**, allowing the old plot to return to forest. The settlement may also move.

- **Advantages:** needs little capital; ash adds nutrients; long fallow restores fertility; suits low population densities.
- **Disadvantages:** needs a lot of land; destroys forest; low yields; bush fires; cannot support a growing population.

### Bush fallowing (rotational bush fallow)

![The bush fallowing cycle: clear the bush, burn, plant crops for two or three years, soil exhausted, fallow while the bush regrows, then clear again](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/bush-fallow-cycle.svg)

*Figure 1. The bush fallowing cycle. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

The farmer stays in a **permanent village** but rotates the farm plots: a plot is cultivated for a few years and then left to **fallow** for several years before being cleared again. This is the main system in Sierra Leone.

As population grows, **fallow periods are shortening** (from 10–15 years in the past to 3–5 years in many places), so soils have less time to recover and yields fall.

### Mixed farming

Crops and animals are raised **together** on the same farm. Animals provide manure for crops; crop residues feed the animals. It spreads risk and keeps soil fertile, but needs more knowledge and capital. In northern Nigeria and Ghana, farmers combine grain crops with cattle, sheep and goats.

### Plantation agriculture

Large estates grow **one cash crop** (monoculture) for export, using much capital, hired labour, modern methods and processing on site.

**Examples:** **rubber** at Harbel, Liberia (Firestone, one of the world's largest rubber plantations); **oil palm** in Pujehun District, Sierra Leone; oil palm and bananas in Cameroon and Côte d'Ivoire.

- **Advantages:** high yields; export earnings; jobs; processing and infrastructure.
- **Disadvantages:** takes large areas of land from local people; dependence on one crop and world prices; foreign ownership; forest clearing.

### Peasant cash-crop farming

Small farmers grow **cash crops** for sale on their own small farms, often alongside food crops: **cocoa** in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire (together producing well over half of the world's cocoa), **cocoa and coffee** in eastern Sierra Leone, **groundnuts** in Senegal and The Gambia, **cotton** in Mali and northern Nigeria.

### Pastoral nomadism

Herders move with their **cattle, sheep, goats and camels** in search of water and pasture, following the seasons. The **Fulani** of the West African savanna and the Tuareg of the Sahara are pastoralists. Movement south in the dry season can lead to **conflicts with crop farmers**.

### Irrigation farming

Water is supplied to crops artificially from rivers, dams or wells, allowing farming in dry areas or in the dry season.

**Examples:** the **Office du Niger** scheme in Mali (rice and sugar cane on the inland delta of the Niger), irrigation schemes around **Kano** and **Sokoto** in Nigeria, and dry-season vegetable gardens in Sierra Leone's inland valley swamps.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Farming in Sierra Leone is mostly **small-scale bush fallowing** by smallholders. **Rice**, the staple food, is grown on **uplands** (rain-fed) and in **inland valley swamps**, **bolilands** and mangrove swamps. **Cassava**, groundnuts, sweet potatoes and vegetables are grown widely. Cash crops include **cocoa and coffee** in Kailahun, Kenema and Kono, **oil palm** across the south and east, and **cashew** in the north. Large commercial farms include oil palm plantations in Pujehun and sugar cane near Makeni.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Shifting cultivation** and **bush fallowing** are not the same. In shifting cultivation the farmer (and often the village) **moves** to new land; in bush fallowing the village is **permanent** and only the farm plots rotate.
:::

## Key points

- **Subsistence** (own food, small, simple tools) vs **commercial** (for sale, larger, modern inputs).
- **Shifting cultivation:** slash and burn, move on. **Bush fallowing:** permanent village, rotating plots with shortening fallows.
- **Mixed farming:** crops and animals together. **Plantations:** large, one export crop (rubber in Liberia, oil palm in Sierra Leone).
- **Peasant cash crops:** cocoa (Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire), groundnuts (Senegal). **Pastoral nomadism:** Fulani herders.
- **Irrigation:** Office du Niger (Mali), Kano and Sokoto (Nigeria).

## Quick check

1. State two differences between subsistence and commercial farming.
2. Why are fallow periods getting shorter in Sierra Leone?
3. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of plantation agriculture.
4. Which people practise pastoral nomadism in West Africa?
5. Name an irrigation scheme in West Africa.

**Answers:**

1. Any two: purpose (own food vs sale); farm size; tools and inputs; labour; number of crops; yields.
2. Because the growing population needs more land, so plots are farmed again before they have fully recovered.
3. Advantage: high yields, export earnings, jobs or infrastructure. Disadvantage: land taken from local people, monoculture risk, foreign ownership or forest clearing.
4. The Fulani (and the Tuareg in the Sahara).
5. The Office du Niger (Mali), or the Kano or Sokoto schemes (Nigeria).
