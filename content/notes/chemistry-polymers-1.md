Plastic buckets, water tanks, nylon fishing nets, polyester school shirts, rubber and even starch and proteins are all **polymers**: giant molecules built from thousands of small repeating units. This lesson explains the two ways in which small molecules join to make polymers: **addition** and **condensation** polymerisation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define monomer, polymer, polymerisation and degree of polymerisation
- describe addition polymerisation, with examples, equations and repeat units
- deduce the monomer from a polymer structure, and the polymer from a monomer
- describe condensation polymerisation, with nylon and Terylene as examples
- compare addition and condensation polymerisation
- name natural polymers and their monomers

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition}
A **monomer** is a small molecule that can join with many others like it. A **polymer** is a very large molecule (a macromolecule) made of many monomer units joined together. **Polymerisation** is the process of joining monomers into a polymer.
:::

The **degree of polymerisation, n,** is the number of monomer units in one polymer molecule:

$$n = \frac{M_r \text{ of polymer}}{M_r \text{ of monomer}}$$

## Addition polymerisation

![Ethene molecules join into a long chain of poly(ethene); a dicarboxylic acid and a diol join into a polyester, releasing water at every link](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/polymerisation.svg)

*Figure 1. Addition and condensation polymerisation. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
**Addition polymerisation** is the joining of many **unsaturated** monomers (containing **C=C**) to form a polymer **without the loss of any other molecule**. The polymer is the **only product**, and it has the same empirical formula as the monomer.
:::

The C=C double bonds open and the monomers link up into a saturated chain:

$$n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2 \rightarrow (\text{–CH}_2\text{–CH}_2\text{–})_n$$

The part in brackets is the **repeat unit**. Conditions for poly(ethene): high pressure (about 1,000–2,000 atm) and 200 °C with a trace of oxygen as initiator (**low-density** poly(ethene)), or low pressure with a **Ziegler–Natta catalyst** (**high-density** poly(ethene)).

| Monomer | Polymer | Repeat unit | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ethene**, CH₂=CH₂ | **Poly(ethene)**, polythene | –CH₂–CH₂– | Plastic bags, bottles, buckets, water tanks |
| **Propene**, CH₂=CHCH₃ | **Poly(propene)** | –CH₂–CH(CH₃)– | Ropes, crates, chairs, carpets |
| **Chloroethene**, CH₂=CHCl | **PVC**, poly(chloroethene) | –CH₂–CHCl– | Water pipes, cable insulation, raincoats |
| **Phenylethene** (styrene), CH₂=CHC₆H₅ | **Polystyrene** | –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)– | Packaging, food boxes, insulation |
| **Tetrafluoroethene**, CF₂=CF₂ | **PTFE** (Teflon) | –CF₂–CF₂– | Non-stick pans, plumber's tape |
| **Methyl 2-methylpropenoate** | **Perspex** | – | Safety glass, signs, car lights |

**Finding the monomer from a polymer:** take the two-carbon repeat unit and put a **double bond** between the two carbons. For example, –CH₂–CHCl– comes from CH₂=CHCl.

## Condensation polymerisation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Condensation polymerisation** is the joining of monomers, each with **two reactive functional groups**, with the **loss of a small molecule** (usually **water** or HCl) at every link.
:::

**Nylon (a polyamide):** a **diamine** reacts with a **dicarboxylic acid** (or its acid chloride). **Amide links**, –CO–NH–, the same as peptide bonds, join the units:

$$\text{dicarboxylic acid} + \text{diamine} \rightarrow \text{nylon} + \text{water}$$

With HOOC–R–COOH and H₂N–R′–NH₂ as the monomers, the repeat unit is **–OC–R–CO–NH–R′–NH–**, and two molecules of water are lost for each repeat unit.

Nylon 6,6 is made from hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane. Uses: ropes, **fishing nets**, clothing, tooth brushes, gears.

**Terylene (a polyester):** a **dicarboxylic acid** (benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid) reacts with a **diol** (ethane-1,2-diol). **Ester links**, –CO–O–, join the units. Uses: clothing fibres (often mixed with cotton), bottles (PET), sails.

| Feature | Addition polymerisation | Condensation polymerisation |
|---|---|---|
| **Monomers** | Unsaturated (C=C) | Two functional groups each (–COOH, –OH, –NH₂) |
| **By-product** | **None** | Small molecule lost (**H₂O** or HCl) |
| **Empirical formula** | Same as the monomer | Different from the monomers |
| **Links in chain** | C–C bonds | Ester or amide links |
| **Examples** | Poly(ethene), PVC, polystyrene | Nylon, Terylene, proteins, starch |

## Natural polymers

| Natural polymer | Monomer | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Starch, cellulose, glycogen** | Glucose | Condensation |
| **Proteins** (wool, silk, enzymes) | Amino acids | Condensation (peptide links) |
| **Natural rubber** | Isoprene (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene) | Addition |
| **DNA** | Nucleotides | Condensation |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Degree of polymerisation.** A sample of PVC has a relative molecular mass of 62,500. How many monomer units does each chain contain? (C = 12, H = 1, Cl = 35.5)

- Mᵣ of CH₂=CHCl $= 24 + 3 + 35.5 = 62.5$
- $n = 62{,}500 \div 62.5 = 1{,}000$
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Fishing communities from Tombo to Shenge use **nylon nets and ropes**. Nylon, a condensation polymer, is strong, light and does not rot in sea water the way older cotton or raffia nets did. That durability has a downside: lost "ghost nets" keep trapping fish and turtles for years.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In addition polymerisation, **no small molecule is lost**. If an equation shows water forming, the polymerisation is **condensation**, not addition.
:::

## Key points

- **Monomers** join to form **polymers**; n = Mᵣ(polymer) ÷ Mᵣ(monomer).
- **Addition:** C=C monomers join with **no by-product**: ethene → poly(ethene), propene → poly(propene), chloroethene → **PVC**, styrene → polystyrene.
- **Condensation:** monomers with two functional groups join, **losing water** at each link: **nylon** (amide links), **Terylene** (ester links).
- Natural polymers: **starch, cellulose** (glucose), **proteins** (amino acids), **rubber** (isoprene).

## Quick check

1. What is the monomer of PVC?
2. State one difference between addition and condensation polymerisation.
3. Name the link that joins the units in nylon.
4. A poly(ethene) sample has Mᵣ = 56,000. Find n. (C = 12, H = 1)

**Answers:**

1. Chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl.
2. Addition polymerisation gives no by-product; condensation polymerisation loses a small molecule such as water at each link (or: addition monomers have C=C, condensation monomers have two functional groups).
3. The amide (peptide) link, –CO–NH–.
4. Mᵣ of C₂H₄ = 28; n = 56,000 ÷ 28 = 2,000.
