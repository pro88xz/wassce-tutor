## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define demand and effective demand
- draw a demand schedule and a demand curve
- state the law of demand and explain why the demand curve slopes downwards

## What is demand?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are **willing and able to buy** at a given price, over a given period of time.
:::

Three ideas are packed into this definition:

1. **Willing and able**: wanting something is not enough. A pupil who would love a laptop but has no money for it does not *demand* it in the economic sense.
2. **At a given price**: demand is always linked to a price.
3. **Over a period of time**: per day, per week or per month.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Effective demand** is the desire for a good **backed by the ability to pay** for it. In economics, "demand" always means effective demand.
:::

## The demand schedule

:::callout{type=definition}
A **demand schedule** is a table showing the quantities of a good demanded at different prices.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Weekly demand for bunches of plantain at a market in Kenema**

| Price per bunch (NLe) | Quantity demanded (bunches) |
|---|---|
| 30 | 0 |
| 25 | 20 |
| 20 | 40 |
| 15 | 60 |
| 10 | 80 |
| 5 | 100 |

As the price falls, more bunches are demanded.
:::

An **individual demand schedule** shows one buyer's demand. The **market demand schedule** adds up the quantities demanded by all buyers at each price.

## The demand curve

Plot the schedule on a graph with **price on the vertical axis** and **quantity demanded on the horizontal axis**, then join the points. The result is the **demand curve**, labelled **D**. It slopes **downwards from left to right**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In economics, **price always goes on the vertical (y) axis** and quantity on the horizontal (x) axis, even though price is the cause. Swapping the axes is a common way to lose marks.
:::

## The law of demand

:::callout{type=definition}
**The law of demand** states that, **other things being equal**, the **higher the price** of a good, the **lower the quantity demanded**; and the **lower the price**, the **higher the quantity demanded**.
:::

Price and quantity demanded move in **opposite directions**: an **inverse** relationship.

### Why the demand curve slopes downwards

1. **The income effect.** When the price of a good falls, the buyer's money goes further: their real income rises, so they can buy more.
2. **The substitution effect.** When the price of a good falls, it becomes cheaper compared with its substitutes, so buyers switch to it. If plantain becomes cheaper, some families buy plantain instead of yam.
3. **Diminishing marginal utility.** Each extra unit of a good gives less satisfaction than the one before, so consumers will buy more only at a lower price.
4. **New buyers.** A lower price brings in people who could not afford the good before.
5. **Different uses.** At a lower price a good may be used for more purposes; cheap palm oil may be used for soap-making as well as cooking.

:::callout{type=local-example}
When mangoes are in season and the price falls, families buy more of them, children eat them as snacks, and some traders buy them to make juice. When the season ends and prices rise, people buy far fewer.
:::

## Key points

- **Demand** = the quantity consumers are **willing and able** to buy at a given price, over a period.
- A **demand schedule** is a table; a **demand curve** is its graph, sloping downwards.
- **The law of demand**: price up, quantity demanded down (other things being equal).
- The curve slopes down because of the **income effect**, the **substitution effect** and **diminishing marginal utility**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
You may be asked to **define demand**, **state the law of demand**, **draw a demand curve from a schedule** and **explain why it slopes downwards**. Label the axes (*Price (NLe)*, *Quantity demanded*), plot accurately, label the curve **D**, and always include *other things being equal* when you state the law.
:::

## Quick check

1. Why does a pupil who wants a car but has no money not "demand" one?
2. Using the schedule above, how many bunches are demanded at NLe 15?
3. State the law of demand.

**Answers:** 1. Because demand must be backed by the ability to pay (effective demand). 2. 60 bunches. 3. Other things being equal, the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded, and the lower the price, the higher the quantity demanded.
