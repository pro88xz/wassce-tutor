A country's output can grow while most of its people stay poor, without clean water, good schools or jobs. Economists therefore separate economic growth from economic development. This lesson explains the difference, how development is measured, the features of developing countries, and the trap that keeps many of them poor.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish economic growth from economic development
- explain the indicators used to measure development, including the Human Development Index
- describe the characteristics of developing countries
- explain the vicious circle of poverty and how it can be broken
- explain the obstacles to development in West Africa

## Growth and development

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Economic growth** is a **sustained increase in a country's real output** (real GDP or real GDP per head) over time. It is a **quantitative** change.
- **Economic development** is a **broad improvement in the living standards and welfare** of the people: higher real incomes **plus** better health, education, housing and freedom, less poverty and inequality, and changes in the structure of the economy. It is a **qualitative** as well as quantitative change.
:::

| Economic growth | Economic development |
|---|---|
| Increase in real output | Improvement in welfare and quality of life |
| Measured by real GDP growth | Measured by many indicators (HDI, poverty, literacy, life expectancy) |
| Can happen without development (for example a mining boom whose profits go abroad) | Usually needs growth, but also fair distribution and better services |
| Quantitative | Qualitative and quantitative |

## Measuring development

1. **Real GDP per head**, adjusted for prices.
2. **Life expectancy** at birth.
3. **Infant and maternal mortality** rates.
4. **Literacy rate** and school enrolment.
5. **Access to clean water, sanitation and electricity**.
6. **The share of the population in poverty**.
7. **The structure of employment**: the share in agriculture versus industry and services.
8. **Calorie intake**, doctors per head and housing quality.

### The Human Development Index (HDI)

The **United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)** publishes the **HDI**, which combines three dimensions:

| Dimension | Measured by |
|---|---|
| **A long and healthy life** | Life expectancy at birth |
| **Knowledge** | Mean and expected years of schooling |
| **A decent standard of living** | Gross national income per head (adjusted for prices) |

The HDI runs from 0 to 1. Countries are grouped as very high, high, medium or low human development. Sierra Leone is in the **low** human development group, though its score has risen since the end of the civil war.

## Characteristics of developing countries

1. **Low income per head** and widespread poverty.
2. **Dependence on agriculture**, much of it subsistence, with low productivity.
3. **Dependence on a few primary exports** (minerals, cash crops).
4. **Rapid population growth** and a high dependency ratio.
5. **High unemployment and underemployment**.
6. **Low levels of saving and investment**; shortage of capital.
7. **Poor infrastructure**: roads, power, water, communications.
8. **Low levels of education and health**: high illiteracy, high infant mortality.
9. **Unequal distribution of income** and a dual economy (a small modern sector beside a large traditional one).
10. **Weak technology** and a shortage of skilled workers.
11. **Heavy foreign debt** and dependence on aid.

## The vicious circle of poverty

![A circle of five linked steps: low income leads to low saving, low investment, little capital, low productivity and back to low income](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/vicious-circle.svg)

*Figure 1. The vicious circle of poverty. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Because incomes are **low**, people can **save** little. Low saving means low **investment**, so the economy has **little capital** (machines, roads, power). With little capital, **productivity** stays low, and so incomes stay low. The circle repeats.

A similar circle runs through **demand**: low incomes mean small markets, so firms have little reason to invest, which keeps productivity and incomes low.

**Breaking the circle:**

1. **Foreign investment and aid** bring capital from outside.
2. **Raising saving** through banks, cooperatives and mobile money.
3. **Education and training**, which raise productivity directly.
4. **Better infrastructure**, which makes all investment more productive.
5. **Land and agricultural reform**, raising farm output.
6. **Regional integration**, which widens markets.

## Obstacles to development in West Africa

| Obstacle | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Shortage of capital** | Low saving, weak banks and capital flight |
| **Rapid population growth** | Gains in output are shared among more people |
| **Poor infrastructure** | Unreliable power and bad roads raise costs |
| **Low human capital** | Illiteracy, poor health and the emigration of skilled people (brain drain) |
| **Political instability and corruption** | Discourage investment and waste public funds |
| **Dependence on primary exports** | Earnings fluctuate with world prices |
| **Heavy debt** | Repayments take money from development |
| **Weak institutions** | Poor property rights, slow courts, red tape |
| **Disease and disasters** | Malaria, Ebola, floods |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's mining output has at times made GDP grow fast, for example when large iron-ore exports began in the early 2010s. But because mining employs relatively few people and much of the profit goes abroad, rapid **growth** did not bring equally rapid **development** for most households. This is a classic example of growth without development.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Economic growth" and "economic development" are not interchangeable. A question asking you to "distinguish" them wants the **difference**: growth is a rise in output; development is a rise in welfare.
:::

## Key points

- **Growth** is a rise in real output; **development** is a broad rise in welfare and quality of life.
- Development is measured by **income per head, life expectancy, literacy, poverty and the HDI**.
- Developing countries have **low incomes, dependence on agriculture and primary exports, rapid population growth and low capital**.
- The **vicious circle of poverty**: low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income.
- Obstacles include **capital shortage, poor infrastructure, low human capital, instability and debt**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE often asks you to **distinguish economic growth from economic development**, to **state the characteristics of developing countries**, to **explain the vicious circle of poverty**, and to discuss **obstacles to development and how to overcome them**. Draw or describe the circle step by step, and suggest a way to break it.
:::

3. Why does low income lead to low investment in a poor country?
