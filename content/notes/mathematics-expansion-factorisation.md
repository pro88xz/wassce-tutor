Expanding and factorising are opposite skills. **Expanding** removes brackets: $(x + 3)(x - 2)$ becomes $x^2 + x - 6$. **Factorising** puts them back: $x^2 + x - 6$ becomes $(x + 3)(x - 2)$. Factorisation is the key to solving quadratic equations, simplifying algebraic fractions and much more, and WASSCE tests it in almost every paper. This lesson teaches both directions, the three special products you must know by sight, and the four methods of factorising.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- expand the product of two brackets
- recognise and use the special products $(a + b)^2$, $(a - b)^2$ and $(a + b)(a - b)$
- factorise by common factor, by grouping, as a difference of two squares, and as a quadratic trinomial
- check a factorisation by expanding

## Expanding two brackets

Multiply **each** term in the first bracket by **each** term in the second. A reliable way is **FOIL**: **F**irst, **O**uter, **I**nner, **L**ast.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Expand $(x + 3)(x - 2)$.**

| F | O | I | L |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x \cdot x = x^2$ | $x \cdot (-2) = -2x$ | $3 \cdot x = 3x$ | $3 \cdot (-2) = -6$ |

$$x^2 - 2x + 3x - 6 = x^2 + x - 6$$

**Expand $(2a - 5)(3a + 4)$.**

$$6a^2 + 8a - 15a - 20 = 6a^2 - 7a - 20$$
:::

## Three special products

:::callout{type=definition}
| Product | Expansion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $(a + b)^2$ | $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ | $(x + 5)^2 = x^2 + 10x + 25$ |
| $(a - b)^2$ | $a^2 - 2ab + b^2$ | $(3y - 2)^2 = 9y^2 - 12y + 4$ |
| $(a + b)(a - b)$ | $a^2 - b^2$ | $(x + 7)(x - 7) = x^2 - 49$ |
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**$(x + 5)^2$ is NOT $x^2 + 25$.** Squaring a bracket means multiplying it by itself: $(x + 5)(x + 5)$, which gives the middle term $10x$. Forgetting the middle term is one of the most common errors in the whole examination.
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
The difference of two squares makes some arithmetic easy: $103 \times 97 = (100 + 3)(100 - 3) = 100^2 - 3^2 = 10\,000 - 9 = 9991$. And $67^2 - 33^2 = (67 + 33)(67 - 33) = 100 \times 34 = 3400$. WASSCE objective questions use this trick often.
:::

## Factorising: four methods

### 1. Common factor

Take out the **highest common factor (HCF)** of all the terms.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
- $6x + 15 = 3(2x + 5)$
- $4a^2 - 10ab = 2a(2a - 5b)$
- $9x^3y + 12x^2y^2 = 3x^2y(3x + 4y)$
:::

### 2. Grouping (four terms)

Group the terms in pairs, factorise each pair, then take out the common bracket.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Factorise $ax + ay + bx + by$.**

$$a(x + y) + b(x + y) = (x + y)(a + b)$$

**Factorise $6pq - 9p + 4q - 6$.**

$$3p(2q - 3) + 2(2q - 3) = (2q - 3)(3p + 2)$$
:::

### 3. Difference of two squares

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
- $x^2 - 16 = (x + 4)(x - 4)$
- $9m^2 - 25n^2 = (3m + 5n)(3m - 5n)$
- $2x^2 - 50 = 2(x^2 - 25) = 2(x + 5)(x - 5)$ (common factor first)
:::

### 4. Quadratic trinomials: $ax^2 + bx + c$

**When $a = 1$:** find two numbers that **multiply** to $c$ and **add** to $b$.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Factorise $x^2 + 7x + 12$.** Numbers that multiply to $12$ and add to $7$: $3$ and $4$.

$$x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$$

**Factorise $x^2 - 2x - 15$.** Multiply to $-15$, add to $-2$: $-5$ and $3$.

$$x^2 - 2x - 15 = (x - 5)(x + 3)$$
:::

**When $a \neq 1$:** the **ac method**. Find two numbers that multiply to $a \times c$ and add to $b$; split the middle term; then group.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Factorise $6x^2 + 7x - 3$.**

1. $a \times c = 6 \times (-3) = -18$. Two numbers that multiply to $-18$ and add to $7$: $9$ and $-2$.
2. Split the middle term: $6x^2 + 9x - 2x - 3$.
3. Group: $3x(2x + 3) - 1(2x + 3)$.
4. **Answer: $(2x + 3)(3x - 1)$.**

**Check by expanding:** $6x^2 - 2x + 9x - 3 = 6x^2 + 7x - 3$ ✓
:::

### Signs: a quick guide

| Signs in $x^2 \;?\; bx \;?\; c$ | The two numbers are |
|---|---|
| $+b$, $+c$ | both positive |
| $-b$, $+c$ | both negative |
| $\pm b$, $-c$ | one positive, one negative (the larger takes the sign of $b$) |

## A strategy for any factorisation

1. **Common factor?** Always take it out first.
2. **Two terms?** Try the difference of two squares.
3. **Three terms?** Try a quadratic trinomial.
4. **Four terms?** Try grouping.
5. **Check** by expanding.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Factorising also helps with mental arithmetic in the market. If a trader sells 47 tins at NLe 35 and 53 tins at NLe 35, the takings are $35(47 + 53) = 35 \times 100 = $ **NLe 3500**, which is the common factor method in everyday life.
:::

$$6x^2 + 15x - 8x - 20 = 6x^2 + 7x - 20$$
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Expand: (2y − 3)²"}
$$4y^2 - 12y + 9$$
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Factorise completely: 3x² − 27"}
$$3(x^2 - 9) = 3(x + 3)(x - 3)$$
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Factorise: x² − 9x + 20"}
Multiply to $20$, add to $-9$: $-4$ and $-5$.

$$(x - 4)(x - 5)$$
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Factorise: 2x² − 5x − 12"}
$ac = -24$; numbers that add to $-5$: $-8$ and $3$.

$$2x^2 - 8x + 3x - 12 = 2x(x - 4) + 3(x - 4) = (x - 4)(2x + 3)$$
:::

:::reveal{title="6. Evaluate without a calculator: 7.5² − 2.5²"}
$$(7.5 + 2.5)(7.5 - 2.5) = 10 \times 5 = 50$$
:::

:::reveal{title="7. Factorise: xy − 3x + 2y − 6"}
$$x(y - 3) + 2(y - 3) = (y - 3)(x + 2)$$
:::

## Key points

- **Expand** two brackets by multiplying every term by every term (FOIL).
- Know by sight: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$, $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$.
- **Factorise**: common factor first, then difference of two squares, trinomial, or grouping.
- For $ax^2 + bx + c$, find two numbers that multiply to $ac$ and add to $b$.
- Always **check** by expanding.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Factorisation appears in objective questions (*"Factorise completely…"*, *"Evaluate $83^2 - 17^2$"*) and as the first step of many theory questions on quadratic equations and algebraic fractions. The marks are lost by forgetting the common factor, by sign errors in trinomials, and by writing $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$.
:::
