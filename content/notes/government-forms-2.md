Nigeria, Sierra Leone's ECOWAS neighbour, has thirty-six states, each with its own governor, house of assembly and courts, yet one federal government in Abuja. Its constitution shares out power between the two levels. This lesson explains federalism, why large and diverse countries choose it, how it compares with a confederation, and the strengths and weaknesses of each.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define federal government and state its features
- explain how a federal constitution divides power
- state the reasons for adopting federalism, and its merits and demerits
- define confederation and state its features, merits and demerits
- compare unitary, federal and confederal systems

## Federal government

:::callout{type=definition}
A **federal government** is one in which the constitution divides governmental powers between a central (federal) government and the governments of the component units (states or regions), so that each level is supreme in its own sphere.
:::

![The constitution divides power into an exclusive list for the federal government, a concurrent list shared by both, and residual matters for the states](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/federal-lists.svg)

*Figure 1. A federal constitution shares out power in lists. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

### Features of federalism

1. **Division of powers** by the constitution into **exclusive**, **concurrent** and **residual** lists.
2. **Written and rigid constitution**, so that neither level can change the division alone.
3. **Supremacy of the constitution** over both levels of government.
4. **Two levels of government**, each with its own executive and legislature.
5. **An independent supreme court** to settle disputes between the levels (judicial review).
6. **Bicameral federal legislature**, with one house giving equal representation to the states (for example, the Nigerian Senate has three senators per state plus one for the Federal Capital Territory).

### Reasons for adopting federalism

- **Large territory** that is hard to govern from one centre
- **Ethnic, religious and cultural diversity**, so that each group can manage its own affairs
- **Fear of domination** of minorities by the majority
- **Desire to unite** separate units while keeping some independence
- **Economic advantage** of a larger common market and pooled resources
- **Colonial history**, as in Nigeria's regions under British rule

### Merits

1. **Accommodates diversity** and protects minorities.
2. **Brings government closer to the people**, encouraging local participation.
3. **Prevents dictatorship** because power is divided.
4. **Encourages development** through competition between states.
5. **Reduces the workload** of the central government.

### Demerits

1. **Expensive:** many governments, legislatures and officials to pay for.
2. **Conflicts** between federal and state governments over powers and revenue.
3. **Duplication** of functions and delay in decisions.
4. **Unequal development** between rich and poor states.
5. **Risk of secession**, as in the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970) over Biafra.

## Confederation

:::callout{type=definition}
A **confederation** is a loose union of sovereign states that agree to give a central authority limited powers, usually over defence, foreign affairs or trade, while each member keeps its sovereignty and may leave the union.
:::

**Features:** member states remain sovereign; the central body is weak and depends on the members for money and troops; decisions often need unanimity; citizens owe loyalty mainly to their own state; members can withdraw (secede).

**Merits:** allows cooperation without loss of sovereignty; members keep their identity; easy to form and to leave.

**Demerits:** weak and unstable central authority; decisions are slow; members can block action; confederations often collapse or become federations.

**Example:** the **Senegambia Confederation** (1982–1989) linked Senegal and The Gambia, with a common defence and a confederal parliament, but it was dissolved in 1989 when the members could not agree on closer union.

## Comparing the three systems

| Feature | Unitary | Federal | Confederal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where sovereignty lies | Central government | Shared, under the constitution | Member states |
| Power of the centre | Strong; delegates to local bodies | Shared with the states | Weak; given by members |
| Constitution | May be flexible | Written and rigid | A treaty or agreement |
| Right to secede | No | No | Yes |
| Examples | Sierra Leone, Ghana | Nigeria, USA | Senegambia (1982–1989) |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Exclusive, concurrent or residual?** In Nigeria:

- **Defence and currency** are on the **exclusive** list: only the federal government acts.
- **Education and health** are on the **concurrent** list: both may legislate; if their laws conflict, federal law prevails.
- **Chieftaincy matters** are **residual**: not listed, so they belong to the states.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone has never been federal, but it belongs to the **Mano River Union** (with Liberia, Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire) and to **ECOWAS**. These are not confederations: they are international organisations for cooperation, and members keep full sovereignty. Learning the differences helps in questions on regional integration later in the course.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In a confederation, sovereignty stays with the **member states**, not the centre. In a federation it is **shared** under the constitution. Students often mix these up.
:::

## Key points

- **Federalism** divides power by a **written, rigid constitution** into **exclusive, concurrent and residual** lists, with a **supreme court** as umpire.
- Adopted for **large size, diversity, protection of minorities and unity**; drawbacks include **cost, conflict, duplication and secession**.
- A **confederation** is a loose union of **sovereign** states with a **weak centre** and the **right to secede**: the **Senegambia Confederation (1982–1989)**.

## Quick check

1. What is the concurrent list in a federation?
2. Give two reasons why a country may adopt federalism.
3. State two features of a confederation.
4. Which two countries formed the Senegambia Confederation?

**Answers:**

1. The list of subjects on which both the federal and state governments may make laws; federal law prevails if they conflict.
2. Any two: large territory; ethnic and cultural diversity; protection of minorities; desire to unite separate units; economic advantages; colonial history.
3. Any two: member states remain sovereign; the central authority is weak; decisions often require unanimity; members may secede; the centre depends on members for funds.
4. Senegal and The Gambia.
