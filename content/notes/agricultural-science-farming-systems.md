Drive from Freetown to Kailahun and you pass many kinds of farming: patches of upland rice cut from the bush, vegetable gardens in valley bottoms, villages with goats and chickens beside cassava fields, and large oil palm plantations. Each is a different **farming system**, a different way of organising land, crops, animals and labour. Choosing the right system decides how much a farm produces and whether its soil stays fertile.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a farming system
- describe the main farming systems: shifting cultivation, bush fallow, continuous cropping, mixed farming, mixed cropping, crop rotation, plantation farming and others
- state the advantages and disadvantages of each system
- explain which systems suit Sierra Leone and why

## What is a farming system?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **farming system** is the way a farmer organises the use of land, labour, capital, crops and animals to produce food and raw materials.
:::

## The main farming systems

### Shifting cultivation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Shifting cultivation** is a farming system in which a farmer clears a piece of land, cultivates it for a few years until the soil loses its fertility, then **abandons** it and moves to clear new land, often moving the homestead too.
:::

- **Advantages:** needs little capital; the bush regains fertility while the land rests; few pests build up.
- **Disadvantages:** needs a lot of land; destroys forests; yields are low; cannot support a growing population.

### Bush fallow (land rotation)

:::callout{type=definition}
**Bush fallow** is a farming system in which farmers cultivate land for a few years and then leave it to rest under natural bush (fallow) for some years, while they farm other land near the same **permanent village**.
:::

Bush fallow differs from shifting cultivation because the **village stays put**; only the farm plots move in rotation. As population grows, fallow periods get shorter and the soil has less time to recover.

### Continuous cropping

The same land is cultivated **every year** without a fallow period. Fertility must be kept up with manure, fertiliser and good soil management. It is common near towns and where land is scarce.

### Mixed cropping

:::callout{type=definition}
**Mixed cropping** is growing **two or more crops together** on the same piece of land in the same season.
:::

- **Advantages:** insurance against the failure of one crop; better use of land and labour; legumes add nitrogen; cover reduces erosion and weeds.
- **Disadvantages:** hard to mechanise; crops compete; fertiliser and spraying are harder to apply.

Example: rice mixed with cassava, maize, pepper and okra on an upland farm.

### Crop rotation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Crop rotation** is growing **different crops in succession** on the same piece of land in a planned order over a number of seasons.
:::

A legume (such as groundnut) is often followed by a heavy feeder (such as maize), and deep-rooted crops follow shallow-rooted ones. Rotation keeps the soil fertile, breaks the life cycles of pests and diseases, and controls weeds.

### Mixed farming

:::callout{type=definition}
**Mixed farming** is a farming system in which **crops are grown and animals are reared on the same farm**, each supporting the other.
:::

- **Advantages:** animal manure fertilises crops; crop residues feed animals; two sources of income spread the risk; work is spread through the year.
- **Disadvantages:** needs more capital, skill and management; animals may damage crops if not controlled.

### Plantation farming

:::callout{type=definition}
**Plantation farming** is the large-scale growing of a single **perennial cash crop**, such as oil palm, rubber, cocoa or sugar cane, usually by a company using hired labour and modern methods.
:::

- **Advantages:** high output; export earnings; employment; processing on site.
- **Disadvantages:** needs large areas and capital; monoculture spreads pests and diseases; can displace local farmers.

### Other systems

- **Monocropping (monoculture):** growing one crop on the same land year after year.
- **Pastoral farming (nomadism):** herding animals from place to place in search of pasture and water.
- **Ley farming:** alternating crops with grass or legume pasture for grazing on the same land.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Mixed farming** and **mixed cropping** are not the same. Mixed farming combines **crops and animals**; mixed cropping combines **two or more crops** on the same land at the same time.
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
Most farmers in Sierra Leone practise **bush fallow** with **mixed cropping**: upland rice planted with cassava, maize, pepper and sesame, and the land left to rest after a year or two. As population grows and fallows shorten, soils become poorer, which is why **crop rotation**, **inland valley swamp rice** and **mixed farming** with manure are being encouraged. **Plantations** of oil palm and sugar cane operate in the south and north.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Choosing a system.** A farmer near Makeni has 3 acres, a few goats and limited money. Which system suits her?

**Mixed farming with crop rotation**: goat manure keeps her small area fertile without costly fertiliser, crop residues feed the goats, rotating groundnut, maize and cassava keeps the soil healthy and limits pests, and two sources of income spread her risk. Shifting cultivation would not work: she has too little land.
:::

## Key points

- A **farming system** is how land, labour, capital, crops and animals are organised.
- **Shifting cultivation** moves the farm and homestead; **bush fallow** rotates plots around a permanent village.
- **Mixed cropping** = several crops together; **crop rotation** = crops in succession; **mixed farming** = crops plus animals.
- **Plantations** grow one perennial cash crop on a large scale.
- Bush fallow with mixed cropping dominates in Sierra Leone; shorter fallows make rotation, swamp rice and mixed farming more important.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE asks you to **define and describe farming systems**, to **state their advantages and disadvantages**, and to **distinguish** between similar systems: shifting cultivation and bush fallow, mixed farming and mixed cropping. A short definition followed by two advantages and two disadvantages for each system earns full marks.
:::
