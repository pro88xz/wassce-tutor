:::callout{type=definition}
The **cash book** is the book in which a business records all its **cash and bank transactions**: money received on the **debit (left) side** and money paid on the **credit (right) side**.
:::

It is both a **book of original entry** and a **ledger account**, because it takes the place of the cash account and the bank account. There are three main types: the **single-column cash book** (cash only), the **two-column (double-column) cash book** (cash and bank) and the **three-column cash book** (discount, cash and bank). This lesson explains the cash book and works through a two-column cash book step by step.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and purposes of the cash book
- explain why the cash book is both a book of original entry and a ledger account
- describe the single-column, two-column and three-column cash books
- prepare a two-column cash book with balances
- explain the meaning of a bank overdraft in the cash book

## Purposes of the cash book

1. To record **all cash and bank receipts and payments** in date order.
2. To show the **cash in hand** and the **balance at bank** at any time.
3. To act as the **cash and bank accounts** in the ledger, saving separate accounts.
4. To record **cash discounts** (in the three-column cash book).
5. To help **control cash** and detect theft or errors.

## Types of cash book

| Type | Money columns on each side | Records |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-column** | Cash | cash only (used by very small businesses) |
| **Two-column (double-column)** | Cash, Bank | cash and bank transactions |
| **Three-column** | Discount, Cash, Bank | cash, bank and cash discounts |
| **Analytical (columnar)** | several analysis columns | receipts and payments analysed by type |

## The two-column cash book

The cash book follows the double-entry rules for assets:

- **Debit side:** money **coming in**: balance brought down, cash sales, receipts from customers, capital introduced, loans received.
- **Credit side:** money **going out**: payments to suppliers, expenses, drawings, assets bought, balance carried down.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Record these transactions of Bockarie Traders for June 2026 in a two-column cash book and balance it (NLe).**

- 1 June: cash in hand 3,000; cash at bank 18,000.
- 4 June: cash sales 7,500.
- 6 June: paid rent by cheque 4,000.
- 10 June: received a cheque from Kamara 5,000, paid into the bank.
- 14 June: paid wages in cash 2,800.
- 18 June: paid cash into the bank 4,000 (contra).
- 23 June: paid Sesay by cheque 9,500.
- 27 June: owner took cash for private use 1,000.

**Debit (receipts)**

| Jun | Details | Cash | Bank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Balances b/d | 3,000 | 18,000 |
| 4 | Sales | 7,500 | |
| 10 | Kamara | | 5,000 |
| 18 | Cash (contra) | | 4,000 |
| | **Totals** | **10,500** | **27,000** |
| Jul 1 | Balances b/d | 2,700 | 13,500 |

**Credit (payments)**

| Jun | Details | Cash | Bank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Rent | | 4,000 |
| 14 | Wages | 2,800 | |
| 18 | Bank (contra) | 4,000 | |

## Contra entries

A **contra entry** is a transaction where **both sides are in the cash book**:

- **Cash paid into the bank:** debit the **bank** column, credit the **cash** column.
- **Cash withdrawn from the bank** for office use: debit the **cash** column, credit the **bank** column.

Each side is marked **"c"** (contra), and nothing is posted to the ledger, because both entries are already in the cash book.

## Bank overdraft

The **cash** column can never have a credit balance: you cannot pay out more cash than you have. But the **bank** column can, if the bank allows an **overdraft**. A credit balance in the bank column means the business **owes the bank**: it is a **current liability**, shown as "balance c/d" on the **debit** side and brought down on the **credit** side.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
When a cheque is received and **paid into the bank** on the same day, enter it in the **bank** column, not the cash column. Only notes and coins kept in the office go in the cash column.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Two-column cash book**: a cash book with cash and bank columns on each side.
- **Three-column cash book**: a cash book with discount, cash and bank columns on each side.
- **Contra entry**: an entry for cash paid into the bank or withdrawn from it, which appears on both sides of the cash book, is marked "c" and is not posted.
:::

## Key points

- The cash book records **all cash and bank** transactions: **receipts debit, payments credit**.
- It is both a **book of original entry** and a **ledger account**.
- Types: **single-column, two-column, three-column, analytical**.
- **Contra entries** (cash to bank or bank to cash) appear on both sides, marked "c", and are not posted.
- A **credit balance** on the bank column is an **overdraft** (a liability); the cash column cannot be overdrawn.

## Quick check

1. Why is the cash book both a book of original entry and a ledger?
2. On which side are receipts recorded?
3. What is a contra entry? Give an example.
4. Why can the cash column never have a credit balance?
5. What does a credit balance on the bank column mean?

**Answers:**

1. Transactions are first recorded in it from source documents, and it also takes the place of the cash and bank accounts in the ledger.
2. The debit side.
3. A transaction in which both the debit and the credit are in the cash book, such as cash paid into the bank.
4. Because a business cannot pay out more cash than it actually holds.
5. A bank overdraft: the business owes the bank money.
