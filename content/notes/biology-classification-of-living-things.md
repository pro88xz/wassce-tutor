# Classification of living things: groups, features and scientific naming

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain why organisms are classified; distinguish major taxonomic ranks; describe broad features used to group organisms; and apply the rules of binomial nomenclature.

## Why classification is necessary
Classification is the systematic grouping of organisms according to shared characteristics and relationships. It makes the enormous diversity of life easier to study, identify and communicate. A reliable system also helps biologists compare organisms and investigate evolutionary relationships. Similar appearance alone is not always sufficient: unrelated organisms may develop similar adaptations because they live in similar environments.

Taxonomy is the science of identifying, naming and classifying organisms. Identification uses observable or measured characteristics to determine an organism's group. A classification system should use consistent criteria and be revised when new evidence, including molecular evidence, becomes available.

## Taxonomic hierarchy
The main ranks commonly taught are kingdom, phylum (called division in many plant classifications), class, order, family, genus and species. Each lower rank contains organisms with more characteristics in common. A species is commonly defined as a group of organisms that can interbreed naturally to produce fertile offspring, although this definition does not apply equally well to every organism, such as organisms that reproduce asexually.

An organism's scientific name uses binomial nomenclature: two words, the genus name followed by the specific epithet. For example, humans are *Homo sapiens*. The genus begins with a capital letter and the specific epithet with a lower-case letter. Both words are italicised when printed; when handwritten, they are underlined separately. Scientific names reduce confusion caused by different local names.

## Broad groups and distinguishing features
A traditional school-level grouping recognises major kingdoms such as animals, plants, fungi, protoctists and prokaryotes. Plants are generally multicellular eukaryotes with cellulose cell walls; many photosynthesise using chlorophyll. Animals are multicellular eukaryotes that obtain nutrients by ingestion and lack cell walls. Fungi obtain nutrients by absorption, commonly have chitin-containing cell walls and often grow as hyphae. Many protoctists are single-celled eukaryotes, although the group is diverse. Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus; bacteria are examples.

These descriptions are broad guides, not substitutes for examining a particular organism. Classification schemes have changed as evidence has improved, so follow the terminology specified by the question or syllabus being studied.

## Using observable characteristics
Useful features include cell type, number of cells, cell-wall composition, mode of nutrition, body organisation, reproduction and genetic similarities. A dichotomous key identifies an organism through a sequence of paired choices. Each choice should be based on a clear feature that can be observed reliably. Avoid vague choices such as “large” unless a measurable boundary is provided.

## WASSCE examination focus
Practise arranging taxonomic ranks from broadest to most specific, writing scientific names correctly, and comparing groups using paired features. If asked to construct a key, use mutually exclusive statements and ensure every organism can be followed to one identification.

## Check your understanding
1. State two reasons for classifying organisms.
2. Arrange genus, family, kingdom and species from broadest to most specific.
3. Write the scientific name *Homo sapiens* correctly in a sentence.
4. Give two differences between plants and fungi.
5. What is a dichotomous key?

## Answers for self-assessment
1. It organises biodiversity and supports identification, comparison and communication.
2. Kingdom, family, genus, species.
3. *Homo sapiens*.
4. Plants commonly photosynthesise and have cellulose walls; fungi absorb nutrients and commonly have chitin-containing walls.
5. An identification tool that uses a sequence of paired alternative statements.
