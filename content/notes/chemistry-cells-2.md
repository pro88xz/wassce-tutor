Why does a zinc–copper cell give 1.10 V and a magnesium–copper cell about 2.7 V? Standard electrode potentials let you calculate any cell's voltage. The same chemistry explains the batteries in torches, phones and cars, and the rusting that eats away roofing sheets and vehicles. This lesson covers electrode potentials, practical batteries and corrosion.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define standard electrode potential and describe the standard hydrogen electrode
- use E° values to calculate the e.m.f. of a cell and predict whether a reaction is feasible
- describe the dry (Leclanché) cell and the lead–acid accumulator
- distinguish primary from secondary cells
- explain rusting as an electrochemical process and describe experiments on its conditions
- describe methods of preventing corrosion, including sacrificial protection

## Standard electrode potentials

:::callout{type=definition}
The **standard electrode potential (E°)** of a half-cell is its voltage measured against the **standard hydrogen electrode** (taken as **0.00 V**), at 25 °C, 1 atm and 1.0 mol/dm³ ion concentrations.
:::

| Half-cell (reduction) | E° (V) |
|---|---|
| Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg | −2.37 |
| Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al | −1.66 |
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn | −0.76 |
| Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe | −0.44 |
| Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | −0.13 |
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | **0.00** |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | +0.34 |
| Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag | +0.80 |

- A **more negative** E° means the metal **loses electrons more easily**: a strong reducing agent.
- A **more positive** E° means the ion **gains electrons more easily**: a strong oxidising agent.

### Calculating the e.m.f. of a cell

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = E^\circ_{\text{right}} - E^\circ_{\text{left}}$$

The **cathode** is the half-cell with the **more positive** E°.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**1. Daniell cell (Zn and Cu).**

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = (+0.34) - (-0.76) = +1.10 \text{ V}$$

**2. Magnesium–copper cell.**

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = (+0.34) - (-2.37) = +2.71 \text{ V}$$

**3. Is Zn + Fe²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Fe feasible?** Fe²⁺ is reduced (cathode) and Zn oxidised (anode):

$$E^\circ = (-0.44) - (-0.76) = +0.32 \text{ V}$$

The e.m.f. is **positive**, so the reaction is **feasible**: zinc displaces iron. A negative value would mean it does not happen.
:::

## Practical cells (batteries)

| Primary cells | Secondary (rechargeable) cells |
|---|---|
| Used once; the reaction cannot be reversed | Can be **recharged** by passing current the opposite way |
| Dry cell (torch batteries), alkaline cell | Lead–acid accumulator (car battery), lithium-ion (phones), nickel–cadmium |

### The dry (Leclanché) cell

- **Negative electrode (anode):** the **zinc case**: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
- **Positive electrode:** a **carbon rod**, surrounded by **manganese(IV) oxide** (which removes the hydrogen formed, acting as a depolariser)
- **Electrolyte:** a **paste of ammonium chloride** (and zinc chloride), not a liquid, so the cell does not spill
- Voltage about **1.5 V**

### The lead–acid accumulator (car battery)

- **Negative plates:** lead, Pb. **Positive plates:** lead(IV) oxide, PbO₂.
- **Electrolyte:** dilute **sulphuric acid**.
- On **discharge**, both plates turn into lead(II) sulphate and the acid becomes more dilute; each cell gives about **2 V** (six cells give 12 V).
- On **charging**, the reactions are reversed.
- The **density of the acid** shows the state of charge: it falls as the battery runs down.

## Corrosion and rusting

:::callout{type=definition}
**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with its surroundings. **Rusting** is the corrosion of **iron and steel**, forming **hydrated iron(III) oxide**, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.
:::

![Three test tubes with iron nails: in air and water the nail rusts; with dry air (calcium chloride) and with boiled water under oil (no air) it does not](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/rusting-tubes.svg)

*Figure 1. Rusting needs both water and oxygen. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Conditions for rusting:** both **water** and **oxygen (air)** must be present. Rusting is speeded up by **salt** (an electrolyte), **acidic gases** such as SO₂, and higher temperature.

**Rusting is electrochemical.** On a wet iron surface, small cells form:

- Iron is oxidised where it is anodic: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
- Oxygen is reduced where it is cathodic: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
- Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ form iron(II) hydroxide, which air oxidises further to **rust**.

### Preventing rusting

| Method | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Painting, oiling, greasing, plastic coating** | **Barrier** keeps out air and water | Gates, bridges, bicycle chains, wire fences |
| **Galvanising** (zinc coating) | Barrier, **and** zinc corrodes first even if scratched (**sacrificial** protection) | Roofing sheets ("zinc"), buckets |
| **Tin plating** | Barrier only; if scratched, iron rusts faster | Food cans |
| **Electroplating** (chromium, nickel) | Barrier; attractive finish | Car and bicycle parts |
| **Sacrificial protection** | A more reactive metal (Mg or Zn blocks) attached to the iron corrodes instead | Ship hulls, pipelines |
| **Alloying** | Stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel) does not rust | Cutlery, sinks |

:::callout{type=local-example}
The corrugated roofing sheets used across Sierra Leone are called "**zinc**" because they are **galvanised** iron. Near the sea in Freetown, salty air speeds up corrosion, so even galvanised roofs rust within some years once the zinc layer wears away. Painting them extends their life. Old car and torch batteries contain lead, acid and other harmful chemicals, and should never be thrown into drains.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **scratched tin can** rusts **faster** than unprotected iron, because iron is more reactive than tin and becomes the anode. A **scratched galvanised** sheet does **not** rust at the scratch, because zinc is more reactive than iron and corrodes instead.
:::

## Key points

- **E°** is measured against the hydrogen electrode (0.00 V); more negative E° = better reducing agent.
- **E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode)**; a positive value means the reaction is **feasible**.
- **Dry cell:** zinc case (−), carbon rod (+), NH₄Cl paste, about 1.5 V. **Lead–acid:** Pb and PbO₂ in H₂SO₄, about 2 V per cell, rechargeable.
- **Rusting** needs **water and oxygen**; salt speeds it up; it is an electrochemical process.
- Prevention: **barriers** (paint, oil, plating), **galvanising and sacrificial protection**, **alloying**.

## Quick check

1. Using the table, calculate the e.m.f. of a cell made from zinc and silver half-cells.
2. Name the electrolyte in a car battery.
3. What two substances are needed for iron to rust?
4. Explain why zinc protects iron even when a galvanised sheet is scratched.

**Answers:**

1. $(+0.80) - (-0.76) = +1.56$ V.
2. Dilute sulphuric acid.
3. Water and oxygen (air).
4. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it loses electrons and corrodes instead of the iron (sacrificial protection).
