Every text you will study for WASSCE Literature belongs to one of three families: **prose**, **drama** or **poetry**. The family tells you how the text is built, how to read it and what an examiner will expect you to say about it. A novel is read in chapters and judged on its plot and characters; a play is written to be acted, so dialogue and stage directions matter; a poem works through lines, sound and images. This lesson explains each genre, its main types and features, and how the genres overlap.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define genre and name the three genres of literature
- state the main features and types of prose, drama and poetry
- distinguish the three genres from one another
- explain how the genres overlap, with examples

## What is a genre?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **genre** is a category of literature defined by its form and features. The three main genres are **prose**, **drama** and **poetry**. Each has **sub-genres** (types): the novel and the short story are types of prose; tragedy and comedy are types of drama; the sonnet and the elegy are types of poetry.
:::

## Prose

:::callout{type=definition}
**Prose** is literature written in ordinary sentences and paragraphs, without the line breaks and regular rhythm of verse. Prose **fiction** tells an invented story through a **narrator**.
:::

**Types of prose**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Novel** | A long work of prose fiction with a developed plot, several characters and often sub-plots, e.g. Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* |
| **Novella** | Shorter than a novel, longer than a short story, usually focused on one main line of action |
| **Short story** | A brief work, often read at one sitting, with few characters and a single main incident |
| **Fable, folktale, legend** | Traditional stories, often with a moral or explaining origins |
| **Literary non-fiction** | Biography, autobiography, memoir and essay, written with literary skill about real life |

**Elements of prose fiction:** **plot** (the sequence of events), **characters**, **setting** (time and place), **point of view** (who tells the story: first person "I" or third person "he/she"), **theme** (the central idea), **style** (the writer's use of language) and **narrative technique** (how the story is told, e.g. flashback, letters, diary entries).

## Drama

:::callout{type=definition}
**Drama** is literature written to be **performed** by actors on a stage before an audience. The story is told mainly through **dialogue** and **action**, not through a narrator.
:::

**Features of drama**

- **Dialogue**: conversation between characters carries the story
- **Stage directions**: instructions, usually in italics, on movement, setting, lighting and how lines are spoken
- **Acts and scenes**: the divisions of a play
- **Cast list** (dramatis personae): the characters, listed at the start
- **Conflict**: the struggle that drives the action
- **Devices of the stage**: soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony

:::callout{type=definition}
A **soliloquy** is a speech in which a character, alone on stage, speaks his or her thoughts aloud. An **aside** is a short remark to the audience that other characters on stage are not meant to hear. **Dramatic irony** is when the audience knows something a character does not.
:::

**Types of drama**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tragedy** | A serious play in which the hero falls from high position to ruin, usually through a flaw of character, e.g. Shakespeare's *Macbeth* |
| **Comedy** | A light play that amuses and ends happily, often with marriage or reconciliation |
| **Tragicomedy** | Mixes serious and comic elements; may threaten disaster but end happily |
| **Farce** | Extreme comedy built on absurd situations, horseplay and coincidence |
| **Melodrama** | Exaggerated emotion and simple good-against-evil characters, with suspense |

## Poetry

:::callout{type=definition}
**Poetry** is literature written in **lines** and often grouped in **stanzas**, using rhythm, sound and concentrated, figurative language to express feeling and ideas. It says much in few words.
:::

**Features of poetry:** **lines** and **stanzas**; **rhythm** and **metre** (the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables); often **rhyme**; **imagery** (pictures in words); **figures of speech** such as simile, metaphor and personification; **sound devices** such as alliteration, assonance and onomatopoeia; and a **speaker** or **persona** who may not be the poet.

**Types of poetry**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Lyric** | A short poem expressing personal feeling |
| **Sonnet** | Fourteen lines with a fixed rhyme scheme |
| **Ode** | A serious, dignified poem praising a person or thing |
| **Elegy** | A poem of mourning for the dead (an oral **dirge** is similar) |
| **Ballad** | A song-like poem that tells a story, often in four-line stanzas |
| **Epic** | A very long narrative poem about a hero and great deeds |
| **Free verse** | Poetry without regular rhyme or metre |

## Comparing the three genres

| | Prose | Drama | Poetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Written in** | Sentences and paragraphs | Dialogue and stage directions | Lines and stanzas |
| **Story told by** | A narrator | Characters, through speech and action | A speaker (persona) |
| **Divided into** | Chapters | Acts and scenes | Stanzas |
| **Meant to be** | Read | Performed and watched | Read aloud or heard |
| **Main types** | Novel, novella, short story | Tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy, farce | Lyric, sonnet, ode, elegy, ballad, epic |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Identify the genre of each extract and give one reason.**

**Extract 1**

> KARIM: *(slamming the door)* You promised me the land!\
> FATMATA: *(quietly)* I promised you nothing.

**Extract 2**

> The harmattan came early that year. By the time Musu reached the market, the dust had turned her black shoes grey, and she had decided she would never marry the chief's son.

**Extract 3**

> Morning comes to Tagrin in a boat of light,\
> the fishermen lean on the wind,\
> and the nets come home heavy.

**Answers.** (1) **Drama**: the speaker's name comes before each line, and the stage directions in brackets tell the actors how to move and speak. (2) **Prose**: continuous sentences told by a narrator, with a character and a setting. (3) **Poetry**: it is arranged in lines, with imagery and personification ("a boat of light", "lean on the wind").
:::

## How the genres overlap

The boundaries are not walls:

- **Verse drama**: Shakespeare wrote much of his plays in **blank verse** (unrhymed lines of regular metre), so they are drama *and* poetry.
- **Narrative poetry**: the ballad and the epic tell stories, with plot and characters like prose.
- **Poetic prose**: some novelists write passages so rich in imagery and rhythm that they read like poetry.
- **Songs in plays and novels**: many African plays and novels include songs, chants and proverbs from oral tradition.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Calling a play a "novel" or a poem a "story".** Use the right terms for the genre: a play has **acts, scenes, a playwright and an audience**; a novel has **chapters, a novelist, a narrator and a reader**; a poem has **stanzas, a poet and a speaker**. Examiners notice and penalise mixed terms.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Name the three genres of literature."}
Prose, drama and poetry.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Give two features that make drama different from prose."}
Any two: drama is written to be performed; it is told through dialogue and action rather than a narrator; it uses stage directions; it is divided into acts and scenes.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. What is the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?"}
A soliloquy is a speech by a character alone on stage revealing his or her thoughts. An aside is a short remark to the audience, made with other characters present, that they are not meant to hear.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Which type of poem mourns the dead?"}
The elegy (in oral literature, the dirge).
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Why can Shakespeare's plays be called both drama and poetry?"}
They are written to be performed on stage, but much of their dialogue is in verse (blank verse), with regular metre and poetic language.
:::

## Key points

- A **genre** is a category of literature; the three are **prose**, **drama** and **poetry**.
- **Prose**: sentences and paragraphs, told by a narrator; novel, novella, short story.
- **Drama**: written to be performed; dialogue, stage directions, acts and scenes; tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy, farce, melodrama.
- **Poetry**: lines and stanzas, rhythm, sound and imagery; lyric, sonnet, ode, elegy, ballad, epic, free verse.
- The genres **overlap**: verse drama, narrative poems, poetic prose.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Paper 1 regularly asks you to identify the genre or type of an extract and to define terms such as *novella*, *tragedy*, *farce*, *soliloquy*, *aside*, *elegy* and *ballad*. Paper 2 is on prose and Paper 3 on drama and poetry, so knowing each genre's features tells you what to discuss: plot, narrator and setting for prose; conflict, dialogue and stage devices for drama; imagery, sound and form for poetry.
:::
