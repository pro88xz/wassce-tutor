:::callout{type=definition}
The **Sokoto jihad** was the Islamic reform movement and holy war led by the Fulani scholar **Usman dan Fodio** against the Hausa rulers of **Gobir** and the other Hausa states, beginning in **1804**.
:::

It was caused by **religious** grievances (rulers mixing Islam with traditional practices), **political oppression**, **heavy and unjust taxes**, and **social injustice**. It created the **Sokoto Caliphate**, the largest state in nineteenth-century West Africa, with its capital at **Sokoto (founded 1809)**. This lesson explains the causes, course, success and effects of Usman dan Fodio's jihad.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning of jihad and the background to the nineteenth-century jihads
- explain the causes of the Sokoto jihad
- outline the main events of the jihad from 1804 to 1812
- explain why the jihad succeeded
- describe how the Sokoto Caliphate was organised
- explain the effects of the Sokoto jihad

## What was a jihad?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **jihad** (Arabic: "struggle" or "striving") is an effort in the way of God. In West African history the word usually means a **holy war** fought by Muslim reformers to **purify Islam**, remove rulers they regarded as unbelievers or bad Muslims, and set up states governed by **Islamic law (Sharia)**.
:::

The Sokoto jihad was part of a wave of reform movements in the Western Sudan led mainly by **Fulani** scholars:

| Jihad | Leader | Date |
|---|---|---|
| **Futa Jallon** | Karamoko Alfa | from about 1725 |
| **Futa Toro** | Sulaiman Bal and Abdul Qadir | from about 1776 |
| **Sokoto (Hausaland)** | **Usman dan Fodio** | **1804** |
| **Masina** | **Seku Ahmadu** | **1818** |
| **Tukulor empire** | **al-Hajj Umar Tall** | **1852** |

## Usman dan Fodio

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figure"}
**Usman dan Fodio (1754–1817)** was a Fulani Muslim scholar and teacher of the **Qadiriyya** brotherhood, born in **Gobir**, one of the Hausa states.
:::

From about **1774** he travelled and **preached** across Hausaland, condemning corruption, injustice and the mixing of Islam with traditional worship. His learning and simple life won him many followers among the Fulani and the ordinary Hausa peasants (**talakawa**).

## Causes of the Sokoto jihad

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Religious** | Hausa rulers were Muslims in name but mixed Islam with **traditional religion**, such as sacrifices to spirits; Usman wanted to **purify Islam** and rule by Sharia |
| **Political** | the rulers were **oppressive and corrupt**; Yunfa, king of Gobir and a former pupil of Usman, turned against him and tried to restrict his preaching and his community |
| **Economic** | **heavy and illegal taxes**, such as the cattle tax (**jangali**) on Fulani herdsmen, market taxes and the seizure of property, angered peasants and herders |
| **Social** | **injustice** in the courts, bribery, forced labour and the **enslavement of free-born Muslims** |
| **Ethnic** | the **Fulani**, both settled scholars and cattle herders, felt oppressed by the Hausa ruling class |
| **Influence of earlier jihads** | the success of the Futa Jallon and Futa Toro jihads showed that reform by force was possible |
| **Usman's leadership** | his learning, writings and fame gave the movement a respected leader |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**The immediate cause**

In **1802–1804** relations between Usman's community at **Degel** and **Yunfa** of Gobir broke down. Yunfa attacked a group of Usman's followers and threatened Degel. On **21 February 1804**, Usman and his followers made a **hijra** (migration, copying the Prophet's flight from Mecca) to **Gudu**. There his followers elected him **Amir al-Mu'minin** (Commander of the Faithful), and the **jihad was declared** against Gobir.
:::

## Course of the jihad, 1804–1812

1. **1804:** victory over the Gobir army at **Tabkin Kwotto**, which encouraged supporters across Hausaland.
2. **1804–1808:** Usman gave **flags** to local leaders, who led risings in the other Hausa states: **Kano, Katsina, Zaria (Zazzau)** and **Daura** fell to the jihadists.
3. **1808:** **Alkalawa**, the capital of Gobir, was captured and **Yunfa was killed**.
4. **1809:** **Muhammad Bello**, Usman's son, founded **Sokoto** as the new capital.
5. The jihad spread to **Bauchi**, **Adamawa** (led by Modibbo Adama), **Gombe**, **Nupe** and later **Ilorin**.
6. **1812:** Usman divided the empire for administration between his son **Bello** (east, at Sokoto) and his brother **Abdullahi** (west, at **Gwandu**). Usman retired to teaching and writing and died in **1817**; Bello became caliph.

## Why the jihad succeeded

1. **Strong leadership** of Usman, Abdullahi and Bello, who were scholars, organisers and soldiers.
2. **Islam as a unifying ideology**: it gave fighters a cause and the promise of reward.
3. **Support of the oppressed**: Hausa peasants (**talakawa**), Fulani herders and enslaved people who hated the old rulers.
4. **Disunity of the Hausa states**, which had long fought one another and did not help each other.
5. **Unpopularity and corruption** of the Hausa rulers.
6. **The flag-bearer system**: local leaders fought in their own areas at the same time.
7. **Fulani cavalry** and the mobility of the herders.

## How the Sokoto Caliphate was organised

![The Sokoto Caliphate: Usman dan Fodio as Commander of the Faithful; Sokoto under Muhammad Bello ruling the eastern emirates such as Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Bauchi, Adamawa, Gombe, Hadejia and Katagum; Gwandu under Abdullahi ruling the western emirates such as Ilorin, Nupe, Agaie and Lapai](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/sokoto-caliphate-structure.svg)

*Figure 1. How the Sokoto Caliphate was organised. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Level | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Caliph** | **Sultan of Sokoto** (Amir al-Mu'minin) | supreme political and religious leader; appointed and could remove emirs |
| **Gwandu** | the Emir of Gwandu | supervised the **western emirates** |
| **Emirates** | an **emir** in each | collected taxes, kept order, ran **Islamic courts** under an **alkali** (judge), raised soldiers |
| **Districts and villages** | district and village heads | collected taxes and kept order locally |

Emirs sent **tribute** to Sokoto or Gwandu, and the caliph's **officials (waziri and others)** travelled to inspect them. This system of emirates was later used by the British for **indirect rule**.

## Effects of the Sokoto jihad

1. **Political unification:** dozens of rival Hausa states were united in **one caliphate** of about thirty emirates.
2. **Spread of Islam:** Islam spread from the towns to the countryside and to new areas such as Adamawa and Nupe.
3. **Islamic education and literacy:** Usman, Abdullahi, Bello and Usman's daughter **Nana Asma'u** wrote hundreds of books and poems; schools and learning spread.
4. **Sharia law and fairer administration**, at least in the early years.
5. **New ruling class:** the **Fulani** replaced the Hausa kings as rulers.
6. **Collapse of Old Oyo:** the jihad spread to **Ilorin**, which helped to bring down the **Oyo Empire** in the 1830s.
7. **Trade and towns grew** under greater peace and unity; **Kano** became a great commercial centre.
8. **Warfare and slave raiding** on the frontiers increased.
9. **Inspired other jihads**, notably those of Seku Ahmadu in Masina and al-Hajj Umar.
10. **Basis for British indirect rule** in Northern Nigeria after 1903.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The jihads did not reach Sierra Leone as wars, but the wider **Fula and Mandinka Islamic revival**, especially from **Futa Jallon**, spread Islam into northern Sierra Leone. Fula and Mandinka traders and teachers brought Islam and Arabic learning to peoples such as the **Susu, Limba, Temne and Koranko**, and later to Freetown, where Fula and Mandinka scholars founded mosques and schools.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not describe the jihad only as a "Fulani war against the Hausa". Many Hausa peasants supported Usman, and his main aim was **religious reform**. Strong answers show that **religious, political, economic and social** causes worked together.
:::

## Key points

- **Jihad**: a holy struggle to purify Islam and set up states ruled by **Sharia**.
- **Usman dan Fodio** (1754–1817) began the jihad in **1804** after his **hijra to Gudu**.
- Causes: **religious impurity, oppression, heavy taxes, injustice, Fulani grievances**, earlier jihads.
- Key events: **Tabkin Kwotto (1804)**, fall of **Alkalawa (1808)**, founding of **Sokoto (1809)**, division of the caliphate **(1812)**.
- Effects: **unity, spread of Islam and learning, Fulani rule, fall of Oyo, trade**, but also **slave raiding**.

## Quick check

1. Who led the Sokoto jihad, and when did it begin?
2. What was the hijra of 1804?
3. Give three causes of the jihad.
4. Who founded Sokoto, and when?
5. How was the caliphate divided in 1812?
6. Give three effects of the jihad.

**Answers:**

1. Usman dan Fodio, in 1804.
2. The migration of Usman and his followers from Degel to Gudu on 21 February 1804, after which the jihad was declared.
3. Any three: mixing of Islam with traditional religion; oppression by Hausa rulers; heavy and illegal taxes; injustice and corruption; enslavement of Muslims; Fulani grievances; the example of earlier jihads.
4. Muhammad Bello, Usman's son, in 1809.
5. Bello administered the eastern emirates from Sokoto, and Abdullahi the western emirates from Gwandu.
6. Any three: political unity of Hausaland; spread of Islam; growth of Islamic learning; Sharia law; Fulani rule; collapse of Old Oyo; growth of trade; slave raiding; inspiration of other jihads.
