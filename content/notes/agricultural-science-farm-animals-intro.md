Farm animals turn grass, crop residues and kitchen waste into meat, milk, eggs and income. In Sierra Leone, most rural families keep a few **chickens, goats or sheep**, often roaming freely around the village; **N'Dama cattle** are herded in the north; pigs are kept in some communities; and commercial **poultry farms** supply eggs and broilers to the towns. This lesson introduces the farm animals, how they are classified, the main breeds kept in Sierra Leone, the importance of livestock, the systems of management, and the general management practices every livestock keeper should know.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- classify farm animals by their digestive system and their use
- name local and exotic breeds of the main farm animals
- state the importance of livestock to the farmer and the nation
- describe extensive, semi-intensive and intensive systems of management
- describe common management practices such as identification, castration and record keeping
- state the problems of livestock production in Sierra Leone

## Classifying farm animals

![Farm animals classified by digestive system (ruminants: cattle, sheep, goats; non-ruminants: pigs, rabbits; poultry: chickens, ducks, turkeys) and by use (meat, milk, eggs, draught, skins and wool)](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/farm-animal-classes.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Ruminants** (cud-chewers) have a **four-chambered stomach** and can digest **cellulose** in grass with the help of microbes: cattle, sheep, goats.
- **Non-ruminants** (monogastrics) have a **simple stomach** and need more concentrated feed: pigs, rabbits.
- **Poultry** are domestic birds with a **crop and gizzard**: chickens, ducks, turkeys, guinea fowl.
:::

## Breeds

| Animal | Local / African breeds | Exotic breeds |
|---|---|---|
| **cattle** | **N'Dama** (small, hardy, **tolerant of trypanosomiasis**), West African Shorthorn (Muturu), White Fulani (Zebu) | Friesian (milk), Jersey (milk), Brahman |
| **goats** | **West African Dwarf** goat (hardy, prolific, tolerant of trypanosomiasis) | Saanen (milk), Boer (meat) |
| **sheep** | **West African Dwarf (Djallonké)** sheep | Merino (wool) |
| **pigs** | local (village) pigs | Large White, Landrace, Duroc |
| **poultry** | local (village) chickens: hardy, good mothers, few eggs | **broilers** (fast-growing meat birds), **layers** (e.g. brown egg-laying hybrids) |

:::callout{type=tip}
**Local breeds are hardy; exotic breeds are productive.** Local breeds survive heat, poor feed and diseases such as trypanosomiasis but grow slowly and give little milk or few eggs. Exotic breeds produce much more but need good feed, housing and health care. **Crossbreeding** tries to combine the two.
:::

## Importance of livestock

- **Food**: meat, milk, eggs: high-quality **protein**, vitamins and minerals.
- **Income** and **savings**: animals are sold when money is needed (school fees, medical bills); a "bank on legs".
- **Manure** for crops.
- **Draught power** (oxen, donkeys) in some areas.
- **Raw materials**: hides and skins (leather), wool, feathers, bones and blood (feed and fertiliser).
- **Social and cultural** uses: ceremonies, dowries, gifts, festivals.
- **Employment** for herders, traders, butchers, feed millers and veterinarians.
- **By-product use**: animals eat crop residues and kitchen waste.

## Systems of management

| System | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **extensive** (free range) | animals roam to find their own food, with little housing or care | cheap; little labour | slow growth; theft and losses; diseases and parasites spread; damage to crops; hard to manage breeding |
| **semi-intensive** | animals graze or forage in the day and are housed at night, with some supplementary feed | moderate cost; better control and growth | needs fencing, housing and some feed |
| **intensive** | animals kept **confined** all the time and given all their feed and care | fast growth, high output; easy control of disease, breeding and records | high cost of housing, feed and labour; disease spreads fast if hygiene fails |

**Transhumance** is the seasonal movement of cattle herds to find grazing and water, practised by herders in the north and across West Africa.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Thinking intensive is always best.** Intensive systems need money, reliable feed supply and skills. For many village farmers, a **semi-intensive** system (housing at night, grazing by day, vaccination and a little extra feed) gives the best return on a small investment.
:::

## General management practices

- **Identification**: ear tags, ear notching, tattooing, branding, leg bands (poultry); needed for records and breeding.
- **Housing**: dry, well ventilated, easy to clean, protected from rain, sun, thieves and predators.
- **Feeding and watering**: a balanced ration and clean water at all times.
- **Health care**: vaccination, deworming, control of ticks and other external parasites, isolation of sick animals.
- **Breeding control**: selecting good males, castrating unwanted males, timing mating.
- **Castration**: removing or destroying the testes of males not needed for breeding; they become calmer and fatten better.
- **Dehorning / disbudding**: removing horns or horn buds to prevent injuries.
- **Hoof trimming**, **culling** (removing unproductive, old or sick animals).
- **Record keeping**: births, weights, feed, diseases, treatments, sales.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Goats in the village.** In many villages, goats roam freely and eat crops, get lost, and pick up worms and diseases such as **PPR** (peste des petits ruminants). Families that build a simple **raised, slatted house**, tie or herd the goats during the farming season, **vaccinate against PPR** and **deworm** regularly lose far fewer animals and sell bigger, healthier goats.
:::

## Problems of livestock production in Sierra Leone

- **Diseases and parasites** (PPR, trypanosomiasis, Newcastle disease, African swine fever, worms, ticks), with few veterinary services in rural areas.
- **Poor feed**, especially in the dry season; high cost of commercial feed.
- **Low-producing local breeds**; few improved animals.
- **Theft** and predators.
- **Farmer–herder conflicts** over crop damage and grazing land.
- **Lack of capital**, credit and skills; poor marketing and processing facilities.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Distinguish ruminants from non-ruminants, with two examples of each."}
**Ruminants** have a four-chambered stomach and chew the cud, digesting cellulose with microbes (cattle, sheep, goats). **Non-ruminants** have a simple stomach (pigs, rabbits).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Why is the N'Dama breed of cattle important in Sierra Leone?"}
It is **tolerant of trypanosomiasis** (spread by tsetse flies), and hardy in hot, humid conditions.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Give four importances of livestock to a farmer."}
Any four: food (meat, milk, eggs); income and savings; manure; draught power; hides, skins and other raw materials; social and cultural uses.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. State two advantages and two disadvantages of the extensive system."}
Advantages: cheap; little labour. Disadvantages: slow growth; theft and losses; spread of disease and parasites; crop damage; uncontrolled breeding (any two).
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Name three methods of identifying farm animals."}
Any three: ear tags, ear notching, tattooing, branding, leg bands.
:::

## Key points

- Classes: **ruminants** (cattle, sheep, goats), **non-ruminants** (pigs, rabbits), **poultry**.
- Local breeds (**N'Dama, West African Dwarf goat and sheep, village chickens**) are hardy; exotic breeds are productive.
- Livestock give **food, income, manure, draught, raw materials** and social value.
- Systems: **extensive, semi-intensive, intensive**.
- Practices: **identification, housing, feeding, health care, castration, dehorning, culling, records**.
