Preparing a pure, dry salt is a classic WASSCE practical and theory question. The method you choose depends on one thing: **is the salt soluble or insoluble?** This lesson gives the four main methods with their steps and equations, a yield calculation, and explains why some salt solutions are not neutral (hydrolysis).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- choose a suitable method for preparing a given salt
- describe the preparation of soluble salts by adding excess solid to an acid
- describe the titration method for sodium, potassium and ammonium salts
- describe the preparation of insoluble salts by precipitation
- describe direct combination
- explain the hydrolysis of salts and predict the pH of salt solutions

## Method 1: excess solid + acid (soluble salts)

Used for soluble salts of metals **other than** Na, K and NH₄. React the acid with an excess of an **insoluble** reactant:

- a **metal**: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
- an **insoluble base (oxide or hydroxide)**: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
- a **carbonate**: MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Preparing copper(II) sulphate crystals from copper(II) oxide.**

1. Warm some dilute sulphuric acid in a beaker.
2. Add **copper(II) oxide** a little at a time, stirring, until **no more dissolves** (excess black solid remains). Using excess ensures all the acid is used up.
3. **Filter** to remove the excess copper(II) oxide. The filtrate is blue copper(II) sulphate solution.
4. **Evaporate** the filtrate until it is saturated (crystals form on a cold glass rod dipped in it).
5. Leave to **cool and crystallise**.
6. **Filter** off the blue crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water and **dry** between filter papers.

$$\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Yield.** What mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O crystals can be made from 4.0 g of copper(II) oxide? (CuO = 80, CuSO₄·5H₂O = 250)

- Moles of CuO $= 4.0 \div 80 = 0.050$ mol.
- Ratio CuO : CuSO₄·5H₂O = 1 : 1.
- Mass $= 0.050 \times 250 = 12.5$ g (theoretical yield).
:::

## Method 2: titration (soluble salts of Na, K and NH₄)

Sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds are **all soluble**, so an excess cannot be filtered off. Instead, exactly neutralise the acid with the alkali:

1. Pipette a measured volume of alkali (e.g. 25.0 cm³ NaOH) into a conical flask; add a few drops of **indicator**.
2. Run in acid from a **burette** until the indicator just changes colour; note the volume.
3. **Repeat without the indicator**, using exactly the same volumes, so the salt is not contaminated.
4. Evaporate and crystallise as before.

$$\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

## Method 3: precipitation (insoluble salts)

Also called **double decomposition**. Mix solutions of two soluble salts that contain the ions of the required insoluble salt.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Preparing barium sulphate.**

1. Mix solutions of **barium chloride** and **sodium sulphate** (or dilute sulphuric acid).
2. A white precipitate of barium sulphate forms at once.
3. **Filter**; wash the residue with distilled water to remove soluble impurities.
4. **Dry** in a warm oven.

$$\text{BaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)} + 2\text{NaCl(aq)}$$

Ionic equation: $\text{Ba}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)}$.
:::

Other examples: lead(II) iodide (yellow) from lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide; silver chloride (white) from silver nitrate and sodium chloride.

## Method 4: direct combination (synthesis)

Some salts are made by combining the elements directly, especially **anhydrous** salts that would react with water:

$$2\text{Fe(s)} + 3\text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{FeCl}_3\text{(s)}$$

$$\text{Fe(s)} + \text{S(s)} \rightarrow \text{FeS(s)}$$

## Hydrolysis of salts

Not all salt solutions are neutral. **Hydrolysis** is the reaction of a salt's ions with water to give an acidic or alkaline solution.

| Salt formed from | Example | Solution | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄ | **Neutral**, pH 7 | Neither ion reacts with water |
| Strong acid + weak base | NH₄Cl, (NH₄)₂SO₄, CuSO₄, FeCl₃ | **Acidic**, pH < 7 | NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺ releases H⁺ |
| Weak acid + strong base | Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa, NaHCO₃ | **Alkaline**, pH > 7 | CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ releases OH⁻ |

This is why **washing soda** solution feels soapy and turns red litmus blue, and why **ammonium sulphate** fertiliser slowly makes soils more acidic.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Local soap makers who use **wood ash** take advantage of hydrolysis. The potassium carbonate in the ash is the salt of a weak acid and a strong base, so its solution is strongly **alkaline**. That alkali is strong enough to turn palm kernel oil into soap.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In the titration method, the experiment is **repeated without indicator**. Evaporating the first flask would give a salt contaminated with indicator.
:::

## Key points

- **Insoluble salts:** precipitation; filter, wash, dry.
- **Soluble Na, K, NH₄ salts:** titration, then repeat without indicator and crystallise.
- **Other soluble salts:** add **excess** metal, insoluble base or carbonate to the acid, **filter off the excess**, evaporate to saturation and crystallise.
- **Direct combination** makes anhydrous salts such as FeCl₃.
- **Hydrolysis:** strong acid + weak base → acidic solution; weak acid + strong base → alkaline solution.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
WASSCE often asks you to **describe, with equations, the preparation of a named salt**: a soluble salt (CuSO₄, ZnSO₄, MgCl₂), a sodium salt (NaCl by titration) or an insoluble salt (BaSO₄, PbI₂). Explain **why excess solid is used** and why crystals are not evaporated to dryness. Hydrolysis questions ask you to predict the pH of salt solutions.
:::

## Quick check

1. Why is excess copper(II) oxide added to the acid when preparing copper(II) sulphate?
2. Name two solutions you could mix to prepare lead(II) iodide.
3. Why can sodium chloride not be prepared by adding excess sodium to hydrochloric acid?
4. Predict whether a solution of ammonium chloride is acidic, neutral or alkaline, and explain.

**Answers:**

1. To make sure all the acid reacts, so the salt is not contaminated with acid; the excess is easily filtered off.
2. Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide solutions.
3. Sodium reacts explosively with the acid (and with water), so the reaction cannot be controlled; titration with NaOH is used instead.
4. Acidic: it is the salt of a strong acid and a weak base, and NH₄⁺ ions release H⁺ ions in water.
