Stir salt into water and it vanishes; stir in mud and it clouds the water, then slowly settles; stir in powdered milk and the mixture stays cloudy but never settles. These are the three kinds of mixture of a substance in a liquid: **solutions, suspensions and colloids**. This lesson explains how they differ and why colloids matter from cooking to water treatment.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define solution, solute and solvent, and name common solvents and their uses
- distinguish saturated, unsaturated and supersaturated solutions
- distinguish true solutions, suspensions and colloids by their properties
- describe the Tyndall effect
- name the types of colloids, with examples, and describe coagulation

## Solutions

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **solution** is a **uniform (homogeneous) mixture** of two or more substances.
- The **solute** is the substance that dissolves; the **solvent** is the substance it dissolves in.
- A solution in water is an **aqueous** solution.
:::

Salt solution: salt is the solute, water the solvent. Solutions are not only solids in liquids. Air is a solution of gases, soda water a gas in a liquid, and brass a solid solution of zinc in copper.

### Solvents and their uses

| Solvent | Dissolves | Everyday use |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | Salts, sugars, many acids and bases | Cooking, cleaning, medicines |
| **Ethanol** | Iodine, resins, perfume oils, some dyes | Tincture of iodine, perfumes, medicines |
| **Propanone** (acetone) | Nail varnish, grease, plastics | Nail-varnish remover |
| **Kerosene / turpentine** | Oil paint, grease, tar | Thinning paint, removing grease and tar |
| **Trichloroethane / tetrachloroethene** | Grease | Dry cleaning |

**"Like dissolves like":** ionic and polar substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar substances (oils, fats, grease) dissolve in non-polar solvents such as kerosene.

## Saturated, unsaturated and supersaturated solutions

:::callout{type=definition}
- An **unsaturated** solution can dissolve **more** solute at that temperature.
- A **saturated** solution contains the **maximum** amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solute present.
- A **supersaturated** solution contains **more** solute than a saturated solution at that temperature. It is **unstable**: adding a tiny crystal (seeding) makes the excess crystallise out at once.
:::

**Testing for saturation:** add a little more solute at the same temperature. If it does not dissolve, the solution is saturated.

**Making a supersaturated solution:** prepare a saturated solution of sodium thiosulphate (or sodium ethanoate) while hot, then let it cool undisturbed.

## Solutions, suspensions and colloids

| Property | True solution | Colloid | Suspension |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Particle size** | Very small (below about 1 nm): ions and molecules | Intermediate (about 1–1000 nm) | Large (above about 1000 nm) |
| **Appearance** | Clear and transparent | Usually cloudy or translucent | Cloudy, opaque |
| **Settles on standing?** | No | No | **Yes** |
| **Passes through filter paper?** | Yes | Yes | **No**: residue left |
| **Tyndall effect** | **No**: beam invisible | **Yes**: beam visible | Particles may be seen |
| **Examples** | Salt solution, sugar solution | Milk, starch solution, fog, blood, gelatin | Muddy water, chalk in water, sand in water |

## The Tyndall effect

![A light beam passes invisibly through salt solution but is clearly visible through starch solution, because colloid particles scatter the light](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/tyndall-effect.svg)

*Figure 1. The Tyndall effect distinguishes a colloid from a true solution. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
The **Tyndall effect** is the **scattering of light** by colloid particles, which makes the path of a light beam **visible** in a colloid but not in a true solution.
:::

You see the Tyndall effect when sunlight streams through dusty air in a room or through mist in the morning, and when car headlights shine through fog.

## Types of colloids

A colloid has a **dispersed phase** (the particles) spread through a **dispersion medium**.

| Type | Dispersed phase in medium | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Sol** | Solid in liquid | Starch solution, paint, ink, blood |
| **Gel** | Liquid in solid | Jelly, gelatin, hair gel |
| **Emulsion** | Liquid in liquid | **Milk**, mayonnaise, palm-oil sauces, cream |
| **Foam** | Gas in liquid | Shaving foam, whipped cream, soap lather |
| **Solid foam** | Gas in solid | Foam rubber, bread, pumice |
| **Aerosol** | Liquid or solid in gas | Fog, mist, clouds (liquid); smoke, dust (solid) |

An **emulsifying agent** keeps an emulsion from separating. Soap emulsifies grease in water; egg yolk emulsifies oil in mayonnaise.

### Coagulation of colloids

Colloid particles often carry the **same electric charge**, so they repel each other and stay dispersed. Adding ions of opposite charge cancels this and makes the particles **clump and settle**: this is **coagulation**.

- **Alum** (Al³⁺ ions) coagulates clay particles in **water treatment**.
- **Blood clotting**: ferric chloride or alum stops bleeding from small cuts by coagulating blood.
- **Smoke precipitators** in factories use electric charge to remove smoke particles.
- **River deltas** form where river clay (a colloid) meets the ions of sea water.

:::callout{type=local-example}
When fresh **palm-oil soup** or **groundnut soup** is stirred, the oil spreads through the water as tiny droplets, making an **emulsion**. If the soup is left to cool, the oil separates and floats to the top, because there is little emulsifier to hold it. Fog over the Freetown peninsula on cool mornings is an aerosol, and car headlights show its Tyndall effect.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Milk is **not** a true solution: it is a **colloid** (an emulsion of fat droplets in water). It passes through filter paper and does not settle, but it shows the Tyndall effect and looks cloudy.
:::

## Key points

- A **solution** is a uniform mixture of **solute** in **solvent**; "like dissolves like".
- **Saturated** = maximum solute at that temperature; **supersaturated** = more than that (unstable).
- **True solution:** tiny particles, clear, no settling, no Tyndall effect. **Colloid:** intermediate particles, Tyndall effect, no settling. **Suspension:** large particles, settles, filterable.
- Colloid types: **sol, gel, emulsion, foam, aerosol**.
- **Coagulation** by oppositely charged ions (alum) is used in water treatment.

## Quick check

1. Name the solvent you would use to remove an oil-paint stain.
2. How would you show that a solution is saturated?
3. Give two differences between a colloid and a suspension.
4. What type of colloid is (a) milk, (b) smoke?

**Answers:**

1. Kerosene or turpentine.
2. Add a little more solute at the same temperature; if it does not dissolve, the solution is saturated.
3. Any two: colloid particles are smaller; colloids do not settle, suspensions do; colloids pass through filter paper, suspensions do not; colloids are often translucent, suspensions opaque.
4. (a) Emulsion; (b) aerosol (solid in gas).
