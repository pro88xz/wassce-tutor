# Respiratory systems and gas exchange: fish, insects and mammals

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- describe gas exchange in fish, insects and mammals
- relate the structure of gills, tracheae and alveoli to their functions
- explain ventilation and the maintenance of diffusion gradients
- compare aquatic and terrestrial respiratory surfaces
- identify the route air follows through the human respiratory system

## Gas exchange in fish

Fish obtain dissolved oxygen from water using gills. Each gill is supported by a gill arch and bears gill filaments with many thin lamellae. The filaments and lamellae provide a large surface area. Their thin epithelium gives a short diffusion distance, and a dense capillary network transports gases.

Water passes over the gill lamellae while blood flows through them in the opposite direction. This counter-current arrangement maintains a diffusion gradient along much of the exchange surface: water at each point has a higher oxygen concentration than the blood flowing beside it. Oxygen therefore continues to diffuse into the blood. Carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction.

During ventilation, water enters through the mouth and passes over the gills before leaving through the opercular opening in bony fish. Water contains less oxygen per unit volume than air, so fish must move substantial volumes of water across their gills.

## Gas exchange in insects

Insects use a tracheal system. Air enters through external openings called spiracles, passes along branching tubes called tracheae and reaches fine tracheoles close to body cells. Oxygen diffuses from the tracheoles into tissues, while carbon dioxide diffuses out.

This system delivers oxygen directly to tissues, so insect blood (haemolymph) usually plays little or no role in oxygen transport. Spiracles can close to reduce water loss. In some active insects, body movements help ventilate the tracheal system.

## The mammalian respiratory system

In humans, air enters through the nose or mouth and passes through the pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi and bronchioles before reaching the alveoli in the lungs. The trachea is supported by C-shaped rings of cartilage that prevent it from collapsing. Its lining contains mucus-producing cells and cilia; mucus traps dust and microorganisms, and cilia move it towards the throat.

The lungs contain millions of alveoli. Each alveolus has a thin, moist wall and is surrounded by capillaries. Together, the alveoli provide a very large surface area. Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into the blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into the alveoli and is exhaled.

## Ventilation in mammals

During inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and flattens. External intercostal muscles contract, lifting the ribs up and out. The volume of the thoracic cavity increases, pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure, and air moves in.

During quiet exhalation, the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax. The thoracic cavity volume decreases, pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure, and air moves out. Forced exhalation uses additional muscles.

## Comparing respiratory surfaces

Fish gills are adapted to extract oxygen from water and use counter-current flow. Insects have a branching tracheal system that brings air close to cells. Mammals use lungs with alveoli and a blood circulatory system to transport oxygen over long distances. All three systems provide a large, thin exchange surface and maintain concentration gradients.

## WASSCE examination focus

Learn the route of air and the mechanism of inhalation. In comparisons, do not say that insect blood carries most oxygen; the tracheal system delivers it directly to tissues. For fish, explain how counter-current flow maintains the diffusion gradient rather than simply stating that water and blood move in opposite directions.
