The **ledger** is the **main book of account**, containing all the **accounts** of a business, in which transactions are classified after they have been recorded in the books of original entry. Because a business may have hundreds of accounts, the ledger is divided into the **sales ledger** (customers' accounts), the **purchases ledger** (suppliers' accounts), the **general (nominal) ledger** (income, expense, asset and liability accounts) and the **private ledger** (confidential accounts such as capital and drawings). This lesson explains the ledger, its divisions, and how entries are posted to it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define the ledger and explain its importance
- describe the divisions of the ledger and the accounts kept in each
- describe the layout of a ledger account and folio references
- post entries from the books of original entry to the ledger
- explain the difference between a book of original entry and the ledger

## The ledger and its divisions

![The ledger divided into the sales ledger for debtors, the purchases ledger for creditors, the general ledger for nominal and real accounts, and the private ledger for confidential accounts such as capital and drawings](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/ledger-divisions.svg)

*Figure 1. The divisions of the ledger. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Division | Accounts kept | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales ledger (debtors' ledger)** | **personal accounts of credit customers** (trade receivables) | Kamara, Bah |
| **Purchases ledger (creditors' ledger)** | **personal accounts of credit suppliers** (trade payables) | Sesay Wholesalers, J. Conteh |
| **General (nominal) ledger** | **nominal** accounts (sales, purchases, wages, rent) and **real** accounts (premises, vehicles, inventory) | wages account, motor van account |
| **Private ledger** | **confidential** accounts the owner wants kept private | capital, drawings, loans from the owner |
| **Cash book** | the cash and bank accounts | (kept separately) |

**Advantages of dividing the ledger:**

1. **Work can be shared** among several clerks.
2. Errors are **easier to locate**.
3. **Control accounts** can be kept for each ledger.
4. **Confidential** information is protected.
5. Information about debtors and creditors is **easier to find**.

## Layout of a ledger account

Each account has a **debit (left)** side and a **credit (right)** side, and each side has the same four columns:

- **Date** of the transaction;
- **Details**: the name of the **other account** in the double entry;
- **Folio**: the **page reference** of the book of original entry or of the other account, so that every entry can be traced;
- **Amount** in NLe.

## Posting from the books of original entry

| From | To |
|---|---|
| **Sales day book** | debit each customer (sales ledger); credit sales total (general ledger) |
| **Purchases day book** | credit each supplier (purchases ledger); debit purchases total |
| **Returns inwards book** | credit each customer; debit returns inwards total |
| **Returns outwards book** | debit each supplier; credit returns outwards total |
| **Cash book** | the opposite side of each receipt or payment to the named account |
| **Journal** | the debits and credits as shown |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Post these entries to the ledger of Amie's Store (NLe).**

- 2 May: bought goods on credit from Sesay, 8,000 (purchases day book).
- 4 May: sold goods on credit to Kamara, 6,000 (sales day book).
- 9 May: returned goods to Sesay, 500 (returns outwards book).
- 20 May: Kamara paid 6,000 by cheque (cash book).

**Sesay (purchases ledger)**

| Dr | NLe | Cr | NLe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Returns out | 500 | Purchases | 8,000 |

**Kamara (sales ledger)**

| Dr | NLe | Cr | NLe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 6,000 | Bank | 6,000 |

**General ledger:** Purchases Dr 8,000 (Sesay); Sales Cr 6,000 (Kamara); Returns outwards Cr 500 (Sesay). **Cash book:** bank Dr 6,000 (Kamara).

Sesay is still owed $8\,000 - 500 = 7\,500$; Kamara's account is cleared.
:::

## Book of original entry and ledger compared

| Book of original entry | Ledger |
|---|---|
| records transactions **first**, in **date order** | **classifies** transactions by account |
| shows each transaction **once**, in one place | shows each transaction **twice** (double entry) |
| does not show balances (except the cash book) | shows the **balance** of each account |
| e.g. sales day book, journal | e.g. Kamara's account, wages account |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
In the **details** column, write the name of the **other** account in the double entry, not the name of the account you are writing in. In Kamara's account, the sale is shown as "Sales", not "Kamara".
:::

## Key points

- The **ledger** holds all the accounts and **classifies** transactions.
- Divisions: **sales ledger** (debtors), **purchases ledger** (creditors), **general/nominal ledger**, **private ledger**; the **cash book** holds cash and bank.
- **Folio** references link entries to their source.
- Postings follow the rules: day book items to **personal accounts**, day book totals to **sales, purchases or returns** accounts.

## Quick check

1. Define the ledger.
2. In which ledger is a credit customer's account kept?
3. What is a folio reference?
4. Why might the capital account be kept in a private ledger?
5. State two differences between the journal and the ledger.

**Answers:**

1. The main book of account containing all the accounts of a business, in which transactions are classified.
2. The sales (debtors') ledger.
3. A page reference showing where the other part of an entry, or its source, can be found.
4. To keep the owner's financial information confidential.
5. Any two: the journal records transactions first in date order, the ledger classifies them by account; the journal shows each transaction once, the ledger twice; the ledger shows balances, the journal does not.
