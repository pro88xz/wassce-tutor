# Mass wasting: slope processes, causes and management

## Learning objectives
You should be able to define mass wasting, classify common forms of slope movement, explain factors that trigger slope failure and evaluate ways to reduce risk.

## Meaning of mass wasting
Mass wasting, also called mass movement, is the downslope movement of weathered rock, soil or other material under the direct influence of gravity. Water often increases the weight of material or reduces friction, but gravity is the driving force.

Mass wasting differs from river or coastal erosion because the material moves downslope largely under gravity rather than being carried by a flowing agent.

## Main types of mass movement
- **Rockfall:** blocks detach from a steep slope or cliff and fall or bounce downslope. It is common where joints are widened by weathering.
- **Landslide:** a general term for rapid movement of a mass of rock or soil. A slide may move along a relatively distinct failure surface.
- **Rotational slide or slump:** material moves along a curved failure surface, often producing a backward-tilted block and a steep scarp.
- **Translational slide:** a mass moves along a more planar surface, such as a bedding plane or weak soil layer.
- **Mudflow or debris flow:** water-saturated soil, mud, rock fragments and sometimes vegetation move rapidly down a channel or slope.
- **Soil creep:** very slow downslope movement, often visible through tilted fences, bent tree trunks or displaced walls.

## Conditions that promote slope failure
Slope stability depends on the balance between forces driving material downslope and the strength resisting movement. Failure becomes more likely when:
- prolonged or intense rainfall saturates soil, increases its weight and raises pore-water pressure;
- vegetation is removed, reducing root reinforcement and increasing surface runoff;
- slopes are cut too steeply for roads, buildings or quarrying;
- water is allowed to collect in cracks or behind retaining structures;
- weak rock layers dip towards a slope or are undercut;
- earthquakes or vibrations disturb an already unstable slope.

No single factor causes every landslide. The combination of slope angle, geology, water, land use and triggering events determines the level of risk.

## Impacts
Mass movement can cause deaths and injuries, destroy houses and roads, bury farmland, block rivers and disrupt water supplies. A blocked river may create a temporary lake that later floods downstream if the natural dam fails. Sediment entering streams can damage aquatic habitats and infrastructure.

## Reducing risk
Hazard mapping and land-use planning should prevent new construction on unstable slopes and unsafe drainage channels. Maintain vegetation where appropriate, control surface water, install engineered drains and use retaining structures only after proper geotechnical assessment. Road cuts and quarries require stable slope design. Early-warning arrangements, evacuation plans and public education are important in exposed communities.

In steep parts of Freetown, intense rainfall combined with slope disturbance and vegetation clearance can increase landslide risk. Practical management must combine drainage, land-use control, slope engineering and community preparedness; tree planting alone cannot stabilise every slope.

## WASSCE examination focus
Use a process sequence: identify the material and slope condition, explain the trigger, describe the movement and state the resulting impact. Distinguish slow soil creep from rapid landslides and explain why saturated material may become unstable.

## Quick check
1. What is the main driving force in mass wasting?
2. Distinguish rockfall from soil creep.
3. State three factors that can trigger slope failure.
4. Give two measures for reducing landslide risk in a steep urban area.

**Answers:** 1. Gravity. 2. Rockfall is rapid falling of detached blocks; soil creep is very slow downslope movement of soil. 3. Saturation, vegetation removal, over-steepening, undercutting, weak geology or earthquakes. 4. Drainage control, hazard zoning, slope engineering, vegetation protection and evacuation planning.
