Every concrete-block house in Freetown depends on calcium compounds: cement, mortar and whitewash. Farmers use lime on acid soils, doctors use plaster of Paris for broken bones, and steelworks use limestone to remove impurities. This lesson follows calcium through the limestone cycle and covers cement, concrete and plaster.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the limestone cycle with equations
- describe the manufacture of quicklime and the slaking of lime
- state the properties and uses of calcium carbonate, calcium oxide and calcium hydroxide
- describe the manufacture of cement and the setting of cement and mortar
- describe plaster of Paris and its uses
- state the uses of calcium chloride and calcium sulphate

## The limestone cycle

![The limestone cycle: limestone heated in a kiln gives quicklime and CO₂; quicklime slaked with water gives slaked lime; slaked lime absorbs CO₂ to form calcium carbonate again](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/lime-cycle.svg)

*Figure 1. Limestone, quicklime and slaked lime. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

### Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃

Found as **limestone**, **marble**, **chalk** and in **seashells** and coral.

- **Decomposes** on strong heating (about 900 °C): CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
- Reacts with dilute acids, giving CO₂
- Insoluble in water, but dissolves slowly in rainwater containing CO₂ (forming Ca(HCO₃)₂: hard water and caves)

### Calcium oxide, CaO (quicklime)

**Manufacture:** limestone is heated in a **lime kiln**. A current of air carries away the CO₂, so the reversible reaction goes to completion:

$$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$

**Properties:**

- white solid; a **basic oxide**
- reacts **vigorously and exothermically with water** (slaking): the lumps hiss, swell and crumble into powder
- absorbs water and CO₂ from the air

**Uses:** drying agent (for ammonia); making slaked lime; **flux** in steelmaking (removes silica as slag); neutralising acid soils and industrial waste; making glass and calcium carbide.

### Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime)

$$\text{CaO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(s)} \qquad \Delta H \text{ strongly negative}$$

**Properties:**

- white powder; **slightly soluble** in water
- its clear solution is **lime water**, used to test for CO₂
- a **strong base**: neutralises acids; gives ammonia when heated with ammonium salts
- absorbs CO₂: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

**Uses:**

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| **Reducing soil acidity** | Neutralises acids in farm soil |
| **Mortar** | Mixed with sand and water; hardens as it absorbs CO₂ from the air, forming CaCO₃ that binds the sand |
| **Whitewash** | A suspension painted on walls; dries to white calcium carbonate |
| **Water treatment** | Adjusting pH; removing temporary hardness (Clark's method) |
| **Making bleaching powder** | With chlorine |
| **Testing for CO₂** | As lime water |

## Cement and concrete

**Manufacture of Portland cement:**

1. **Limestone and clay** (aluminium silicates) are crushed, mixed and heated to about **1450 °C** in a long, sloping **rotary kiln**.
2. The limestone decomposes and the lime combines with the clay to form **calcium silicates and aluminates**, which come out as hard lumps called **clinker**.
3. The clinker is cooled and **ground** to a fine powder with about **5% gypsum** (calcium sulphate), which **slows the setting** so the cement can be worked.

**Setting:** when water is added, the calcium silicates and aluminates **react with water (hydration)** and form interlocking crystals. The paste hardens over hours and keeps gaining strength for weeks. This is a **chemical** change, which is why concrete must be kept damp ("cured") while it sets.

| Material | Made from | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Cement mortar** | Cement + sand + water | Joining blocks and bricks; plastering walls |
| **Concrete** | Cement + sand + gravel + water | Foundations, floors, columns |
| **Reinforced concrete** | Concrete poured round **steel rods** | Beams, slabs, bridges: concrete resists squeezing, steel resists stretching |
| **Sandcrete blocks** | Cement + sand + water, moulded | Walls of houses |

## Calcium sulphate and plaster of Paris

**Gypsum** is CaSO₄·2H₂O. Heated gently to about 120 °C, it loses three-quarters of its water and becomes **plaster of Paris**:

$$\text{CaSO}_4\cdot2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{CaSO}_4\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O} + 1\tfrac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Mixed with water, plaster of Paris **takes the water back** and sets into a hard mass of gypsum, expanding slightly so it fills a mould exactly.

**Uses:** **casts for broken bones**; moulds and models; ceilings and wall finishes; blackboard chalk; gypsum is also added to cement.

## Calcium chloride, CaCl₂

- **Deliquescent**: absorbs water strongly, so the anhydrous salt is a **drying agent** (not for ammonia).
- Lowers the melting point of NaCl in the Downs cell.
- Used to melt ice on roads in cold countries.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Building in Sierra Leone runs on cement chemistry. **Sandcrete blocks** are moulded from cement, sand and water in block yards, and must be watered for several days while the cement hydrates. Blocks that dry out too quickly crumble. Much of the cement used in the country is made by grinding **imported clinker with gypsum**. Lime **whitewash** is still widely used on walls and tree trunks.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Cement does **not** set by drying out like mud. It sets by a **chemical reaction with water** (hydration), so it needs water to keep hardening. Letting fresh concrete dry too fast makes it weak.
:::

## Key points

- **Limestone cycle:** CaCO₃ → (heat) CaO + CO₂; CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂; Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.
- **Quicklime (CaO):** made in a lime kiln; drying agent, flux, soil treatment.
- **Slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂):** lime water, mortar, whitewash, soil liming, water treatment.
- **Cement:** limestone + clay at 1450 °C → clinker, ground with **gypsum**; sets by **hydration**.
- **Plaster of Paris** (CaSO₄·½H₂O) sets by taking up water: used for **casts and moulds**.
- **CaCl₂:** deliquescent drying agent.

## Quick check

1. Write the equation for the slaking of lime.
2. Why is a current of air passed through a lime kiln?
3. What is the purpose of the gypsum added to cement clinker?
4. How does lime mortar harden?

**Answers:**

1. CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.
2. To carry away the CO₂, so the reversible decomposition goes to completion.
3. It slows down the setting of the cement.
4. The calcium hydroxide absorbs CO₂ from the air and forms calcium carbonate, which binds the sand.
