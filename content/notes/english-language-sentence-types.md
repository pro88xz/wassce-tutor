*"It rained."* *"It rained, so we stayed indoors."* *"Because it rained, we stayed indoors."* *"Because it rained, we stayed indoors, but the others went out."* Four sentences, four structures. WASSCE examiners reward **sentence variety** under Expression (20 marks), and the objective paper asks you to identify sentence types. This lesson teaches the four structural types and the four functional types, shows how to combine and vary sentences, and how to avoid the two sentence errors that cost most marks: fragments and run-ons.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- identify simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences
- identify declarative, interrogative, imperative and exclamatory sentences
- combine short sentences in different ways
- recognise and correct fragments, run-on sentences and comma splices
- vary your sentences to improve your essays

## Sentences by structure

:::callout{type=definition}
| Type | Made of | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | **one** main clause | *The bus left early.* |
| **Compound** | **two or more** main clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or semicolon | *The bus left early, **so** we walked.* |
| **Complex** | **one** main clause + **one or more** subordinate clauses | ***Because the bus left early**, we walked.* |
| **Compound-complex** | **two or more** main clauses + **at least one** subordinate clause | ***Because the bus left early**, we walked, **and** we arrived late.* |
:::

### Simple sentences

One main clause, though it can be long:

> *The tired market women from Kissy walked slowly home in the evening rain.*

Still **simple**: one subject (*women*), one finite verb (*walked*).

### Compound sentences: the FANBOYS

Two main clauses joined by a **coordinating conjunction**, usually with a comma before it:

| **F**or | **A**nd | **N**or | **B**ut | **O**r | **Y**et | **S**o |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| reason | addition | negative addition | contrast | choice | contrast | result |

> *I wanted to go, **but** I had no money.*
> *She studied hard, **so** she passed.*

Or with a **semicolon**, sometimes with a linking adverb:

> *The rain stopped; **however**, the roads stayed flooded.*

### Complex sentences

A main clause plus a **subordinate** clause (see the lesson on phrases and clauses):

> ***When the results came out**, the whole family celebrated.*
> *The teacher **who marked the papers** was impressed.*

### Compound-complex sentences

> ***Although the lorry broke down**, the driver fixed it, **and** we reached Kenema before dark.*

Two main clauses (*the driver fixed it*; *we reached Kenema*) plus one subordinate clause (*although the lorry broke down*).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Identify each sentence type:**

| Sentence | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Musa and Abu played football after school.* | simple | one clause, with a compound subject |
| *Musa played football, but Abu went home.* | compound | two main clauses + *but* |
| *Musa played football because Abu asked him.* | complex | main + adverbial clause of reason |
| *Musa played football because Abu asked him, but he hurt his leg.* | compound-complex | two main clauses + one subordinate |
:::

## Sentences by function

| Type | Purpose | Ends with | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Declarative** | makes a statement | . | *The market opens at six.* |
| **Interrogative** | asks a question | ? | *When does the market open?* |
| **Imperative** | gives a command or request | . or ! | *Close the gate.* / *Please sit down.* |
| **Exclamatory** | expresses strong feeling | ! | *What a crowd there was!* / *How hot it is!* |

:::callout{type=tip}
An **imperative** sentence usually has no visible subject: the subject *you* is understood. *"(You) Close the gate."* It is still a complete sentence.
:::

## The two errors that cost marks

### Fragments

A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but lacking a main clause:

| Fragment | Corrected |
|---|---|
| *Because the road was bad.* | *We were late **because the road was bad**.* |
| *Running to catch the bus.* | ***I fell** while running to catch the bus.* |
| *The man who sold us the radio.* | *The man who sold us the radio **has disappeared**.* |

### Run-ons and comma splices

A **run-on** joins two main clauses with nothing between them; a **comma splice** joins them with only a comma:

> ✗ *It was late we went home.* (run-on)
> ✗ *It was late, we went home.* (comma splice)

Four ways to fix them:

| Fix | Example |
|---|---|
| a **full stop** | *It was late. We went home.* |
| a **comma + conjunction** | *It was late, **so** we went home.* |
| a **semicolon** | *It was late**;** we went home.* |
| **subordination** | ***Because** it was late, we went home.* |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**The comma splice with "however".** *"It rained, however the match continued"* is a comma splice. *However* is not a conjunction. Write: *"It rained**; however,** the match continued."* or *"It rained. **However,** the match continued."*
:::

## Varying your sentences

Examiners reward **variety**: a mix of lengths and structures, and sentences that begin in different ways.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Flat (all simple, all starting with "I"):**

*I went to Lumley Beach on Sunday. I went with my cousins. I swam in the sea. I ate grilled fish. I was very happy.*

**Varied:**

*On Sunday my cousins and I went to Lumley Beach. While the others played football on the sand, I swam out past the waves, and when we finally sat down to eat grilled fish under the palm trees, I was happier than I had been all term. What a day it was!*

The second version uses simple, compound, complex and exclamatory sentences, and starts them with a time phrase, a subordinate clause and *What*.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Identify the type: 'Although she was ill, Isatu sat the examination.'"}
**Complex**: one main clause (*Isatu sat the examination*) + one subordinate clause (*although she was ill*).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Identify the type: 'The rain fell, the wind howled, and the lights went out.'"}
**Compound**: three main clauses joined by commas and *and*.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Correct the comma splice: 'The bell rang, the students rushed out.'"}
*The bell rang, **and** the students rushed out.* / ***When** the bell rang, the students rushed out.* / *The bell rang**;** the students rushed out.*
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Complete the fragment: 'Which made everyone laugh.'"}
*The teacher slipped on the wet floor, **which made everyone laugh**.* (Any main clause that the relative clause can refer to.)
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Combine into one complex sentence: 'The road was repaired. Traders could reach the market.'"}
*Because the road was repaired, traders could reach the market.* / *Once the road was repaired, traders could reach the market.*
:::

## Key points

- **Structure**: simple (one main clause), compound (two or more main clauses), complex (main + subordinate), compound-complex (two main + at least one subordinate).
- **Function**: declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory.
- **Fragments** lack a main clause; **run-ons** and **comma splices** join main clauses wrongly. Fix with a full stop, a conjunction, a semicolon or subordination.
- **Vary** sentence length, structure and openings to earn Expression marks.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The objective paper may ask you to identify a sentence type. Much more importantly, **every essay** is marked for sentence variety and correctness: comma splices and fragments cost **Mechanical accuracy** marks, while varied, controlled sentences earn **Expression** marks. This lesson is worth practising in every piece you write.
:::
