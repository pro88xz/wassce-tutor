A sodium street lamp glows orange; a neon sign glows red; the gas in a fluorescent tube glows blue-violet. Each element emits its own set of colours because its electrons can only sit in particular energy levels. This is the key to identifying elements, even in distant stars.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain energy levels, excitation and ionisation
- relate the energy of an emitted photon to the levels involved, $hf = E_2 - E_1$
- distinguish emission and absorption spectra, and line and continuous spectra
- calculate wavelengths of spectral lines

## Energy levels

In Bohr's model, an electron in an atom can only have certain **definite energies**, called **energy levels**. The lowest is the **ground state**; higher ones are **excited states**. Energies are usually given in **electronvolts (eV)**, where $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J, and are **negative** because the electron is bound (zero energy means just free).

*Figure 1. Energy levels of hydrogen (not to scale). WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Excitation**: an electron absorbs energy and jumps to a **higher** energy level.
- **Ionisation**: the electron absorbs enough energy to **leave the atom completely**. The **ionisation energy** of hydrogen from the ground state is **13.6 eV**.
- **De-excitation**: an excited electron falls back to a **lower** level, **emitting a photon** of light.
:::

## Emission of photons

When an electron falls from a level of energy $E_2$ to a lower level $E_1$, it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference:

$$hf = E_2 - E_1 \qquad \lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E_2 - E_1}$$

where $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J s (Planck's constant) and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s⁻¹.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**An electron in a hydrogen atom falls from the n = 3 level (−1.51 eV) to the n = 2 level (−3.40 eV). Find the wavelength of the photon emitted.**

$$\Delta E = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\ \text{eV} = 1.89 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.02 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

$$\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{\Delta E} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{3.02 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.58 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$$

656 nm: the **red** line of the hydrogen spectrum.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Convert electronvolts to **joules** (× 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) before using $E = hf$ with $h$ in J s. And use the **difference** between levels, not the value of one level.
:::

Large energy gaps give high-frequency (blue, violet or ultraviolet) photons; small gaps give red or infrared ones.

## Types of spectra

| Spectrum | How it is produced | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | hot dense solids, liquids or gases (a lamp filament, the Sun's surface) | all colours, no gaps |
| **Line emission** | a hot **low-pressure gas** (sodium or neon lamp) | bright coloured **lines** on a dark background |
| **Line absorption** | white light passing through a cooler gas | **dark lines** on a continuous spectrum, at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines |

Each element has its own unique set of lines — a "fingerprint". The dark absorption lines in the Sun's spectrum (Fraunhofer lines) showed which elements are present in the Sun; **helium** was discovered in the Sun this way before it was found on Earth.

## Uses of spectra

- **Identifying elements** in chemical samples (flame tests and spectroscopes).
- **Astronomy**: finding the composition, temperature and motion of stars.
- **Lighting**: sodium street lamps, neon signs, fluorescent tubes.
- **Lasers**: light produced by stimulated emission between energy levels.

## Key points

- Electrons occupy definite energy levels; ground state is lowest.
- Excitation: absorb energy, jump up; ionisation: leave the atom (13.6 eV for hydrogen).
- Falling between levels emits a photon: $hf = E_2 - E_1$.
- Line spectra are fingerprints of elements; continuous spectra come from hot dense bodies.

## Quick check

1. What is emitted when an electron falls to a lower energy level?
2. An electron falls through 2.0 eV. Find the energy of the photon in joules.
3. What does a line spectrum show about an element?

**Answers:** 1. A photon of light (electromagnetic radiation). 2. $2.0 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J. 3. That its atoms have definite energy levels; the lines identify the element.
