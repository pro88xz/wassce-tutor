The essay is the single biggest question in Paper 2: **50 marks**, half the paper. Most students write it the way they think it should be written. Strong students write it the way it is **marked**. This lesson explains the WAEC marking scheme for continuous writing and shows what earns marks in each part.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the four parts of the essay marking scheme and their marks
- explain what examiners look for under each part
- recognise the common faults that lose marks
- meet the length requirement with useful words, not padding

## The marking scheme

:::callout{type=definition}
WAEC marks continuous writing (essays, letters, speeches, articles, reports) under four headings:

| Heading | Marks | What it rewards |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | 10 | relevant, well-developed ideas that answer the question |
| **Organisation** | 10 | correct format, clear paragraphs, logical order, introduction and conclusion |
| **Expression** | 20 | vocabulary, sentence variety, fluency, suitable tone |
| **Mechanical accuracy** | 10 | grammar, spelling and punctuation |
:::

Expression is worth the most. That is good news: it is the part you can improve most with practice.

## Content: answer the question asked

Examiners ask: *Has the candidate answered the question, all of it, with enough developed ideas?*

- Read the question twice. If it asks for **causes and solutions**, you need both.
- Plan **four or five** main points. Each becomes a paragraph.
- **Develop** each point: explain it, give an example, show its effect. A list of undeveloped points is thin content.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Question:** *Write a letter to your friend telling them about the changes in your school since they left.*

**Thin content:** "We have a new library. We have a new principal. We have new rules."

**Developed content:** "The biggest change is the new library. The old storeroom behind the science block has been painted and filled with shelves, and we can now borrow two books a week. I have already read three novels this term, which I could never have afforded to buy."
:::

## Organisation: the shape of your writing

Examiners look for:

1. **The correct format** for the type of writing (letter, speech, article, report). Missing an address or a salutation in a letter costs marks here.
2. **A clear introduction** that leads into the topic.
3. **Paragraphs**, one main idea each, in a sensible order.
4. **Linking words** between paragraphs: *firstly, moreover, however, as a result, finally*.
5. **A conclusion** that rounds off the piece.

## Expression: the twenty marks

This is where good writing earns its reward:

- **Vocabulary**: precise, varied words used correctly. *"The market was crowded"* is fine; *"The market heaved with traders and shoppers"* is better.
- **Sentence variety**: mix short and long sentences; start them in different ways.
- **Tone and register** suited to the reader: formal for a principal, friendly for a cousin.
- **Fluency**: ideas flow; nothing is awkward or repeated.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Memorised "big English".** Phrases like *"In this contemporary dispensation of our nation's socio-economic milieu…"* used without meaning do **not** impress examiners. Clear, correct, lively English scores higher than decorated nonsense.
:::

## Mechanical accuracy: the cost of errors

Every error in grammar, spelling and punctuation can cost you. The most common ones:

| Error | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-verb agreement | *"The number of students are rising."* | *"The number of students **is** rising."* |
| Tense shifts | *"He came in and sits down."* | *"He came in and **sat** down."* |
| Comma splice | *"It was late, we went home."* | *"It was late, **so** we went home."* |
| Commonly confused words | *"Their going to Bo."* | *"**They're** going to Bo."* |
| Capital letters | *"freetown, monday, english"* | *"Freetown, Monday, English"* |

Leave a few minutes at the end to read your essay through and correct errors.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Krio influences everyday English in Sierra Leone, which is natural in speech but costs marks in writing. *"I am coming"* to mean *"I will be back soon"*, *"He has been sick since"* without saying since when, or *"I don't know him well, him and me"* are spoken patterns. In the examination, write **standard English**.
:::

## Length

WAEC sets a minimum length for the essay (the question paper states it; it has been **450 words** in recent years). Writing far less loses content and organisation marks. Writing far more gives you more chances to make errors. Aim to reach the minimum comfortably with **developed points**, not repetition.

## Key points

- The essay is worth **50 marks**: **Content 10, Organisation 10, Expression 20, Mechanical accuracy 10**.
- **Content**: answer every part of the question with developed points.
- **Organisation**: correct format, introduction, paragraphs, links, conclusion.
- **Expression**: precise vocabulary, varied sentences, the right tone.
- **Accuracy**: check agreement, tense, punctuation and spelling.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
You choose one essay from several options, so choose the one you can **develop best**, not the one that sounds most impressive. Examiners mark the whole piece against all four headings. A well-organised, accurate essay with clear ideas scores well even without rare vocabulary.
:::

## Quick check

1. Which heading carries the most marks, and how many?
2. A letter has no address. Which heading loses marks?
3. Correct: *"Each of the students have a uniform."*
4. Why is a list of eight undeveloped points weaker than four developed paragraphs?

**Answers:** 1. Expression, 20 marks. 2. Organisation (format). 3. *"Each of the students **has** a uniform."* 4. Content rewards **developed** ideas: explanation and examples, not just a list.
