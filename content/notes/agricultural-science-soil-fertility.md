A **fertile** soil supplies crops with all the nutrients they need, in the right amounts and at the right time. Every harvest carries nutrients away from the farm, and in Sierra Leone's heavy rains more are washed out of the soil. Unless they are replaced, yields fall year after year. This lesson explains the nutrients plants need, the signs of deficiency, how soils lose fertility, and how farmers maintain it with fallowing, rotation, manures, compost, lime and fertilisers, including how to calculate fertiliser amounts.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish soil fertility from soil productivity
- name the macronutrients and micronutrients and state the roles of N, P and K
- describe deficiency symptoms of the main nutrients
- explain how soils lose fertility and how fertility is maintained
- classify fertilisers and describe methods of application
- calculate fertiliser requirements from nutrient percentages
- describe how to make compost

## Fertility and productivity

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Soil fertility** is the ability of a soil to supply plant nutrients in adequate amounts and in the right proportions.
- **Soil productivity** is the ability of a soil to produce a **high yield** of a crop. It depends on fertility **and** on water, good structure, drainage, the absence of pests and toxins, and good management.

A fertile soil is not always productive: a fertile swamp that is badly flooded at the wrong time will not yield well.
:::

## Plant nutrients

**Macronutrients** (needed in large amounts): **nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K)**, calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sulfur (S). Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water.

**Micronutrients (trace elements)** (needed in tiny amounts): iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), boron (B), molybdenum (Mo), chlorine (Cl).

![Roles of nitrogen (leaf growth, green colour), phosphorus (roots, flowering, seeds) and potassium (strong stems, disease resistance, fruit quality), with the signs when each is lacking](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/npk-roles.svg)

| Nutrient | Role | Deficiency signs |
|---|---|---|
| **nitrogen** | leaf and stem growth; chlorophyll; proteins | older leaves turn yellow (chlorosis); stunted growth |
| **phosphorus** | root development; flowering, seed and fruit formation; early maturity | purplish leaves; poor roots; delayed maturity |
| **potassium** | strong stems; disease resistance; starch and sugar formation; fruit and tuber quality | scorched leaf edges; weak stems that fall over (lodging) |
| **calcium** | cell walls; root tips | death of growing points |
| **magnesium** | part of chlorophyll | yellowing between leaf veins |
| **iron** | chlorophyll formation | yellowing of young leaves |

## How soils lose fertility

- **Leaching**: rain washes soluble nutrients below the root zone.
- **Erosion**: removes the fertile topsoil.
- **Crop removal**: every harvest takes nutrients away.
- **Bush burning**: destroys organic matter; nitrogen and sulfur are lost as gases.
- **Continuous cropping** without replacing nutrients; short fallows.
- **Denitrification** in waterlogged soil and **volatilisation** of ammonia from urea left on the surface.

## Maintaining soil fertility

- **Bush fallowing**: resting the land so that natural vegetation restores organic matter.
- **Crop rotation**: alternating crops with different needs and including **legumes**.
- **Legumes**: groundnut, cowpea and pigeon pea fix nitrogen through *Rhizobium*.
- **Organic manures**: **farmyard manure** (animal dung and bedding), **poultry manure**, **green manure** (a crop such as *Mucuna* ploughed in while green), and **compost**.
- **Mulching**: covering the soil with dry grass or leaves; protects it, keeps moisture, and adds organic matter as it decays.
- **Liming** acid soils.
- **Inorganic fertilisers**, used correctly.
- **Controlling erosion** and **avoiding bush burning**.

## Fertilisers

:::callout{type=definition}
| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **organic** | from plant and animal remains | farmyard manure, poultry manure, compost, green manure |
| **inorganic (chemical)** | manufactured | urea, NPK, single superphosphate, muriate of potash |
| **straight** | supplies **one** main nutrient | **urea** (46% N), sulfate of ammonia (21% N), single superphosphate (P), muriate of potash (K) |
| **compound** (mixed) | supplies **two or more** nutrients | **NPK 15-15-15**, NPK 20-10-10 |
:::

The numbers on an NPK bag give the **percentage** of N, of phosphate (P₂O₅) and of potash (K₂O). **NPK 15-15-15** contains 15% of each.

### Organic compared with inorganic

| Organic manure | Inorganic fertiliser |
|---|---|
| improves **structure** and water holding | no effect on structure |
| releases nutrients **slowly** | acts **quickly** |
| low nutrient content; **bulky** to carry | concentrated; easy to transport |
| cheap or free on the farm | costly; must be bought |
| feeds soil organisms | overuse can acidify soil and pollute water |

### Methods of application

- **Broadcasting**: spreading evenly over the whole surface (rice, pasture).
- **Band or side placement**: in a band beside the row of plants.
- **Ring placement**: in a ring around a tree or plant (tree crops, plantain).
- **Top dressing**: applying nitrogen fertiliser to a growing crop (e.g. urea to rice at tillering).
- **Foliar application**: spraying a dilute solution on the leaves.
- **Placement in the planting hole** (basal application) before or at planting.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Putting fertiliser on the leaves or touching the seed.** Concentrated fertiliser **scorches** leaves and roots and can kill seeds. Apply it a few centimetres from the plant, on moist soil, and work it in. Never apply just before heavy rain, which washes it away.
:::

## Fertiliser calculations

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**(a) How much nitrogen is in a 50 kg bag of urea (46% N)? (b) A farmer needs to supply 60 kg of nitrogen to a hectare of rice. How much urea is needed?**

**(a)** $\dfrac{46}{100} \times 50 = 23$ kg of nitrogen.

**(b)** Each kilogram of urea supplies 0.46 kg N, so

$$\text{urea needed} = \frac{60}{0.46} \approx 130 \text{ kg}$$

That is about two and a half 50 kg bags.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A recommendation is 300 kg of NPK 15-15-15 per hectare. (a) How much of each nutrient does this supply per hectare? (b) How much NPK is needed for a 0.4 ha plot?**

**(a)** $\dfrac{15}{100} \times 300 = 45$ kg each of N, P₂O₅ and K₂O.

**(b)** $300 \times 0.4 = 120$ kg, which is $120 \div 50 = 2.4$ bags of 50 kg.
:::

## Making compost

1. Choose a shaded, well-drained site near the garden.
2. Build layers: **plant materials** (grass, leaves, crop residues, kitchen waste) about 20 cm, then **animal manure** or a little fertiliser, then a thin layer of **soil**; repeat.
3. **Water** each layer so the heap is damp but not soaking.
4. Push a stick into the heap to check **heat**: it should become warm inside as microbes work.
5. **Turn** the heap every two to three weeks to let in air.
6. After about **two to three months**, compost is dark, crumbly and earthy-smelling, ready to use.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Poultry manure for vegetable gardens.** Around Freetown and Bo, vegetable growers buy poultry manure from nearby chicken farms. It is rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, improves the sandy garden soils, and costs less than chemical fertiliser. It must be **aged or composted** first, because fresh poultry manure can scorch young plants.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Distinguish between soil fertility and soil productivity."}
**Fertility** is the soil's ability to supply nutrients in adequate amounts and proportions. **Productivity** is its ability to produce a high yield, which also depends on water, structure, drainage and management.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. State the role of phosphorus and one symptom of its deficiency."}
Role: root development, flowering, seed and fruit formation, early maturity. Deficiency: purplish leaves, poor roots, delayed maturity.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Distinguish straight from compound fertilisers, with an example of each."}
A **straight** fertiliser supplies one main nutrient (urea, 46% N). A **compound** fertiliser supplies two or more (NPK 15-15-15).
:::

:::reveal{title="4. How much nitrogen is in 25 kg of sulfate of ammonia (21% N)?"}
$\dfrac{21}{100} \times 25 = 5.25$ kg N.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. Give three advantages of organic manure over inorganic fertiliser."}
Any three: improves soil structure and water holding; releases nutrients slowly; cheap or free; feeds soil organisms; does not acidify or pollute when used properly.
:::

## Key points

- **Fertility** = nutrient supply; **productivity** = yield, which needs fertility plus good conditions.
- Macronutrients **N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S**; micronutrients **Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl**.
- **N** leaves (yellowing if lacking), **P** roots and flowering (purple leaves), **K** stems and disease resistance (scorched edges).
- Fertility is lost by **leaching, erosion, crop removal, burning, continuous cropping**.
- Maintain it with **fallowing, rotation, legumes, manures, compost, mulching, liming, fertilisers**.
- Calculations: nutrient = % × mass of fertiliser; fertiliser needed = nutrient needed ÷ fraction in the fertiliser.
