# Valleys, escarpments and the evolution of landscapes

## Learning objectives
You should be able to distinguish common valley and slope forms, explain how escarpments develop and interpret the combined influence of structure, process and time on landscapes.

## Valleys
A valley is an elongated low area between higher ground, commonly occupied or formerly occupied by a river or glacier.

A **V-shaped valley** is usually associated with river downcutting and slope weathering in the upper course. A **U-shaped valley** is characteristic of glacial erosion: a moving glacier widens and deepens a pre-existing valley, producing a broad floor and steep sides. A **gorge** is a narrow, steep-sided valley that may form through rapid river incision or waterfall retreat.

A **rift valley** is a tectonic depression formed when crustal blocks move apart and the central block subsides between faults. It differs from a river valley because its primary origin is tectonic rather than fluvial erosion.

## Escarpments
An escarpment is a long, relatively steep slope or cliff separating areas at different elevations. It may form through faulting, differential erosion of alternating rock layers or the retreat of a steep slope over time.

Where hard rock overlies softer rock, the softer layer may be eroded more rapidly. The resistant layer forms a cap, while undercutting and weathering cause the steep face to retreat. The gentler dip slope may follow the inclination of the resistant strata, producing a cuesta where rock layers are tilted.

## Structural control and differential erosion
The geological structure of an area affects the form of its landscape. Resistant rocks may form ridges and high ground; weaker rocks may be lowered into valleys. Faults, joints and bedding planes guide river courses and weathering. The same river can cut deeply through a resistant layer where gradient and discharge are high, then widen its valley across weaker material.

## Landscape evolution
Landscapes change continuously. Tectonic uplift may increase river gradients and encourage incision. Weathering weakens rock, while rivers, wind, ice and waves remove and transport sediment. Deposition builds floodplains, fans and coastal features. Vegetation and human land use can alter rates of erosion and sediment supply.

Older models sometimes describe landscapes as progressing through fixed youthful, mature and old stages. These models can help organise description, but real landscapes do not all follow one universal sequence. Climate, tectonic activity, rock type and changing base level produce different histories.

## Reading a landscape profile
A cross-section shows changes in elevation along a chosen line. A steep slope is represented by a rapid change in height over a short horizontal distance; a gentle slope changes height more gradually. When drawing a profile from a contour map, keep horizontal distance and vertical height scales clear and label the vertical exaggeration if the scales differ.

## WASSCE examination focus
Use the origin of a landform to distinguish similar-looking features. A rift valley is tectonic; a V-shaped valley is commonly fluvial; a U-shaped valley is glacial. When explaining an escarpment, refer to structure, differential erosion and slope retreat.
