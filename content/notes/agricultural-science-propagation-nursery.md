Every crop starts from **planting material**: a seed, a stem cutting, a sucker, a tuber or a budded seedling. Good planting material is the cheapest way to raise yields; poor or diseased material wastes all the work that follows. Many crops, from tomatoes and peppers to cocoa, oil palm and citrus, are first raised in a **nursery**, where young plants get special care before going to the field. This lesson explains sexual and vegetative propagation, seed viability and germination tests, budding, grafting and layering, and how to establish and manage a nursery.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- distinguish sexual and asexual (vegetative) propagation, with examples
- state the advantages and disadvantages of each
- explain seed viability, dormancy and germination tests
- describe cuttings, layering, budding and grafting
- describe the establishment and management of a nursery

## Two ways to propagate plants

![Plant propagation divides into sexual (seeds: maize, rice, cocoa, tomato) and asexual or vegetative, which includes natural organs (tubers, rhizomes, corms, suckers) and artificial methods (cuttings, layering, budding, grafting)](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/propagation-methods.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Sexual propagation** uses **seeds**, formed by fertilisation of flowers.
- **Asexual (vegetative) propagation** uses a **part of the parent plant** (stem, root, leaf, bud) to produce a new plant. The new plant is genetically **identical** to the parent (a clone).
:::

| | Sexual (seeds) | Vegetative |
|---|---|---|
| **advantages** | seeds are cheap, light, easy to store and transport; few diseases are carried in seed; can produce new varieties (variation) | plants are **true to type**; mature and fruit **earlier**; used for crops that set few or no viable seeds (cassava, plantain, pineapple); good characteristics are kept |
| **disadvantages** | offspring may differ from parents; some take long to mature (e.g. mango from seed); some seeds are dormant or short-lived | planting material is bulky and perishable; **diseases (especially viruses) are carried on**; no new variation |

### Vegetative organs and methods

| Method or organ | Crop examples |
|---|---|
| **stem cuttings** | cassava, sweet potato (vine cuttings), sugarcane (setts) |
| **tubers and setts** | yam (setts or minisetts), Irish potato |
| **suckers** | plantain, banana, pineapple (also crowns and slips) |
| **corms** | cocoyam |
| **rhizomes** | ginger |
| **budding** | citrus (orange, lime), rubber |
| **grafting** | mango, avocado, cashew |
| **layering** | many fruit and ornamental trees |
| **tissue culture** | plantain, banana, cassava, pineapple: disease-free plantlets |

## Seeds: viability, dormancy and germination

- **Viability**: the ability of a seed to germinate. Old or badly stored seeds lose viability.
- **Dormancy**: a period when a viable seed will not germinate even in good conditions, because of a hard coat, immature embryo or chemical inhibitors. Broken by **scarification** (nicking or rubbing the seed coat), **soaking** in water, or heat treatment.
- **Seed rate** depends on germination percentage, seed size, spacing and planting method.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A farmer tests 200 maize seeds on damp paper. After 7 days, 170 have germinated. Find the germination percentage. Is the seed good enough?**

$$\text{germination percentage} = \frac{170}{200} \times 100 = 85\%$$

Certified maize seed usually needs at least about 80–85% germination, so this seed is acceptable. If it were much lower, the farmer would need to plant more seed per hole or buy new seed.
:::

## Budding, grafting and layering

![T-budding: a T-cut in the rootstock bark, a bud with a bark shield, inserted and tied. Cleft grafting: the stock cut and split, the scion cut to a wedge, inserted, tied and sealed](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/budding-grafting.svg)

:::callout{type=definition}
- The **rootstock** (stock) is the rooted lower part, chosen for vigour and disease resistance.
- The **scion** is the bud or shoot taken from a plant with good fruit qualities.
- **Budding**: a single **bud** is joined to the rootstock (T-budding in citrus).
- **Grafting**: a **shoot** (scion with several buds) is joined to the rootstock (cleft, whip or side grafting in mango).
- **Layering**: a branch is made to root **while still attached** to the parent, then cut off and planted. In **air layering**, a ring of bark is removed, the wound is wrapped in moist soil or coir and plastic, and roots form there.
:::

**Conditions for success:** the stock and scion must be **compatible** (closely related); their **cambium** layers must touch; cuts must be clean with a sharp knife; the union must be tied firmly and protected from drying; the work is best done when plants are actively growing.

**Advantages:** combines a strong, disease-resistant root system with good fruiting; early fruiting; true-to-type fruit; can change the variety of an old tree.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Leaving the tie on too long, or letting the rootstock shoots grow.** Once the bud or graft has taken, the tape must be loosened so it does not strangle the stem, and shoots growing from the rootstock below the union must be removed, or they will take over.
:::

## The nursery

A **nursery** is a place where seedlings or young plants are raised and cared for until they are ready for transplanting.

**Why use a nursery?** Small seeds need fine care; seedlings can be protected from sun, heavy rain, pests and weeds; only the strongest are transplanted; land is used efficiently while seedlings grow.

**Types:** nursery **beds** (rice, vegetables); **seed boxes** or trays (tomato, pepper, cabbage); **polybags** or pots (cocoa, oil palm, citrus, coffee).

### Establishing and managing a nursery

1. **Site:** near a water source, well drained, fertile, partly shaded, protected from animals, easy to reach.
2. **Prepare the bed or bags:** fine, weed-free topsoil mixed with compost or manure; beds about 1 m wide so they can be reached from both sides.
3. **Sow** seeds at the right depth and spacing, or in rows; cover lightly.
4. **Shade:** a roof of palm fronds or shade netting to protect from strong sun and heavy rain.
5. **Water** regularly, morning and evening, with a fine rose watering can.
6. **Thin** and **prick out** (transfer crowded seedlings to wider spacing or to bags).
7. **Control** weeds, pests (e.g. ants, crickets) and **damping off** (avoid overwatering).
8. **Harden off**: gradually reduce shade and water before transplanting, so seedlings adjust to field conditions.
9. **Transplant** in the cool of the evening or on a cloudy day, with a ball of soil round the roots; water immediately.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Swamp rice nurseries.** Rice farmers in inland valley swamps raise seedlings in a small, well-prepared nursery bed and transplant them at about **3 weeks old**, two or three seedlings per hill. This uses less seed than broadcasting, gives an even stand that is easier to weed, and lets the main field be prepared while the seedlings grow.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of vegetative propagation."}
Advantages: true to type; early maturity; used for crops without viable seed. Disadvantages: bulky, perishable planting material; carries diseases (especially viruses); no new variation (any two of each).
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Out of 50 cowpea seeds, 42 germinated. Find the germination percentage."}
$\dfrac{42}{50} \times 100 = 84\%$.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Distinguish between budding and grafting."}
**Budding** joins a single bud to the rootstock; **grafting** joins a shoot (scion) with several buds.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. State three conditions for successful grafting."}
Any three: compatible stock and scion; cambium layers in contact; clean cuts with a sharp knife; firm tying; protection from drying; done when plants are actively growing.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. What is hardening off, and why is it done?"}
Gradually reducing shade and watering before transplanting, so that seedlings adapt to the harsher conditions in the field and suffer less transplanting shock.
:::

## Key points

- **Sexual** propagation uses seeds; **vegetative** propagation uses plant parts and gives identical offspring.
- Know the method for each crop: **cassava cuttings, plantain suckers, yam setts, citrus budding, mango grafting**.
- **Germination %** $= \dfrac{\text{seeds germinated}}{\text{seeds tested}} \times 100$; dormancy is broken by scarification or soaking.
- **Budding/grafting** join a scion to a rootstock: compatible, cambium in contact, firmly tied.
- **Nursery**: good site, fine soil, shade, regular watering, pricking out, hardening off, careful transplanting.
