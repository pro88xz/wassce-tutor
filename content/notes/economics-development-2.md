A country cannot build every road, school and power station at once. Development planning decides what to do first, how to pay for it and how to measure progress. This lesson covers what a development plan is, the types of plan, why plans often fail in West Africa, and the role of foreign aid.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define development planning and state its objectives
- describe the types of development plan
- describe the stages in preparing a plan
- explain the problems of development planning in West Africa
- assess the merits and demerits of foreign aid

## What is development planning?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Development planning** is a deliberate, coordinated effort by the government to decide the **targets** of economic and social development, the **projects** and **policies** needed to reach them, and the **resources** required, over a stated period.
:::

**Objectives of development planning:**

1. To achieve **rapid economic growth** and raise income per head.
2. To **reduce unemployment** and poverty.
3. To **reduce inequality** between people and regions.
4. To **diversify** the economy away from one or two exports.
5. To **improve social services**: education, health, water and housing.
6. To develop **infrastructure**.
7. To achieve **price stability** and a sound balance of payments.
8. To make the **best use of scarce resources** by setting priorities.

## Types of plan

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Short-term plan** | One to two years, often the annual budget |
| **Medium-term plan** | Usually three to five years, with specific targets and projects |
| **Long-term (perspective) plan** | Ten to twenty years or more, setting broad national goals |
| **Rolling plan** | A plan revised every year, adding a new year as one is completed |
| **Comprehensive plan** | Covers the whole economy, public and private sectors |
| **Partial (sectoral) plan** | Covers only some sectors, such as agriculture or education |
| **Imperative (directive) plan** | Targets are compulsory, as in a command economy |
| **Indicative plan** | Targets guide and encourage the private sector but are not compulsory |

## Stages in preparing a plan

1. **Survey** the economy: collect data on resources, population, output and problems.
2. **Set objectives and targets** (for example 6% growth a year, 90% primary enrolment).
3. **Estimate the resources** available: domestic revenue, saving, aid and loans.
4. **Choose projects and policies**, and set priorities.
5. **Implement** the plan through ministries, agencies and the budget.
6. **Monitor and evaluate** progress, and revise the plan as needed.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's **Medium-Term National Development Plan 2019–2023** made **human capital development**, especially free quality school education, its flagship. The government's next plan, for **2024–2030**, is built around priorities that include **Feed Salone** (agriculture and food security), human capital, youth employment, and the public service and technology. Each plan sets targets, lists programmes and is funded through the annual budget and development partners.
:::

## Problems of development planning in West Africa

1. **Inadequate and unreliable statistics**, so targets rest on poor data.
2. **Shortage of finance**: low revenue, so plans depend on uncertain foreign aid.
3. **Shortage of skilled planners and administrators** to carry plans out.
4. **Political instability and changes of government**, which abandon plans halfway.
5. **Corruption and mismanagement** of funds.
6. **Over-ambitious targets** that do not match resources.
7. **Lack of cooperation** from the private sector and the public.
8. **External shocks**: falling export prices, oil price rises, epidemics, floods.
9. **Poor coordination** between ministries and between national and local government.
10. **Weak monitoring and evaluation**, so failures are not corrected.

## Foreign aid

:::callout{type=definition}
**Foreign aid** is the transfer of resources (money, goods, technical help) to a country on **concessional terms**: as grants, or as loans with low interest and long repayment periods.
:::

**Forms of aid:** **bilateral** (from one government to another) or **multilateral** (through international bodies such as the World Bank, the UN and the AfDB); **grants** or **soft loans**; **tied** aid (must be spent on goods from the donor country) or **untied**; **project aid**, **budget support**, **food aid** and **technical assistance**.

| Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| Fills the gap between saving and investment | Loans must be repaid, with interest, adding to debt |
| Brings foreign exchange for imports | Tied aid forces purchases from the donor at high prices |
| Supports health, education and infrastructure | Conditions may restrict a country's policy choices |
| Brings technical skills and technology | Can create dependence and weaken local effort |
| Helps in emergencies (Ebola, floods) | May be misused or lost to corruption |
| | Projects may not suit local needs |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Not all aid is a "gift". **Loans** must be repaid, often in foreign currency, and add to the national debt. Only **grants** are free.
:::

## Key points

- **Development planning** sets targets, projects, policies and resources for development over a period.
- Plans can be **short, medium or long term**, **rolling**, **comprehensive or partial**, **imperative or indicative**.
- Stages: **survey, objectives, resources, projects, implementation, evaluation**.
- Planning in West Africa is hampered by **poor data, lack of finance and skills, instability and corruption**.
- **Foreign aid** brings capital and skills but also **debt, conditions and dependence**.

## Quick check

1. What is a rolling plan?
2. Give three objectives of development planning.
3. State two problems of development planning in West Africa.
4. What is tied aid, and why can it be a disadvantage?

**Answers:** 1. A plan revised every year, with a new year added as each year is completed. 2. Any three: rapid growth, less unemployment and poverty, less inequality, diversification, better social services, infrastructure, price stability, best use of resources. 3. Any two: poor statistics, shortage of finance, shortage of skilled staff, instability, corruption, over-ambitious targets, external shocks, poor coordination. 4. Aid that must be spent on goods or services from the donor country; it may be dearer or less suitable than goods from elsewhere.
