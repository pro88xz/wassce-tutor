## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a market and classify market structures
- state the features of perfect competition
- explain short-run and long-run equilibrium of a perfectly competitive firm, using the rule MC = MR

## Markets and market structures

:::callout{type=definition}
A **market** is any arrangement through which **buyers and sellers** come into contact to exchange goods and services. It does not need a physical place: mobile money transfers and online sales are markets too.

**Market structure** describes the **degree of competition** in a market: the number of sellers, the type of product and how easily firms can enter.
:::

| Structure | Number of sellers | Product | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect competition** | Very many | Identical | Free |
| **Monopolistic competition** | Many | Differentiated | Fairly free |
| **Oligopoly** | A few | Identical or differentiated | Difficult |
| **Monopoly** | One | No close substitute | Blocked |

## Features of perfect competition

1. **Very many buyers and sellers**: no single one can affect the price.
2. **Homogeneous (identical) products**: buyers cannot tell one seller's product from another's.
3. **Free entry and exit** of firms.
4. **Perfect knowledge** of prices and conditions.
5. **Perfect mobility** of factors of production.
6. **No transport costs** and **no government interference**.

Because of these features, each firm is a **price taker**: it must accept the price set by the industry's demand and supply.

## Demand and revenue for the firm

The firm can sell **any quantity** at the market price, so its demand curve is **perfectly elastic (horizontal)**. Every unit sells for the same price, so:

$$\text{Price} = \text{AR} = \text{MR}$$

## The profit-maximising rule

:::callout{type=definition}
A firm **maximises profit** where **marginal cost equals marginal revenue (MC = MR)**, with MC rising.
:::

If the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost (MR > MC), producing it raises profit. If it adds more to cost (MC > MR), it lowers profit.

## Worked example: three possible prices

We use the cost figures from *Concepts of Cost and Revenue*: fixed cost NLe 100.

| Output | Total cost | ATC | AVC | MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 140 | 140 | 40 | 40 |
| 2 | 170 | 85 | 35 | 30 |
| 3 | 190 | 63.3 | 30 | 20 |
| 4 | 220 | 55 | 30 | 30 |
| 5 | 270 | 54 | 34 | 50 |
| 6 | 340 | 56.7 | 40 | 70 |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
The firm keeps adding units while MC is below MR, and stops before a unit whose MC is above MR.

**A. Abnormal profit (price NLe 65).** The 5th unit costs 50 (below 65); the 6th would cost 70 (above 65). Produce **5 units**.
TR $= 65 \times 5 = 325$, TC $= 270$, profit $= 325 - 270 = \text{NLe } 55$.
Price (65) is above ATC (54), so the firm earns **abnormal (supernormal) profit**: $(65 - 54) \times 5 = 55$.

**B. Normal profit (price NLe 54).** Again produce **5 units** (the 5th costs 50, the 6th would cost 70).
TR $= 54 \times 5 = 270 = $ TC. Price equals minimum ATC, so the firm earns **normal profit** only.

**C. Loss (price NLe 45).** The 4th unit costs 30 (below 45); the 5th would cost 50 (above 45). Produce **4 units**.
TR $= 45 \times 4 = 180$, TC $= 220$, loss $= \text{NLe } 40$.
:::

## Should a loss-making firm close down?

In the **short run**, fixed costs (NLe 100) must be paid even if the firm produces nothing. In case C:

- Producing 4 units: loss of NLe 40.
- Closing down: loss of NLe 100 (the fixed cost).

The firm should **keep producing** as long as **price covers average variable cost** (45 is above the AVC of 30 at 4 units). If the price fell **below minimum AVC (NLe 30)**, it should **shut down**.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Normal profit** is the minimum profit needed to keep a firm in an industry; it is included in average cost. **Abnormal (supernormal) profit** is any profit above normal profit.
:::

## Long-run equilibrium

Abnormal profit in case A attracts **new firms** (entry is free). Industry supply increases, so the market price falls. This continues until price falls to **minimum ATC** (case B), where only normal profit is earned. Losses in case C drive some firms **out**, supply falls and price rises back to the same point.

In the long run, a perfectly competitive firm is in equilibrium where:

$$\text{P} = \text{AR} = \text{MR} = \text{MC} = \text{minimum ATC}$$

:::callout{type=local-example}
Perfect competition is a model rather than a real market, but a large market for a single farm crop, such as tomatoes or pepper, comes close: many sellers, almost identical produce, and no single trader able to set the price.
:::

## Key points

- Perfect competition: **many firms**, **identical products**, **free entry**, **perfect knowledge**.
- The firm is a **price taker**: P = AR = MR (horizontal demand curve).
- Profit is maximised where **MC = MR**.
- Short run: abnormal profit, normal profit or loss. Keep producing while **P ≥ AVC**.
- Long run: only **normal profit**, at **P = minimum ATC**.

## Quick check

1. Why is a perfectly competitive firm called a price taker?
2. State the profit-maximising rule.
3. In the long run, why can a perfectly competitive firm earn only normal profit?

**Answers:** 1. It is too small to influence the market price and must accept the price set by the industry. 2. Produce where MC = MR (with MC rising). 3. Abnormal profit attracts new firms, which increases supply and lowers the price until only normal profit remains.
