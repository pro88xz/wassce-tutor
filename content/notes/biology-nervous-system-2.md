# The nervous system: the brain, spinal cord and coordination

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the major regions of the human brain
- relate the functions of the cerebrum, cerebellum and medulla oblongata to their structures
- describe the protection of the brain and spinal cord
- explain the role of the spinal cord in reflexes and communication

- explain how the nervous system coordinates responses to stimuli

## The brain as a control centre

The brain is the main coordinating organ of the central nervous system. It receives information from sensory receptors, interprets it, stores and retrieves information, and sends instructions to effectors. Different regions are specialised for different functions, but they work together rather than operating as completely independent units.

The major regions commonly studied at this level include the **cerebrum**, **cerebellum** and **medulla oblongata**. Other structures, including the hypothalamus, help regulate internal conditions.

## Major parts of the brain

### Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest part of the human brain. It consists of two cerebral hemispheres joined by nerve fibres. Its outer layer, the cerebral cortex, contains grey matter and has many folds that increase its surface area.

Important functions include:

- interpreting sensory information from the eyes, ears, skin and other receptors
- initiating and controlling voluntary movements
- memory, learning, reasoning and judgement
- language, thought and conscious awareness

Different areas of the cerebral cortex have specialised roles. Sensory areas receive and interpret particular kinds of information, while motor areas help control voluntary muscle activity. The two hemispheres communicate and cooperate in normal activity.

### Cerebellum

The cerebellum lies below and towards the back of the cerebrum. It helps coordinate voluntary muscular activity, maintain posture and balance, and make movements smooth and accurate.

The cerebellum does not normally initiate a voluntary movement by itself. Instead, it helps adjust the timing and force of muscle contractions using information about body position and movement. Damage can cause poor balance, uncoordinated movements and difficulty performing precise actions.

### Medulla oblongata

The medulla oblongata is part of the brainstem and connects the brain with the spinal cord. It contains centres involved in regulating important involuntary activities, including breathing, heart rate and blood-vessel diameter. It also participates in reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing.

Because it helps regulate vital functions, severe damage to the medulla can be life-threatening.

### Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus is a small region at the base of the brain. It helps maintain homeostasis by regulating body temperature, hunger, thirst and aspects of water balance. It links nervous control with endocrine control through its influence on the pituitary gland.

### Summary of brain regions

| Region | Main functions | What to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Cerebrum | Conscious thought, memory, interpretation of sensations and voluntary movement | Largest region; higher mental activities |
| Cerebellum | Balance, posture and coordination of movement | Makes movements smooth and accurate |
| Medulla oblongata | Regulation of breathing, heart rate and other involuntary activities | Part of brainstem; vital automatic functions |
| Hypothalamus | Temperature, hunger, thirst and homeostatic regulation | Links nervous and endocrine control |

## Protection of the brain

The brain is delicate and is protected by several structures:

1. **Cranium:** the bony part of the skull surrounds and protects the brain.
2. **Meninges:** three protective membranes surround the brain and spinal cord.
3. **Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF):** fluid around the brain and spinal cord acts as a shock absorber, provides buoyancy and helps maintain a stable environment.
4. **Blood-brain barrier:** specialised capillaries restrict the passage of many substances from the blood into brain tissue, helping protect the nervous system from harmful chemicals and fluctuations.

These protective arrangements reduce the risk of injury, but they do not make the brain immune to trauma.

## The spinal cord

The spinal cord is a long column of nervous tissue extending from the brainstem through the vertebral canal. It is protected by the vertebral column, meninges and cerebrospinal fluid.

Its main functions are:

- carrying sensory impulses from the body towards the brain
- carrying motor instructions from the brain to muscles and glands
- coordinating many spinal reflex actions

In a cross-section, the spinal cord has central grey matter surrounded by white matter. Grey matter contains many neurone cell bodies and synapses. White matter contains bundles of nerve fibres, many of which are covered by myelin and form ascending and descending pathways.

Spinal nerves connect the spinal cord to different parts of the body. Each spinal nerve is associated with a sensory root and a motor root. The sensory root carries information into the spinal cord; the motor root carries instructions out.

## Coordination: from stimulus to response

Consider what happens when a person sees a ball approaching.

1. Light reflected from the ball enters the eye and stimulates photoreceptors in the retina.
2. Sensory impulses travel along the optic nerve to visual areas of the brain.
3. The brain interprets the information, including the ball's direction and movement.
4. The motor areas of the brain organise an appropriate voluntary response.
5. Motor impulses travel through the spinal cord and peripheral nerves to skeletal muscles.
6. Muscles contract in a coordinated sequence so the person can move to catch or avoid the ball.

This example shows that a response may involve several receptors, brain regions, pathways and effectors. Coordination is not simply the action of one isolated organ.

## Voluntary, involuntary and reflex control

- **Voluntary actions** are performed with conscious intention, such as writing or choosing to walk.
- **Involuntary activities** occur without conscious direction, such as the normal regulation of heart rate and the movement of food through much of the digestive tract.
- **Reflex actions** are rapid, automatic responses to particular stimuli. They may be coordinated by the spinal cord, as in withdrawal of a hand, or by the brainstem, as in some protective reflexes.

Do not use these terms as if they mean exactly the same thing. Involuntary regulation is a broad category; a reflex is a specific automatic response through a neural pathway.

## Maintaining homeostasis through nervous control

The nervous system helps maintain stable internal conditions by detecting changes and coordinating corrective responses. For example, thermoreceptors detect changes in temperature. The hypothalamus helps coordinate responses such as sweating and changes in blood flow near the skin. Sweating increases heat loss as water evaporates; widening skin blood vessels increases heat transfer to the surroundings.

Nervous responses are generally rapid and often short-lived. Hormonal responses may be slower to begin but can last longer. The two systems cooperate: the hypothalamus, for example, influences hormone release through the pituitary gland.

## Quick check

1. Which part of the brain is most directly involved in balance and coordination?
2. State two functions of the medulla oblongata.
3. Give three ways in which the brain is protected.
4. State two functions of the spinal cord.
5. Why is the cerebellum important when a person is learning to ride a bicycle?
6. Distinguish a reflex action from a general involuntary activity.

**Answers**

1. The cerebellum.
2. Regulation of breathing and heart rate; it also participates in swallowing, coughing and other reflexes.
3. The cranium, meninges and cerebrospinal fluid.
4. It carries sensory impulses to the brain, carries motor instructions from the brain, and coordinates spinal reflexes.
5. It helps coordinate posture, balance, timing and accuracy of muscular activity.
6. A reflex is a rapid, automatic response to a particular stimulus through a neural pathway; involuntary activity is a broader category of activity that is not consciously directed.
