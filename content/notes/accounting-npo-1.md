**Non-profit organisations**, such as **clubs, societies, associations and charities**, are formed to provide a service to members or the public, **not to make a profit**. Their final accounts therefore use different names: a **receipts and payments account** (a summary of the cash book), an **income and expenditure account** (the equivalent of an income statement, showing a **surplus** or **deficit**), and a **statement of financial position** in which capital is called the **accumulated fund**. This lesson explains these accounts and the differences between them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the features of non-profit organisations and their accounts
- prepare a receipts and payments account
- distinguish a receipts and payments account from an income and expenditure account
- explain the accumulated fund, surplus and deficit
- explain the treatment of subscriptions, donations, entrance fees and bar trading

## Terms used by non-profit organisations

| Business term | Non-profit term |
|---|---|
| income statement | **income and expenditure account** |
| net profit | **surplus** (excess of income over expenditure) |
| net loss | **deficit** (excess of expenditure over income) |
| capital | **accumulated fund** |
| cash book summary | **receipts and payments account** |

## Receipts and payments account

![A receipts and payments account summarises all cash in and out, capital and revenue, and its balance is cash in hand; an income and expenditure account includes only revenue items of the period, adjusted for accruals, prepayments and depreciation, and its balance is a surplus or deficit](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/receipts-vs-income.svg)

*Figure 1. Receipts and payments account compared with income and expenditure account. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Kono Youth Sports Club, year ended 31 December 2026 (NLe). Cash at 1 January 4,000. Receipts: subscriptions 18,000; donations 5,000; bar sales 30,000. Payments: bar purchases 21,000; wages 9,000; rent 6,000; new sports equipment 8,000. Prepare the receipts and payments account.**

| Receipts | NLe |
|---|---|
| Balance b/d | 4,000 |
| Subscriptions | 18,000 |
| Donations | 5,000 |
| Bar sales | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **57,000** |

| Payments | NLe |
|---|---|
| Bar purchases | 21,000 |
| Wages | 9,000 |
| Rent | 6,000 |
| Sports equipment | 8,000 |
| Balance c/d | 13,000 |
| **Total** | **57,000** |

Balance $= 57\,000 - 44\,000 = 13\,000$ cash in hand at 31 December.
:::

## Differences

| Receipts and payments account | Income and expenditure account |
|---|---|
| summary of the **cash book** | like an **income statement** |
| records **all** cash, capital and revenue | records **revenue** items only |
| includes amounts for **other periods** | only items for **this period** (accruals concept) |
| no depreciation, accruals or prepayments | includes **depreciation, accruals, prepayments** |
| balance is **cash in hand** (debit) | balance is a **surplus or deficit** |
| starts with an opening cash balance | has no opening balance |

## Special items

- **Subscriptions:** members' fees, adjusted for amounts **in arrears** (owing) and **in advance** (prepaid) through a **subscriptions account** (next lesson).
- **Donations and legacies:** small, regular ones are **income**; large ones for a special purpose may be **capitalised** (added to the accumulated fund or a special fund).
- **Entrance (joining) fees:** treated as income, or capitalised, depending on the club's policy.
- **Bar or canteen:** trading activities are shown in a separate **bar trading account**, and only the **bar profit** goes to the income and expenditure account.

## The accumulated fund

$$\text{Accumulated fund} = \text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities}$$

At the start of the first year it is found with a **statement of affairs**, as for incomplete records. Each year's **surplus** is added and any **deficit** deducted.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Football clubs, youth associations, church and mosque societies, and school old-pupils' associations across Sierra Leone collect dues and run events. Treasurers usually keep only a cash book; turning it into a **receipts and payments account** and then an **income and expenditure account** shows members exactly what the year's activities cost and earned.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not put **purchase of equipment** in the income and expenditure account. It is a **capital** payment: it appears in the receipts and payments account, but in the income and expenditure account only its **depreciation** is charged; the equipment itself goes in the statement of financial position.
:::

## Key points

- Non-profit organisations report a **surplus or deficit**, not a profit or loss; capital is the **accumulated fund**.
- **Receipts and payments account:** summary of cash; balance is **cash in hand**.
- **Income and expenditure account:** revenue items for the period only; balance is **surplus or deficit**.
- Bar trading is shown separately; only **bar profit** enters the income and expenditure account.

## Quick check

1. What is the accumulated fund?
2. What is the balance of a receipts and payments account?
3. Give two differences between a receipts and payments account and an income and expenditure account.
4. How is the profit of a club bar shown?
5. Is new equipment shown in the income and expenditure account?

**Answers:**

1. The capital of a non-profit organisation: total assets minus total liabilities.
2. The cash in hand (and at bank) at the end of the period.
3. Any two: one summarises cash, the other is like an income statement; one includes capital items, the other only revenue items; one includes items for other periods, the other only this period's; one has no depreciation or accruals, the other does; one shows cash in hand, the other a surplus or deficit.
4. In a separate bar trading account, with only the bar profit taken to the income and expenditure account.
5. No: only its depreciation is charged; the equipment itself appears in the statement of financial position.
