The **effect of transactions on the accounting equation** is that every transaction changes **at least two items**, yet the equation **Assets = Capital + Liabilities** stays in balance. Some transactions increase both sides, some decrease both sides, and some simply swap one asset for another. Working through a list of transactions and showing the equation after each one is a favourite WASSCE exercise, because it proves the **dual aspect** of every transaction and leads directly to double entry. This lesson works through the main types of transaction step by step.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the dual aspect of transactions
- show the effect of transactions on assets, liabilities and capital
- prepare a table showing the equation after each transaction
- explain how income and expenses affect capital
- draw up a simple statement of financial position from the final equation

## The four kinds of effect

| Effect | Example | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **Both sides increase** | owner pays NLe 20,000 into the business bank account | assets ↑ (bank), capital ↑ |
| **Both sides increase** | buy stock on credit for NLe 5,000 | assets ↑ (stock), liabilities ↑ (trade payables) |
| **Both sides decrease** | pay a supplier NLe 3,000 by cheque | assets ↓ (bank), liabilities ↓ (trade payables) |
| **One asset up, another down** | buy furniture for NLe 4,000 cash | assets ↑ (furniture) and ↓ (cash): no change in totals |
| **One liability up, another down** | a supplier agrees to turn a NLe 6,000 debt into a loan | liabilities ↑ (loan) and ↓ (trade payables) |

Income and expenses affect **capital**: income (a profit) increases capital; expenses and drawings reduce it.

## Worked example: a month of transactions

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Hawa starts a provisions shop in Kenema. Show the effect of each transaction on the accounting equation (NLe).**

1. Starts the business with NLe 50,000 paid into a business bank account.
2. Buys shop fittings by cheque, NLe 12,000.
3. Buys goods (stock) on credit from Sesay Wholesalers, NLe 15,000.
4. Takes a bank loan of NLe 20,000.
5. Sells goods costing NLe 6,000 for NLe 9,000 cash.
6. Pays Sesay Wholesalers NLe 10,000 by cheque.
7. Pays rent in cash, NLe 1,000.
8. Takes NLe 500 cash for her own use.

| No. | Assets | Liabilities | Capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | bank +50,000 | | +50,000 |
| 2 | fittings +12,000; bank −12,000 | | |
| 3 | stock +15,000 | payables +15,000 | |
| 4 | bank +20,000 | loan +20,000 | |
| 5 | cash +9,000; stock −6,000 | | +3,000 (profit) |
| 6 | bank −10,000 | payables −10,000 | |
| 7 | cash −1,000 | | −1,000 (expense) |
| 8 | cash −500 | | −500 (drawings) |

**Final position:**

- Fittings $= 12\,000$
- Stock $= 15\,000 - 6\,000 = 9\,000$
- Bank $= 50\,000 - 12\,000 + 20\,000 - 10\,000 = 48\,000$
- Cash $= 9\,000 - 1\,000 - 500 = 7\,500$
- **Total assets** $= 12\,000 + 9\,000 + 48\,000 + 7\,500 = 76\,500$
- Liabilities: loan $20\,000$ + payables $5\,000 = 25\,000$
- Capital $= 50\,000 + 3\,000 - 1\,000 - 500 = 51\,500$

Check: $51\,500 + 25\,000 = 76\,500$ ✓
:::

## From equation to statement of financial position

The final equation can be set out as a simple **statement of financial position**:

| Hawa's Provisions: statement of financial position | NLe |
|---|---|
| **Non-current assets:** shop fittings | 12,000 |
| **Current assets:** inventory 9,000; bank 48,000; cash 7,500 | 64,500 |
| **Total assets** | **76,500** |
| **Capital:** 50,000 + profit 3,000 − rent 1,000 − drawings 500 | 51,500 |
| **Non-current liability:** bank loan | 20,000 |
| **Current liability:** trade payables | 5,000 |
| **Total capital and liabilities** | **76,500** |

## Why this matters

- It shows the **dual aspect concept** in action: every transaction has two sides.
- It explains why a **trial balance** should balance.
- It shows that **profit increases capital** and **drawings reduce it**.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A sale is **not** "all profit". When goods costing NLe 6,000 are sold for NLe 9,000, stock falls by 6,000, cash rises by 9,000, and only the **difference of 3,000** is profit that increases capital.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Net assets**: total assets minus total liabilities, which equals capital.
:::

## Key points

- Every transaction has **two effects** and the equation **always balances**.
- Effects: **both sides up**, **both sides down**, or a **swap within one side**.
- **Income/profit** increases capital; **expenses, losses and drawings** reduce it.
- A table of transactions ends with a final **statement of financial position**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect a list of transactions with the instruction to **show the effect of each on the accounting equation** in a table, or to **prepare a statement of financial position** after them. Total each column at the end and check that assets equal capital plus liabilities.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is the effect of buying stock on credit?
2. What is the effect of paying a creditor by cheque?
3. Goods costing NLe 4,000 are sold for NLe 5,500 cash. By how much does capital change?
4. What is the effect of the owner taking NLe 1,000 cash for private use?
5. Which transaction changes neither total assets nor total liabilities?

**Answers:**

1. Assets (stock) increase and liabilities (trade payables) increase.
2. Assets (bank) decrease and liabilities (trade payables) decrease.
3. Capital increases by $5\,500 - 4\,000 = 1\,500$: NLe 1,500.
4. Assets (cash) decrease and capital decreases (drawings).
5. Exchanging one asset for another, such as buying furniture for cash.
