In 1820, Oersted noticed a compass needle twitch when he switched on a current in a nearby wire: electricity produces magnetism. That discovery led directly to electromagnets, electric bells, loudspeakers and motors.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the magnetic fields of a straight wire and a solenoid
- use the right-hand grip rule to find field directions
- describe an electromagnet and the factors affecting its strength
- explain the electric bell and the relay

## The field round a straight wire

A current in a straight wire produces a magnetic field of **concentric circles** centred on the wire. The field is strongest close to the wire and weakens with distance.

![Left: around a straight wire carrying current into the page the field lines are concentric circles running clockwise. Right: a current-carrying solenoid produces a field like that of a bar magnet](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/wire-and-solenoid-fields.svg)

*Figure 1. Magnetic fields of a straight wire and a solenoid. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
**Right-hand grip rule (straight wire):** grip the wire with the right hand with the **thumb pointing in the direction of the current**; the **curled fingers** show the direction of the magnetic field lines.
:::

On diagrams, current **into** the page is shown as ⊗ (the tail of an arrow), and current **out of** the page as ⊙ (the point of an arrow). Current into the page gives a **clockwise** field.

## The field of a solenoid

:::callout{type=definition}
A **solenoid** is a long coil of wire.
:::

When current flows, its field is like that of a **bar magnet**: almost uniform inside, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other.

**Finding the poles:**

- **Right-hand grip rule (solenoid):** grip the coil so the **fingers curl in the direction of the current**; the **thumb points to the north pole**.
- Or look at an end: if the current flows **anticlockwise**, that end is a **N** pole; if **clockwise**, it is a **S** pole.

The field strength increases with:

1. **more turns** per unit length
2. **larger current**
3. a **soft-iron core** inside the coil

## Electromagnets

:::callout{type=definition}
An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire wound on a **soft-iron core**. It is a magnet only while current flows.
:::

Soft iron is used because it is **easily magnetised** and **loses its magnetism at once** when the current is switched off (steel would stay magnetised).

**Uses:** lifting scrap iron, electric bells, relays, circuit breakers, loudspeakers, telephone earpieces, magnetic door locks, separating iron from other waste.

## The electric bell

1. Pressing the bell push completes the circuit; current flows through the **electromagnet**.
2. The electromagnet attracts the soft-iron **armature**; the **hammer** strikes the **gong**.
3. As the armature moves, it pulls away from the **contact screw**, **breaking the circuit**.
4. The electromagnet loses its magnetism; the **spring** pulls the armature back, remaking the contact.
5. The cycle repeats rapidly, so the bell rings continuously while the push is pressed.

## The relay

:::callout{type=definition}
A **relay** is a switch operated by an electromagnet.
:::

A **small current** in the coil circuit attracts an armature that closes (or opens) contacts in a **second circuit**, which may carry a **large current** or high voltage. Relays let a small, safe circuit control a large one — for example the starter motor of a car, or a water pump controlled by a float switch.

:::callout{type=local-example}
In the electric bell at a school gate, and in the circuit breakers of a house distribution board, an electromagnet is at work. A circuit breaker's electromagnet pulls open a switch when the current becomes dangerously large.
:::

## Key points

- A current in a straight wire produces circular field lines; right-hand grip rule gives the direction.
- A solenoid's field is like a bar magnet's; increase it with more turns, more current and a soft-iron core.
- Electromagnets use soft iron because it magnetises and demagnetises easily.
- The electric bell makes and breaks its own circuit; a relay lets a small current switch a large one.

## Quick check

1. What shape are the field lines round a long straight current-carrying wire?
2. Give two ways of increasing the strength of an electromagnet.
3. Why is the core of an electromagnet made of soft iron rather than steel?

**Answers:** 1. Concentric circles centred on the wire. 2. For example: increase the current; increase the number of turns (or use a soft-iron core). 3. Soft iron loses its magnetism immediately when the current stops; steel would stay magnetised.
