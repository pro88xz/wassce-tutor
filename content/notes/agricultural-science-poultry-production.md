Poultry farming is the fastest way to turn feed into animal protein and income. A broiler chick weighing about 40 g on day one can reach 2 kg or more in six weeks; a good laying hen gives around 250–300 eggs a year. Around Freetown, Bo, Kenema and Makeni, commercial farms now supply many of the eggs and much of the chicken eaten in the towns, while almost every village household keeps a few local chickens. This lesson covers poultry breeds and systems, housing, brooding, feeding, health, and the management of broilers and layers, with the calculations farmers use to judge performance.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name types and breeds of poultry and distinguish broilers from layers
- describe extensive, semi-intensive and intensive (deep litter and battery cage) systems
- describe brooding of day-old chicks
- describe the feeding, health care and routine management of broilers and layers
- calculate feed conversion ratio and laying percentage
- state the problems of poultry production

## Types of poultry

- **Chickens**: **broilers** (meat birds that grow very fast), **layers** (bred to lay many eggs), **local (village) chickens** (hardy, good mothers, few eggs), and dual-purpose breeds.
- **Ducks**, **turkeys** and **guinea fowl** are also kept.

| | Broilers | Layers |
|---|---|---|
| purpose | meat | eggs |
| growth | very fast; market at **6–8 weeks** (about 2–2.5 kg) | slower; start laying at **18–20 weeks** |
| feeds | starter, then finisher | chick mash, grower mash, **layers' mash** (high calcium) |
| housing | deep litter | deep litter or battery cages |
| lifespan on farm | about 2 months | about 18 months of laying, then sold as "spent" hens |

## Systems of management

| System | Features | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **extensive (free range)** | birds roam and find their own food | cheap; many losses to disease, hawks, theft; few eggs |
| **semi-intensive** | a house at night and a fenced run by day | moderate cost and control |
| **intensive: deep litter** | birds kept in a house on a deep layer of litter | common for broilers and layers; litter absorbs moisture and can become good manure |
| **intensive: battery cages** | layers kept in wire cages in rows | clean eggs, easy records, many birds in little space; high cost; welfare concerns |

## Housing

![A deep litter house in cross-section: overhanging roof, low walls with wire mesh above for ventilation, deep dry litter on a concrete floor, feeders, drinkers, a perch and nest boxes](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/poultry-house.svg)

- **Site**: well drained, away from other poultry houses, with road access and water.
- **Orientation**: long axis **east–west**, so the sun does not shine directly in on the birds.
- **Ventilation**: low walls with **wire mesh** above on the long sides.
- **Roof**: with a **wide overhang** against sun and driving rain.
- **Floor**: concrete, covered with **5–10 cm of litter** (wood shavings, rice husks), kept **dry**.
- **Space**: avoid overcrowding (roughly 8–10 broilers or 4–5 layers per m² on deep litter).
- **Equipment**: feeders, drinkers, nest boxes (about 1 per 4–5 layers), perches, a **footbath** at the door.

## Brooding day-old chicks

**Brooding** is the care of chicks in the first weeks, when they cannot keep themselves warm.

1. Clean and **disinfect** the house; spread fresh litter; set up a **brooder** (heat from bulbs, charcoal pots or gas) with a **brooder guard** (a ring that keeps chicks near the heat).
2. **Temperature**: about **32–35 °C** in the first week, reduced by about 3 °C each week until about 21 °C.
3. **Read the chicks**: huddled under the heat = too cold; spread to the edges and panting = too hot; evenly spread and active = right.
4. Give clean water (with glucose or vitamins on arrival) and **chick starter** feed on trays or papers at first.
5. Light most of the day in the first days so chicks find food and water.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Letting the litter get wet.** Wet, caked litter around drinkers releases **ammonia** and breeds the parasites of **coccidiosis**, which causes bloody diarrhoea and deaths. Fix leaking drinkers, turn the litter, and replace wet patches at once.
:::

## Feeding

- **Broilers**: **broiler starter** (high protein, about 22–23%) for the first 3–4 weeks, then **broiler finisher** to market.
- **Layers**: **chick mash** (to about 8 weeks), **grower mash** (to about 18 weeks), then **layers' mash** with extra **calcium** for strong shells; give oyster shell if shells are thin.
- Clean, cool **water at all times**.
- Use feeders that reduce **wastage**; do not fill to the brim.

## Health and routine management

- **Vaccination**: Newcastle disease, Gumboro, fowl pox and others on schedule (see the animal health lesson).
- **Biosecurity**: footbath, limit visitors, **all-in all-out** batches, clean and disinfect between batches.
- **Coccidiosis** prevention: dry litter, coccidiostats.
- **Debeaking** (beak trimming) of layers, to reduce feather pecking and cannibalism.
- **Lighting**: layers need about **16 hours of light** a day to keep laying.
- **Culling** of sick, weak or non-laying birds.
- **Egg collection** at least **three times a day**; store eggs in a cool place, **blunt end up**.
- **Records**: number of birds, deaths, feed used, eggs collected, weights, vaccinations, sales.

## Measuring performance

:::callout{type=definition}
$$\text{feed conversion ratio (FCR)} = \frac{\text{feed eaten (kg)}}{\text{weight gained (kg)}}$$

A **lower** FCR is better: less feed per kg of meat.

$$\text{laying percentage (hen-day)} = \frac{\text{eggs collected}}{\text{number of hens} \times \text{days}} \times 100$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**(a) A batch of broilers ate 4000 kg of feed and gained 2200 kg in weight. Find the FCR. (b) 500 layers produced 2800 eggs in a week. Find the laying percentage.**

**(a)** $\text{FCR} = \dfrac{4000}{2200} \approx 1.8$

**(b)** $\dfrac{2800}{500 \times 7} \times 100 = \dfrac{2800}{3500} \times 100 = 80\%$
:::

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Eggs for the city.** Layer farms near Freetown supply eggs sold in crates by traders and in shops. The farmers who make money are those who keep **mortality low** (vaccination, biosecurity), keep the **laying percentage high** (good layers' mash, 16 hours of light, enough water) and **control feed waste**. Because feed is the biggest cost, a few percent saved on feed makes a big difference to the profit.
:::

## Problems of poultry production

High and rising **cost of feed**; disease outbreaks (Newcastle, Gumboro, coccidiosis); unreliable supply of good **day-old chicks**; competition from **imported frozen chicken**; unreliable electricity for brooding and cold storage; lack of credit and veterinary services.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Give three differences between broilers and layers."}
Any three: broilers are for meat, layers for eggs; broilers grow very fast and are sold at 6–8 weeks, layers start laying at 18–20 weeks and are kept for over a year; broilers get starter and finisher feed, layers chick, grower and layers' mash; layers may be kept in battery cages.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. How does a farmer know if brooding temperature is too low?"}
The chicks **huddle together** under the heat source and chirp loudly.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Why do layers need extra calcium?"}
To form strong **eggshells**, which are made of calcium carbonate.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Broilers ate 900 kg of feed and gained 500 kg. Find the FCR."}
$900 \div 500 = 1.8$.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. 200 hens laid 1260 eggs in 7 days. Find the laying percentage."}
$\dfrac{1260}{200 \times 7} \times 100 = \dfrac{1260}{1400} \times 100 = 90\%$.
:::

## Key points

- **Broilers** for meat (6–8 weeks); **layers** for eggs (from 18–20 weeks); local chickens are hardy.
- Systems: **extensive, semi-intensive, deep litter, battery cage**.
- House: **east–west**, wire-mesh sides, overhanging roof, dry litter, footbath.
- Brooding: **32–35 °C** in week 1, down about 3 °C a week; watch chick behaviour.
- Feeds: starter, finisher; chick, grower, **layers' mash**; water always.
- Health: **vaccination, biosecurity, dry litter, debeaking, culling, records**.
- **FCR** = feed ÷ gain; **laying %** = eggs ÷ (hens × days) × 100.
