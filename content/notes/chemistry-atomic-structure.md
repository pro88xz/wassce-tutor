# Atomic structure: subatomic particles, isotopes and electron arrangement

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to describe the structure of an atom; define atomic number and mass number; calculate numbers of subatomic particles; explain isotopes; and write simple electron arrangements.

## The atom
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical identity of that element. It has a tiny, dense nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. Negatively charged electrons occupy regions around the nucleus called shells or energy levels. Most of an atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus, while most of its volume is the space occupied by the electron cloud.

A proton has relative charge +1 and relative mass approximately 1. A neutron has relative charge 0 and relative mass approximately 1. An electron has relative charge −1 and a much smaller relative mass, about 1/1840 of a proton. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

## Atomic number and mass number
The atomic number, Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus. It identifies the element. The mass number, A, is the total number of protons and neutrons. Therefore:

Number of protons = Z

Number of electrons in a neutral atom = Z

Number of neutrons = A − Z

For example, an atom written as ²³₁₁Na has 11 protons, 11 electrons if neutral, and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons. If an atom forms an ion, the proton number does not change; only the number of electrons changes. A sodium ion, Na⁺, has lost one electron and therefore has 10 electrons.

## Isotopes
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. For example, carbon-12 and carbon-14 both contain six protons, but they contain six and eight neutrons respectively. Isotopes generally have similar chemical properties because they have the same electron arrangement, although their masses and nuclear stability may differ.

The relative atomic mass shown on a periodic table is often a weighted mean of the masses of naturally occurring isotopes, taking their abundances into account. It is not necessarily a whole number.

## Electron arrangement
Electrons occupy energy levels. For the first 20 elements, the simplified shell model commonly used at this level fills the first shell with up to 2 electrons, the second with up to 8 and the third with up to 8 before the fourth begins. Examples include oxygen, 2,6; sodium, 2,8,1; and calcium, 2,8,8,2. The outer-shell electrons are called valence electrons and strongly influence bonding and chemical behaviour.

Atoms may gain, lose or share electrons to achieve more stable arrangements. This links atomic structure to the formation of ions and covalent bonds.

## WASSCE examination focus
Practise reading nuclide notation and calculating protons, neutrons and electrons. State whether a species is neutral or an ion before calculating its electrons. When comparing isotopes, emphasise equal proton number and different neutron number—not different elements.

## Check your understanding
1. State the relative charges of a proton, neutron and electron.
2. How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in neutral ³⁵₁₇Cl?
3. Define isotopes.
4. Give the electron arrangement of magnesium, atomic number 12.
5. How many electrons are in Al³⁺ if aluminium has atomic number 13?

## Answers for self-assessment
1. +1, 0 and −1 respectively.
2. 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 17 electrons.
3. Atoms of the same element with equal proton numbers but different neutron numbers.
4. 2,8,2.
5. 10 electrons.
