## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- list the factors that determine supply
- distinguish a change in quantity supplied from a change in supply
- describe the types of supply and exceptional supply curves

## Determinants of supply

| Factor | How it affects supply | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost of production** | Higher costs reduce supply; lower costs increase it | Dearer fertiliser means farmers grow less rice |
| **Technology** | Better methods lower costs and raise supply | A power tiller lets a farmer plough more land |
| **Prices of other goods** | If another good becomes more profitable, producers switch to it | Cocoa prices rise, so some farmers grow less coffee |
| **Weather and natural conditions** | Good rains raise farm output; floods and drought cut it | A late rainy season reduces the groundnut harvest |
| **Taxes and subsidies** | Taxes raise costs and reduce supply; subsidies do the opposite | A subsidy on seed rice increases rice supply |
| **Number of producers** | More firms means more supply | New bakeries open in a town |
| **Expectations** | If prices are expected to rise, producers may hold back supply now | Traders keep rice in store before an expected price rise |
| **Government policy** | Quotas and bans limit supply | A ban on exporting a crop raises its supply at home |

## Change in quantity supplied versus change in supply

:::callout{type=definition}
A **change in quantity supplied** is caused **only by a change in the good's own price**. It is a **movement along the same supply curve**: an *extension* when price rises, a *contraction* when price falls.

A **change in supply** is caused by a change in **any other factor**. It is a **shift of the whole supply curve**: to the **right** for an increase, to the **left** for a decrease.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Which is it?**

- The price of plantain rises from NLe 15 to NLe 20, so farmers bring more to market. → **Extension** along the curve.
- Good rains give a bumper harvest, so more plantain is offered at every price. → **Increase in supply**: the curve **shifts right**.
- A rise in transport fares raises traders' costs, so less is offered at every price. → **Decrease in supply**: the curve **shifts left**.
:::

## Types of supply

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint supply** | Producing one good automatically produces another | Beef and hides from cattle; palm oil and palm kernels |
| **Competitive supply** | Resources used for one good cannot be used for another | Land used for rice cannot also grow cassava at the same time |
| **Composite supply** | A want can be met from several sources | Energy from electricity, kerosene, charcoal or solar power |

:::callout{type=local-example}
When more cattle are slaughtered to meet the demand for beef, more hides become available too: hides are in **joint supply** with beef. Their price may fall even though nobody asked for more hides.
:::

## Exceptional supply curves

In some cases the supply curve does not slope upwards:

1. **The supply of labour (backward-bending curve).** As wage rates rise, people at first work more hours. But beyond a certain wage, some prefer more leisure, so they work **fewer** hours at a higher wage.
2. **Perishable goods.** Fresh fish or ripe tomatoes must be sold quickly. If the price falls late in the day, traders may sell **more**, not less, rather than let the goods spoil.
3. **Fixed supply.** Goods such as land, or antiques, cannot be increased however high the price goes: the supply curve is **vertical**.
4. **Expectations of falling prices.** Producers who expect prices to fall further may sell more now, even at a lower price.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Joint supply" and "joint demand" are different. **Joint supply**: one production process gives two goods (beef and hides). **Joint demand**: two goods are wanted together (cars and fuel).
:::

## Key points

- Supply depends on **costs**, **technology**, **prices of other goods**, **weather**, **taxes and subsidies**, **the number of producers** and **expectations**.
- Own-price changes: **movement along** the curve. Other factors: **shift** of the curve.
- Types: **joint**, **competitive**, **composite** supply.
- Exceptions: the **backward-bending** supply of labour, perishables, goods in **fixed supply**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
As with demand, you will be asked to **distinguish a change in supply from a change in quantity supplied, with diagrams**, and to **explain the factors affecting supply**. Give each factor a short heading, explain the direction of its effect, and add an example. Use two separate, fully labelled diagrams for movement and shift.
:::

## Quick check

1. A subsidy is paid to rice farmers. What happens to the supply curve of rice?
2. Are beef and hides in joint, competitive or composite supply?
3. Why might the supply curve of labour bend backwards?

**Answers:** 1. It shifts to the right (supply increases), because the subsidy lowers farmers' costs. 2. Joint supply. 3. At very high wage rates some workers choose more leisure, so they offer fewer hours even though the wage is higher.
