Should decisions about a clinic in Pujehun be made in Pujehun or in Freetown? Governments must decide how much power to keep at the centre and how much to hand down to local bodies. This lesson compares **centralisation** and **decentralisation**, explains the forms decentralisation can take, and looks at how local councils are financed and the problems they face.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define centralisation and decentralisation
- distinguish deconcentration, delegation and devolution
- state the merits and demerits of centralisation and decentralisation
- explain the sources of local government revenue
- explain the problems of local government and how to solve them

## Centralisation and decentralisation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Centralisation** is the concentration of governmental power and decision-making in the **central government**. **Decentralisation** is the **transfer of power, functions and resources** from the central government to lower levels, such as regions, districts or local councils.
:::

![A scale from centralisation (all decisions in the capital) through deconcentration (central officials posted to districts) and delegation (tasks handed to agencies) to devolution (legal powers and money to elected councils)](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/decentralisation-forms.svg)

*Figure 1. How far is power moved from the centre? WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

### Forms of decentralisation

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Deconcentration** | Central ministries post their own officials to the regions and districts; decisions still come from the centre | District education and health offices of central ministries |
| **Delegation** | The centre hands specific tasks to semi-independent bodies, which act on its behalf | Public corporations and agencies |
| **Devolution** | The centre transfers **legal powers, functions and revenue** to elected local councils, which decide for themselves | Sierra Leone's local councils under the Local Government Act |

Devolution is the **strongest** form, because the local body gains real decision-making power.

## Merits and demerits

| | Centralisation | Decentralisation |
|---|---|---|
| **Merits** | Uniform policies across the country; national unity; economies of scale; easier coordination and control; useful in emergencies | Brings government closer to people; local participation; local knowledge; faster decisions; reduces central workload; trains local leaders |
| **Demerits** | Remote from local needs; slow decisions; overloads the centre; neglect of rural areas; risk of dictatorship | Duplication and waste; unequal development between areas; weak local capacity; risk of local corruption; can encourage separatism |

## Financing local government

**Internal (own) sources:**

- **Local tax** (a flat tax on adults)
- **Property rates** on buildings
- **Market dues** and fees for lorry parks
- **Licences**: trading, business, vehicle and entertainment licences
- **Fees and charges** for services
- **Fines** for breaking bye-laws
- **Income from investments** and council property

**External sources:**

- **Grants from central government**, including grants for devolved functions
- **Loans** (with central approval)
- **Donor and development partner support**

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Freetown City Council** has worked to raise more of its own revenue by updating its **property rates**: mapping and valuing buildings across the city so that rates reflect their real value. Better own-source revenue gives a council more freedom to plan and less dependence on central grants, which are often delayed.
:::

## Problems of local government

- **Inadequate funds** and a narrow revenue base
- **Delays in central government grants**
- **Interference** by the central government and politicians
- **Shortage of qualified staff**
- **Corruption and mismanagement** of funds
- **Conflict with traditional authorities** over roles and revenue
- **Low public participation** and poor understanding of councils' role
- **Unwillingness to pay** local taxes and rates

**Possible solutions:** widen the revenue base and improve collection; release grants on time; train council staff; strengthen audits and accountability; clarify the roles of councils and chiefdoms; educate the public about councils and the services they provide.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Classify the decentralisation.** For each, name the form:

- The Ministry of Health posts a District Medical Officer who reports to Freetown: **deconcentration**.
- Parliament creates a water company to supply a city on the government's behalf: **delegation**.
- A district council receives legal powers and funds to run primary health clinics and decides how to spend them: **devolution**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Decentralisation in a **unitary** state is not the same as **federalism**. In a unitary state such as Sierra Leone, powers given to councils come from an **Act of Parliament** and can be changed by Parliament. In a federation, the **constitution** divides power and the centre cannot take it back alone.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Deconcentration**: a form of decentralisation in which central ministries post their own officials to the regions and districts, while decisions still come from the centre.
- **Delegation**: a form of decentralisation in which the centre hands specific tasks to semi-independent bodies that act on its behalf.
- **Devolution**: a form of decentralisation in which the centre transfers legal powers, functions and revenue to elected local councils, which decide for themselves.
- **Property rates**: a local tax on buildings, collected by councils.
:::

## Key points

- **Centralisation** keeps power at the centre; **decentralisation** transfers power, functions and resources downward.
- Forms: **deconcentration** (central officials in districts), **delegation** (tasks to agencies), **devolution** (legal powers and revenue to elected councils).
- Local revenue: **local tax, property rates, market dues, licences, fees, fines, grants**.
- Problems: **inadequate funds, delayed grants, interference, poor staffing, corruption, conflict with chiefs**.

## Quick check

1. What is devolution?
2. Distinguish deconcentration from devolution.
3. Name three internal sources of revenue for local councils.
4. State two problems facing local government in Sierra Leone.

**Answers:**

1. The transfer of legal powers, functions and revenue from central government to elected local councils, which make their own decisions.
2. In deconcentration, central officials are posted to the districts but decisions remain with the centre; in devolution, elected local bodies receive real powers and resources to decide for themselves.
3. Any three: local tax; property rates; market dues; licences; fees and charges; fines; income from investments.
4. Any two: inadequate funds; delayed grants; central interference; shortage of qualified staff; corruption; conflict with traditional authorities; low participation; unwillingness to pay taxes.
