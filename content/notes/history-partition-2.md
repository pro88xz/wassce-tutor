:::callout{type=definition}
The **Berlin Conference of 1884–85** was a meeting of European powers and the United States, called by the German chancellor **Otto von Bismarck**, to agree rules for claiming African territory.
:::

It met from **15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885**, **no African was present**, and its **General Act** set the principle of **effective occupation**, opened the **Congo and Niger rivers** to free navigation, and recognised **King Leopold II's Congo Free State**. Africa was then partitioned through **treaties, conquest, boundary agreements and chartered companies**. This lesson explains the Berlin Conference, the **methods of partition** and the **effects of the partition of Africa**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why the Berlin Conference was called
- state the main decisions of the Berlin Conference
- describe the methods Europeans used to partition Africa
- describe how West Africa was divided among European powers
- explain the effects of the partition on Africa

## The Berlin Conference (1884–1885)

**Why it was called:**

1. Rival claims in the **Congo basin** (Leopold II, France and Portugal) threatened war.
2. Britain and France were competing on the **Niger**.
3. Germany had just claimed colonies and wanted a say.
4. The powers wanted **rules** so they could divide Africa **without fighting each other**.

**Who attended:** **fourteen** states: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, Spain, France, Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire and the **United States**. **No African ruler or state** was invited.

**Decisions of the General Act:**

| Decision | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Effective occupation** | a power claiming a **coastal** territory had to **occupy it effectively**, with an administration able to keep order and protect trade |
| **Notification** | a power taking new land on the coast had to **inform the other powers** |
| **Free navigation** | the **Congo** and **Niger** rivers were open to ships of all nations |
| **Free trade** | free trade in the **Congo basin** |
| **Congo Free State** | Leopold II's claim to the Congo was recognised (it became his personal possession) |
| **Slave trade** | the powers promised to help suppress the slave trade |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why effective occupation sped up the Scramble**

Before Berlin, a few forts or a flag on the coast could support a claim. After 1885, a power had to show **real control**. So each power hurried to **sign treaties, send officials and soldiers inland, and build posts** before a rival did. The rule meant to slow the Scramble actually **sped it up**.
:::

## Methods of partition

1. **Treaties with African rulers:** Europeans persuaded or tricked rulers into signing "treaties of protection" or friendship, often not understood in the same way by both sides.
2. **Military conquest:** where rulers resisted, Europeans used force, as against **Samori Toure**, **Asante**, **Dahomey**, **Benin** and the **Sokoto Caliphate**.
3. **Boundary agreements between European powers:** colonial borders were drawn in Europe on maps, for example the **Anglo-French agreements of 1889, 1895 and 1898** and the **Anglo-German agreement of 1890**.
4. **Chartered companies:** private companies given the right to govern, such as the **Royal Niger Company** (1886) on the Niger and the **British South Africa Company** in Central Africa.
5. **Missionaries, traders and explorers** prepared the way by making contacts and asking for protection.

## The partition of West Africa

![Map of Africa about 1914 coloured by colonial power: Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy and Spain; only Ethiopia and Liberia independent](https://wasscetutor.com/img/history/partition-1914.svg)

*Figure 1. Africa partitioned among European powers, about 1914 (modern borders shown). WASSCE Tutor map.*

| Power | West African colonies |
|---|---|
| **Britain** | **Nigeria**, **Gold Coast** (Ghana), **Sierra Leone**, **The Gambia** |
| **France** | Senegal, French Sudan (Mali), Upper Volta (Burkina Faso), Niger, Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire, Dahomey (Benin), Mauritania; grouped from **1895** as **French West Africa (AOF)** |
| **Germany** | **Togoland** and **Kamerun** (taken by Britain and France in the First World War and later divided between them) |
| **Portugal** | **Portuguese Guinea** (Guinea-Bissau) and **Cabo Verde** |
| **Independent** | **Liberia** |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's borders were drawn by agreements with **France** (for the border with French Guinea, settled in the **1880s and 1895**) and with **Liberia**. These lines cut across peoples: **Kissi** and **Kono** relatives live in both Sierra Leone and Guinea, and **Vai**, **Mende** and **Gola** communities live on both sides of the Mano River border with Liberia.
:::

## Effects of the partition of Africa

| Area | Effects |
|---|---|
| **Political** | loss of **independence** and sovereignty; colonial rule; African rulers lost power or became agents of colonial governments |
| **Artificial boundaries** | borders drawn without regard to peoples **split ethnic groups**, such as the **Ewe** (Ghana and Togo), **Yoruba** (Nigeria and Benin) and **Hausa** (Nigeria and Niger), and grouped rival peoples together; they caused later **border disputes** |
| **Economic** | Africa became a supplier of **raw materials** and a market for European goods; **cash crops**, mines, roads and railways were developed for export |
| **Social** | spread of **Christianity, Western education** and European languages; new towns grew |
| **Modern states** | most present-day African countries have the **boundaries of the partition**; the OAU in 1964 agreed to **accept colonial borders** to avoid war |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The Berlin Conference did **not** divide the whole of Africa at one meeting. It set the **rules**; the actual division happened over the next thirty years through **treaties, conquest and agreements** between the powers.
:::

## Key points

- **Berlin Conference**, called by **Bismarck**, **15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885**, 14 states, **no Africans**.
- Decisions: **effective occupation**, **notification**, free navigation of the **Congo and Niger**, free trade in the Congo basin, recognition of **Leopold's Congo**.
- Methods of partition: **treaties, conquest, boundary agreements, chartered companies**, missionaries and traders.
- Effects: **loss of independence, artificial boundaries, export economies**, spread of Western education and religion, today's **national borders**.
