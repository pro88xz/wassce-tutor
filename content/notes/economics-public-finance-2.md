A tax on fuel, a tax on salaries and a tax on imported goods all raise money for the government, but they fall on different people and change behaviour in different ways. This lesson covers the principles of a good tax system, direct and indirect taxes, and how taxes can be progressive, proportional or regressive.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a tax and state the canons (principles) of a good tax system
- distinguish direct from indirect taxes, with merits and demerits of each
- explain progressive, proportional and regressive taxes, with calculations
- explain the incidence of a tax and the effects of taxation

## What is a tax?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **tax** is a **compulsory payment** by individuals and firms to the government, for which no **direct** service is given in return. It is used to pay for public services and to influence the economy.
:::

**Objectives of taxation:** raising revenue; redistributing income; discouraging harmful goods (tobacco, alcohol); protecting home industries (import duties); controlling inflation by reducing spending; encouraging or discouraging certain activities.

## The canons of taxation

Adam Smith set out four principles of a good tax; later economists added more.

| Canon | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Equity** | People should pay according to their **ability to pay** |
| **Certainty** | The amount, time and manner of payment should be clear to the taxpayer |
| **Convenience** | The tax should be collected at a time and in a way convenient to the payer (for example PAYE deducted from salaries) |
| **Economy** | The cost of collecting it should be small compared with the revenue |
| **Productivity** | It should raise a good amount of revenue |
| **Flexibility** | It should be easy to change when the economy changes |
| **Simplicity** | Taxpayers should be able to understand it |
| **Neutrality** | It should not distort economic decisions unnecessarily |

## Direct and indirect taxes

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **direct tax** is levied on the income or wealth of a person or firm, and is **paid directly to the government by the person on whom it is imposed**. The burden cannot easily be shifted. Examples: personal income tax (PAYE), company (corporation) tax, property tax, capital gains tax.
- An **indirect tax** is levied on **goods and services**. It is collected from sellers, who can shift some or all of the burden to buyers in higher prices. Examples: Goods and Services Tax (GST), import duty, excise duty, export duty.
:::

| | Direct taxes | Indirect taxes |
|---|---|---|
| **Merits** | Can be progressive and equitable; certain; revenue rises with incomes | Hard to evade (included in prices); convenient; can discourage harmful goods; wide coverage |
| **Demerits** | Can discourage work and saving; easy to evade where records are poor; costly to assess | Regressive: the poor pay a larger share of income; raise prices and can cause inflation; revenue falls if sales fall |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** is charged at **15%** on most goods and services, with some essentials exempt. It is an indirect tax collected by registered businesses and paid to the NRA. Salaried workers pay **PAYE** income tax, a direct tax deducted by employers.
:::

## Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

![Graph of tax as a percentage of income against income: a rising line for a progressive tax, a flat line for a proportional tax and a falling line for a regressive tax](https://wasscetutor.com/img/economics/tax-types.svg)

*Figure 1. How the share of income paid in tax changes as income rises. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Type | The **rate** of tax as income rises | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | **Rises**: higher incomes pay a larger percentage | Income tax with higher rates on higher bands |
| **Proportional** | **Stays the same**: everyone pays the same percentage | A flat 10% tax on all incomes |
| **Regressive** | **Falls**: lower incomes pay a larger percentage of income | A fixed poll tax; indirect taxes on essentials |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Three people pay tax on their monthly incomes.**

| Person | Income (NLe) | Tax A (NLe) | Tax B (NLe) | Tax C (NLe) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kadi | 2,000 | 100 (5%) | 200 (10%) | 100 (5%) |
| Sorie | 5,000 | 500 (10%) | 500 (10%) | 150 (3%) |
| Mariama | 10,000 | 2,000 (20%) | 1,000 (10%) | 200 (2%) |

- **Tax A is progressive**: the rate rises from 5% to 20%.
- **Tax B is proportional**: the rate is 10% for everyone.
- **Tax C is regressive**: the rate falls from 5% to 2%, although richer people pay more money in total.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A tax is regressive because the **percentage** of income paid falls as income rises, not because the poor pay more money. In Tax C, Mariama pays more leones than Kadi, but a smaller share of her income.
:::

## Incidence of taxation

The **impact** of a tax is on the person who pays it to the government; the **incidence** is on the person who finally **bears** the burden. For an indirect tax, the seller pays (impact), but much of the burden may be passed to buyers (incidence) in a higher price. Buyers bear more when **demand is inelastic** (see *Price Determination and Market Equilibrium*).

## Effects of taxation

1. **On work and saving**: high income taxes may discourage extra work, enterprise and saving.
2. **On prices**: indirect taxes raise prices and may add to inflation.
3. **On production**: taxes can steer resources away from taxed goods; tax holidays attract investment.
4. **On income distribution**: progressive taxes reduce inequality; regressive taxes increase it.
5. **On consumption**: taxes on tobacco and alcohol discourage their use.
6. **On the balance of payments**: import duties reduce imports.

## Key points

- A **tax** is a compulsory payment to the government with no direct service in return.
- Good taxes are **equitable, certain, convenient and economical** (Adam Smith's canons), and productive and flexible.
- **Direct taxes** fall on income and wealth; **indirect taxes** on goods and services and can be shifted.
- **Progressive** taxes take a rising share of income, **proportional** the same share, **regressive** a falling share.
- **Incidence** is who finally bears a tax.

## Quick check

1. Name Adam Smith's four canons of taxation.
2. Is GST a direct or an indirect tax? Why?
3. Incomes of NLe 1,000 and NLe 4,000 pay taxes of NLe 50 and NLe 400. Is the tax progressive, proportional or regressive?
4. What is the difference between the impact and the incidence of a tax?

**Answers:** 1. Equity, certainty, convenience and economy. 2. Indirect: it is charged on goods and services and the seller can pass it on to buyers. 3. Rates are 5% and 10%, so it is progressive. 4. Impact is on whoever pays the tax to the government; incidence is on whoever finally bears it.
