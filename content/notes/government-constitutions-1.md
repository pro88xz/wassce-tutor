Every club, school prefect body and football association has rules saying who leads it, how leaders are chosen and what members may do. A state needs the same, on a much larger scale. Its **constitution** is the highest law: it creates the organs of government, shares out their powers and protects the rights of citizens. This lesson explains what a constitution is, where its rules come from, the types of constitution and the features of a good one.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a constitution and explain its importance
- explain the sources of a constitution
- distinguish the main types of constitution
- state the features of a good constitution
- describe the main contents of Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution

## What is a constitution?

:::callout{type=definition}
A **constitution** is the body of **fundamental rules, laws and principles** by which a state is governed. It sets out the structure and powers of the organs of government, the relationship between them, and the rights and duties of citizens.
:::

A constitution is often called the **supreme law** (the *grundnorm*) of the land: any other law that conflicts with it is void to the extent of the conflict.

## Importance (functions) of a constitution

1. **Establishes the organs of government** and their powers.
2. **Limits the powers of government**, preventing arbitrary rule.
3. **Protects fundamental human rights.**
4. **Defines the relationship** between the government and the governed.
5. **Provides for the transfer of power**, for example through elections.
6. **Defines citizenship** and national symbols.
7. **Provides stability** and a framework for settling disputes.
8. **Sets out how it can be amended.**

## Sources of a constitution

![Left: seven sources of a constitution. Right: five pairs of types: written or unwritten, rigid or flexible, unitary or federal, presidential or parliamentary, republican or monarchical](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/constitution-sources-types.svg)

*Figure 1. Where constitutions come from, and their types. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Source | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Constitutional conferences and constituent assemblies** | Representatives meet to draft or agree a constitution, for example the Lancaster House conference of 1960 before Sierra Leone's independence |
| **Statutes (Acts of Parliament)** | Laws that deal with constitutional matters, such as electoral laws |
| **Judicial precedents** | Court decisions interpreting the constitution |
| **Conventions** | Unwritten customs and practices that are followed as if binding, especially in Britain |
| **Historical documents** | Such as Magna Carta (1215) and the Bill of Rights (1689) in Britain |
| **Customs and traditions** | Long-standing practices of the people |
| **Works of constitutional experts** | Writings of scholars such as A. V. Dicey |
| **Referendums and decrees** | Popular votes, or decrees issued by military governments |

## Types of constitution

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Written** | Contained mainly in one document | Sierra Leone, USA, Nigeria |
| **Unwritten** | Not contained in one document; found in statutes, conventions and judicial decisions | Britain |
| **Rigid** | Requires a special, difficult procedure to amend | Sierra Leone, USA |
| **Flexible** | Can be amended by the ordinary law-making process | Britain |
| **Unitary** | Concentrates power in a central government | Sierra Leone, Ghana |
| **Federal** | Divides power between central and state governments | Nigeria, USA |
| **Presidential** | Provides for an executive president | Sierra Leone, USA |
| **Parliamentary** | Provides for a cabinet responsible to parliament | Britain, India |
| **Republican** | Head of state is elected | Sierra Leone since 1971 |
| **Monarchical** | Head of state is a hereditary monarch | Britain, Saudi Arabia |

### Written and unwritten compared

| Written | Unwritten |
|---|---|
| Found in one document | Found in many sources |
| Usually rigid | Usually flexible |
| Clear and certain; easy to consult | Can adapt easily to change |
| May be too rigid to meet new needs | Can be changed easily by the ruling party |
| Courts can check unconstitutional laws | Parliament is supreme |

## Features of a good constitution

- **Clear and precise** language, so its meaning is not easily disputed
- **Comprehensive**, covering all essential matters
- **Protects fundamental human rights**
- Provides for **separation of powers and checks and balances**
- **Adaptable**: neither too rigid nor too flexible
- Has the **support (legitimacy)** of the people
- Provides an **independent judiciary**
- Reflects the **history and needs** of the society

## Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution

The **1991 Constitution** (Act No. 6 of 1991) was approved in a **referendum** in 1991 and came into force on **1 October 1991**, restoring multi-party democracy after the one-party Constitution of 1978. Its main parts include:

- **Chapter II:** Fundamental Principles of State Policy
- **Chapter III:** Fundamental Human Rights and Freedoms
- **Chapter V:** The Executive
- **Chapter VI:** The Legislature
- **Chapter VII:** The Judiciary

It also provides for the Ombudsman, finance, the public service, the police and armed forces, chieftaincy, and the procedure for amendment.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone has had several constitutions: the **Independence Constitution of 1961**, the **Republican Constitution of 1971**, the **One-Party Constitution of 1978** and the **1991 Constitution**. A **Constitutional Review Commission** reviewed the 1991 Constitution and reported in **2016**, and debate about reforming it continues. Later lessons in this course examine each of these constitutions in detail.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Unwritten constitution" does **not** mean that nothing is written down. Britain's constitution includes many written statutes and court judgments; it is called unwritten because it is **not collected in a single document**.
:::

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Convention**: an unwritten custom or practice that is followed as if it were binding.
- **Judicial precedent**: a court decision interpreting the constitution, which guides later cases.
- **Written constitution**: a constitution contained mainly in one document.
- **Unwritten constitution**: a constitution not contained in one document but found in statutes, conventions and judicial decisions, as in Britain.
:::

## Key points

- A **constitution** is the body of fundamental rules by which a state is governed; it is the **supreme law**.
- Sources: **conferences, statutes, precedents, conventions, historical documents, customs, experts' works**.
- Types: **written/unwritten, rigid/flexible, unitary/federal, presidential/parliamentary, republican/monarchical**.
- Sierra Leone's **1991 Constitution** is **written, rigid, unitary, presidential and republican**.
