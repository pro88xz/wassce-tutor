## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define price elasticity of demand and calculate it
- interpret elasticity values: elastic, inelastic, unitary, perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic
- explain the factors that determine price elasticity of demand and the total revenue test

## What elasticity measures

The law of demand tells us that quantity demanded falls when price rises. It does not tell us **by how much**. Elasticity answers that question.

:::callout{type=definition}
**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures the **degree of responsiveness** of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its own price.
:::

$$\text{PED} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

An equivalent form, using the original price $P$ and original quantity $Q$:

$$\text{PED} = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \dfrac{P}{Q}$$

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, the answer is negative. In practice we **ignore the minus sign** and look at the size of the number.

## Two worked examples

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Sugar.** The price of a bag of sugar rises from NLe 20 to NLe 25. The quantity demanded falls from 100 bags to 90 bags a week.

- % change in price $= \dfrac{5}{20} \times 100 = 25\%$
- % change in quantity $= \dfrac{-10}{100} \times 100 = -10\%$
- $\text{PED} = \dfrac{-10}{25} = -0.4$, so the coefficient is **0.4**

Since 0.4 is **less than 1**, demand for sugar is **inelastic**: buyers hardly cut back.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A soft drink.** The price of a bottle falls from NLe 10 to NLe 8. The quantity demanded rises from 50 to 70 bottles a day.

- % change in price $= \dfrac{-2}{10} \times 100 = -20\%$
- % change in quantity $= \dfrac{20}{50} \times 100 = 40\%$
- $\text{PED} = \dfrac{40}{-20} = -2$, so the coefficient is **2**

Since 2 is **greater than 1**, demand is **elastic**: buyers respond strongly.
:::

## Interpreting the number

| Coefficient | Name | Meaning | Shape of demand curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Perfectly inelastic** | Quantity does not change at all when price changes | Vertical |
| Between 0 and 1 | **Inelastic** | Quantity changes by a smaller % than price | Steep |
| Exactly 1 | **Unitary** | Quantity changes by the same % as price | A rectangular hyperbola |
| Greater than 1 | **Elastic** | Quantity changes by a larger % than price | Flat |
| Infinity ($\infty$) | **Perfectly elastic** | Any rise in price makes quantity demanded fall to zero | Horizontal |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not judge elasticity from the **slope** of a curve drawn on a page. A curve can look steep or flat depending on the scale of the axes. Calculate the coefficient from the figures.
:::

## Determinants of price elasticity of demand

1. **Availability of substitutes.** Goods with many close substitutes have **elastic** demand. If one brand of soap becomes dearer, buyers switch to another.
2. **Necessities versus luxuries.** Necessities such as salt, rice and medicine have **inelastic** demand. Luxuries such as jewellery are more elastic.
3. **Proportion of income spent.** Goods that take a tiny part of income (matches, salt) have **inelastic** demand; expensive items (cars, fridges) are more elastic.
4. **Habit and addiction.** Cigarettes and alcohol have **inelastic** demand.
5. **Number of uses.** A good with many uses (palm oil) tends to be more elastic: a lower price brings in new uses.
6. **Time.** Demand becomes **more elastic in the long run** as buyers find substitutes.
7. **Durability.** Purchases of durable goods can be postponed when prices rise, so demand is more elastic.

:::callout{type=local-example}
When fuel prices rise, okada riders and keke drivers must still buy fuel to work, so demand for fuel is inelastic in the short run. Over a longer period some people may switch to buses or walk shorter journeys, so demand becomes a little more elastic.
:::

## The total revenue test

Total revenue (what sellers receive) is price × quantity. Elasticity tells a seller what will happen to revenue when price changes.

| Demand is | Raise the price | Lower the price |
|---|---|---|
| **Inelastic** | Total revenue **rises** | Total revenue **falls** |
| **Elastic** | Total revenue **falls** | Total revenue **rises** |
| **Unitary** | Total revenue **unchanged** | Total revenue **unchanged** |

Check it with the examples:

- Sugar (inelastic): revenue rises from $20 \times 100 = \text{NLe } 2{,}000$ to $25 \times 90 = \text{NLe } 2{,}250$ when the price rises.
- Soft drink (elastic): revenue rises from $10 \times 50 = \text{NLe } 500$ to $8 \times 70 = \text{NLe } 560$ when the price falls.

## Key points

- PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; ignore the minus sign.
- Greater than 1: **elastic**. Less than 1: **inelastic**. Equal to 1: **unitary**.
- Determinants: **substitutes**, **necessity**, **share of income**, **habit**, **uses**, **time**, **durability**.
- If demand is inelastic, a price rise **raises** total revenue; if elastic, it **lowers** it.

## Quick check

1. Price rises by 10% and quantity demanded falls by 30%. What is the PED, and is demand elastic or inelastic?
2. Give two reasons why demand for salt is inelastic.
3. A seller faces inelastic demand. Should she raise or lower her price to increase revenue?

**Answers:** 1. $30 \div 10 = 3$: elastic. 2. It is a necessity, it takes a very small share of income, and it has no close substitute. 3. Raise it: with inelastic demand, a higher price increases total revenue.
