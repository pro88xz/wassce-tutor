Ramps, screw jacks, wheel-and-axle windlasses, gears and hydraulic presses complete the family of machines. Each has a velocity ratio you can work out from its geometry alone.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- find the velocity ratio of an inclined plane, wheel and axle, screw jack and gears
- explain how a hydraulic press multiplies force
- describe an experiment to find how the MA and efficiency of a pulley system vary with load

## The inclined plane

![A load pushed up a ramp of sloping length l to a height h; the velocity ratio is l over h, which equals 1 over sin theta](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/inclined-plane-machine.svg)

*Figure 1. An inclined plane as a machine. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

The effort moves the load a distance $l$ along the slope while raising it a height $h$:

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{l}{h} = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}$$

A **longer, gentler** slope has a larger VR and needs a smaller effort.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Workers push a 900 N drum up a plank 4.5 m long onto a lorry 1.5 m high, using an effort of 400 N. Find the VR, MA and efficiency.**

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{4.5}{1.5} = 3 \qquad \text{MA} = \dfrac{900}{400} = 2.25 \qquad \eta = \dfrac{2.25}{3} \times 100\% = 75\%$$
:::

## The wheel and axle

A large wheel of radius $R$ and an axle of radius $r$ are fixed together. The effort is applied at the rim of the wheel; the load hangs from a rope wound on the axle. In one turn, the effort moves $2\pi R$ and the load $2\pi r$:

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{R}{r}$$

Examples: the windlass over a well, a screwdriver, a door knob, a car steering wheel.

## The screw and the screw jack

The **pitch** $p$ of a screw is the distance between two adjacent threads, which is the distance the screw advances in one complete turn. If the effort is applied at the end of a handle (tommy bar) of length $a$, it moves $2\pi a$ per turn while the load rises $p$:

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{2\pi a}{p}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A car jack has a screw of pitch 0.4 cm and a handle 35 cm long. Its efficiency is 25%. Find the effort needed to raise a load of 8800 N. (Take $\pi = \tfrac{22}{7}$.)**

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{2 \times \tfrac{22}{7} \times 35}{0.4} = \dfrac{220}{0.4} = 550$$

$$\text{MA} = 0.25 \times 550 = 137.5 \qquad E = \dfrac{8800}{137.5} = 64\ \text{N}$$

Screw jacks have very large VRs but low efficiency, because of the large friction in the threads. That friction is useful: it stops the car from sinking when the effort is removed.
:::

## Gears

Two toothed wheels in mesh. If the **driving** gear (where the effort is applied) has $N_1$ teeth and the **driven** gear has $N_2$ teeth:

$$\text{VR} = \dfrac{N_2}{N_1}$$

A small driving gear turning a large driven gear increases the turning force but reduces the speed (low gear on a bicycle for climbing hills).

:::callout{type=local-example}
On a bicycle, a rider climbing a steep road in Freetown chooses a small front chainwheel and a large rear sprocket: the large VR lets the legs push with a manageable force, at the cost of lower speed.
:::

## The hydraulic press

Pressure applied to an enclosed liquid is transmitted **equally** in all directions (**Pascal's principle**). A small force $F_1$ on a small piston of area $A_1$ produces pressure $p = F_1/A_1$; the same pressure on a large piston of area $A_2$ gives a large force:

$$\dfrac{F_1}{A_1} = \dfrac{F_2}{A_2} \Rightarrow F_2 = F_1\dfrac{A_2}{A_1}$$

The velocity ratio of an ideal hydraulic press is $\dfrac{A_2}{A_1}$ (equivalently $\dfrac{D^2}{d^2}$ for pistons of diameters $D$ and $d$).

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A hydraulic press has pistons of area 5 cm² and 250 cm². What load can an effort of 80 N raise (ideal case)?**

$$F_2 = 80 \times \dfrac{250}{5} = 4000\ \text{N}$$

Hydraulic jacks, car brakes and barber's chairs use this principle.
:::

## Experiment: MA and efficiency against load

1. Set up a pulley system (for example VR = 4). Measure the VR by moving the effort a known distance and measuring how far the load rises.
2. Hang a load $L$; add effort masses until the load rises slowly at a steady speed. Record $E$.
3. Repeat for six increasing loads.
4. Calculate MA = $L/E$ and efficiency = MA/VR × 100% for each load.
5. Plot MA (and efficiency) against load.

**Result:** MA and efficiency **increase with load** and level off. With a small load, much of the effort goes into lifting the lower block and overcoming friction; with a large load, these wastes are a smaller fraction of the total.

**Precautions:** use a light, flexible string; oil the pulleys; tap the system gently to reduce sticking; make sure the strands are parallel.

## Key points

- Inclined plane: VR = $l/h = 1/\sin\theta$.
- Wheel and axle: VR = $R/r$. Screw jack: VR = $2\pi a/p$. Gears: VR = $N_{driven}/N_{driver}$.
- Hydraulic press: $F_2 = F_1 A_2/A_1$ (Pascal's principle).
- The MA and efficiency of a pulley system increase with load.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Calculations on the **screw jack**, **inclined plane** and **hydraulic press** appear regularly. A classic theory question asks how **efficiency varies with load** and why. In the practical, tabulate load, effort, MA and efficiency neatly, and draw a smooth curve.
:::

## Quick check

1. A ramp 6 m long raises a load 1.2 m. What is its VR?
2. A windlass has a wheel of radius 40 cm and an axle of radius 8 cm. Find its VR.
3. Why does the efficiency of a pulley system increase as the load increases?

**Answers:** 1. $6 \div 1.2 = 5$. 2. $40 \div 8 = 5$. 3. The work wasted lifting the movable block and overcoming friction becomes a smaller fraction of the total work as the load grows.
