The slope is where equilibrium meets real life: cars parked on hills, loads pushed up ramps, a box resting on a tilted board. This lesson works through inclined planes in full and shows how to check every answer.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- resolve the weight of a body on an inclined plane into components
- solve equilibrium problems on smooth and rough inclined planes
- find the force needed to hold a body when the force is horizontal or at an angle
- describe an experiment to verify the parallelogram (or triangle) law of forces

## Forces on a body on an inclined plane

![A block on a slope at angle theta: weight mg acts straight down and splits into mg sin theta down the slope and mg cos theta into the slope; the normal reaction R acts out of the slope and friction F acts up the slope](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/inclined-plane-forces.svg)

*Figure 1. Resolve along and perpendicular to the slope. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

Always resolve **along the slope** and **perpendicular to the slope**: the normal reaction and friction then each lie along one direction only.

| Force | Component along the slope | Component perpendicular to the slope |
|---|---|---|
| Weight $mg$ | $mg\sin\theta$ (down the slope) | $mg\cos\theta$ (into the slope) |
| Normal reaction $R$ | 0 | $R$ (out of the slope) |
| Friction $F$ | $F$ (opposes the tendency to slide) | 0 |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The angle between the weight and the line **perpendicular** to the slope equals θ, the angle of the slope. So the component **down the slope** is $mg\sin\theta$ (not cos). Check with an extreme case: on a flat floor ($\theta = 0$) nothing pulls the block along, and $\sin 0° = 0$. ✓
:::

## Equilibrium on a rough slope (no applied force)

If a block rests on a rough slope without sliding:

$$F = mg\sin\theta \qquad R = mg\cos\theta$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 5 kg box rests on a plank tilted at 25°. Find the friction force and the normal reaction. ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)**

$mg = 50$ N.

$$F = 50\sin 25° = 50 \times 0.423 = 21.1\ \text{N} \qquad R = 50\cos 25° = 50 \times 0.906 = 45.3\ \text{N}$$

If the plank is tilted further, the friction needed grows until it exceeds the maximum friction available, and the box slides.
:::

## Holding a body with a force at an angle

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 40 N block rests on a smooth plane inclined at 30°. It is held by a horizontal force $P$. Find $P$ and the normal reaction.**

Resolve **along the slope** (up positive): the horizontal force has component $P\cos 30°$ up the slope.

$$P\cos 30° = 40\sin 30° \Rightarrow P = 40\tan 30° = 23.1\ \text{N}$$

Resolve **perpendicular to the slope**: both the weight and $P$ press the block into the slope.

$$R = 40\cos 30° + P\sin 30° = 34.6 + 11.5 = 46.2\ \text{N}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 200 N drum is pulled up a smooth ramp inclined at 20° by a rope parallel to the ramp. Find the tension (a) to hold it at rest, (b) to pull it up at a steady speed.**

(a) $T = 200\sin 20° = 68.4$ N.

(b) Steady speed means **no acceleration**, so the body is still in equilibrium: $T = 68.4$ N again. A larger force is needed only to **start** it moving faster.
:::

## Verifying the parallelogram law of forces (practical)

1. Fix a drawing board vertically. Attach two pulleys near the top corners.
2. Tie three strings to a small ring. Pass two over the pulleys and hang known masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ on them; hang a third mass $m_3$ from the ring.
3. When the ring is at rest, mark the directions of the three strings on paper behind them.
4. Draw $P$ and $Q$ (the weights $m_1g$ and $m_2g$) to a suitable scale along the two upper strings. Complete the parallelogram.
5. Measure the diagonal. It should equal the third weight $m_3g$ and point **vertically upward**, opposite to it.

**Precautions:** the pulleys should be frictionless (tap the board so they settle); the ring must not touch the board; mark string directions with two dots far apart.

## Key points

- On a slope, resolve **along** and **perpendicular** to it: $mg\sin\theta$ down the slope, $mg\cos\theta$ into it.
- A body resting on a rough slope: $F = mg\sin\theta$, $R = mg\cos\theta$.
- A horizontal force $P$ on a smooth slope: $P = mg\tan\theta$.
- Constant velocity is equilibrium: the same balance of forces as at rest.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Inclined-plane problems appear in both objective and theory papers: find the **component of weight along the plane**, the **normal reaction**, or the **force needed to hold** the body. The practical paper may ask you to verify the **parallelogram law** using pulleys and masses. Draw and label the force diagram before any calculation.
:::

## Quick check

1. Write the component of weight down a slope of angle θ.
2. A 100 N body rests on a rough 30° slope. What friction acts on it?
3. Why is the tension the same for holding a load still and pulling it up at constant speed on a smooth slope?

**Answers:** 1. $mg\sin\theta$. 2. $100\sin 30° = 50$ N. 3. In both cases the acceleration is zero, so the resultant force is zero.
