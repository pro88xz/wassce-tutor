:::callout{type=definition}
The **trans-Saharan trade** was the long-distance trade across the **Sahara Desert** between **North Africa** and **West Africa**, carried by **camel caravans** for more than a thousand years.
:::

West Africa sent **gold, kola nuts, ivory, leather and slaves** north; North Africa sent **salt, horses, cloth, copper, books and luxury goods** south. The trade made the **Sudanese empires** rich, built cities such as **Timbuktu**, **Gao** and **Kano**, and carried **Islam** into West Africa. It declined from the sixteenth century as the **Atlantic trade** drew commerce to the coast. This lesson explains the routes, goods, organisation and decline of the trade.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the factors that made the trans-Saharan trade possible
- describe the main routes and the goods traded
- explain how the trade was organised
- explain the importance of the trade to West Africa
- explain the causes of its decline

## Factors that made the trade possible

1. **The camel**, which can travel for days without water and carry heavy loads, came into wide use in the Sahara in the early centuries AD.
2. **Oases and wells**, which provided water and food along the routes.
3. **Demand** in each region for the other's goods: West Africa needed **salt**; North Africa and Europe wanted **gold**.
4. **Guides**: the **Berbers** and **Tuareg** knew the desert routes.
5. **Strong states** at each end (the Sudanese empires and the North African states) that protected traders.
6. **Islam**, which gave traders a shared religion, law and system of **credit**.

## Organisation

1. **Caravans** of hundreds or thousands of camels travelled together for safety, usually in the cooler months.
2. **Caravan leaders** and **Tuareg guides** were hired; local rulers along the routes were paid **tolls** for protection.
3. **Muslim merchants** used **credit** and letters of exchange, so traders did not need to carry all their wealth.
4. At the southern "ports" such as **Timbuktu** and **Gao**, goods were transferred to **donkeys, porters and canoes** for the journey south.
5. Gold was sometimes exchanged by **silent trade (dumb barter)**: according to Arab and earlier accounts, traders left goods on a riverbank, withdrew, and the gold producers left gold beside them, each side adjusting until both were satisfied, without meeting.

## Importance to West Africa

1. **Wealth** for rulers through **taxes and tolls**, which financed armies and government.
2. **Growth of cities**: Timbuktu, Gao, Djenné, Kano, Katsina.
3. **Spread of Islam** and **Arabic literacy**.
4. **New goods and ideas**: horses, cloth, books, architecture.
5. **Rise of states**: Ghana, Mali, Songhai, Kanem-Bornu and the Hausa states depended on the trade.
6. **Contact with the wider world**, including the Mediterranean and Europe.

## Causes of decline

1. **The Atlantic trade:** from the sixteenth century, European ships traded directly on the **coast**, drawing gold and trade away from the desert routes.
2. **The Moroccan invasion** of Songhai (1591) and the insecurity that followed.
3. **Raids** by desert nomads on caravans.
4. **Political disorder** in North Africa and the Sudan.
5. Later, **colonial rule** and **railways** to the coast in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The trade was not mainly a slave trade. **Gold** and **salt** were the most important goods for most of its history, although enslaved people were also taken north across the desert.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Caravan**: a large group of traders and camels travelling together across the Sahara for safety.
- **Taghaza**: a place in the Sahara with rich salt mines, on the western trans-Saharan route.
- **Sijilmasa**: a trading town in Morocco at the northern end of the western trans-Saharan route.
:::

## Key points

- Made possible by the **camel, oases, demand for gold and salt, Berber guides, strong states and Islam**.
- Routes: **western** (Sijilmasa–Taghaza–Timbuktu), **central** (Tunis–Ghadames–Agadez–Gao/Kano), **eastern** (Tripoli–Fezzan–Bilma–Kanem).
- North: **gold, kola, ivory, slaves**. South: **salt, horses, cloth, books**.
- Effects: **wealth, cities, Islam, states, contact**.
- Decline: **Atlantic trade, Moroccan invasion, insecurity, colonial railways**.

## Quick check

1. Why was the camel important to the trans-Saharan trade?
2. Name two goods taken north and two taken south.
3. What was silent trade?
4. Name two cities that grew because of the trade.
5. Give two reasons for the decline of the trade.

**Answers:**

1. It could travel long distances without water and carry heavy loads across the desert.
2. North: any two of gold, kola nuts, ivory, leather, slaves. South: any two of salt, horses, cloth, copper, books.
3. Exchange without meeting: goods were left for the other side, which left gold in return, until both sides were satisfied.
4. Any two: Timbuktu; Gao; Djenné; Kano; Katsina; Walata.
5. Any two: the Atlantic trade on the coast; the Moroccan invasion; raids and insecurity; political disorder; colonial railways.
