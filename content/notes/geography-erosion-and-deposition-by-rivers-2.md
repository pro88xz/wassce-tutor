# River landforms: valleys, waterfalls, meanders and floodplains

## Learning objectives
You should be able to explain the formation of major river landforms, compare erosional and depositional features, and relate floodplain development to river behaviour.

## Upper-course landforms
A V-shaped valley forms where a river cuts downwards into its bed while weathering and mass movement widen the valley sides. The valley floor is narrow and the sides are steep. Interlocking spurs develop where the young river winds around projecting areas of resistant higher ground rather than cutting straight through them.

A waterfall may form where resistant rock overlies less resistant rock. The softer rock is eroded more rapidly, creating a step. Hydraulic action and abrasion deepen a plunge pool and undercut the resistant cap rock. The overhang eventually collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a gorge.

A gorge is a narrow, steep-sided valley often associated with waterfall retreat or rapid vertical erosion.

## Middle-course landforms
A meander is a bend in a river channel. In a typical meander, velocity and erosion are greater near the outer bank, where a river cliff or cut bank may develop. On the inner bank, lower velocity encourages deposition and the formation of a slip-off slope or point bar.

Meanders may become more pronounced through repeated outer-bank erosion and inner-bank deposition. A meander neck can become narrow. During a flood, the river may cut through the neck, abandoning the loop and forming an ox-bow lake. The abandoned channel may gradually fill with sediment and vegetation.

## Lower-course depositional landforms
A floodplain is a relatively flat area beside a river that is periodically inundated. During floods, water spreads beyond the channel and loses velocity, depositing fine alluvium. Repeated flooding builds a broad plain that may contain natural levees, backswamps and abandoned channels.

Natural levees form when floodwater first spills over the banks and deposits coarser sediment near the channel edge. Finer particles travel farther across the floodplain and may settle in lower areas. Levees can raise the channel banks relative to the surrounding floodplain, but they do not eliminate flood risk.

A delta may form where a river enters a sea or lake and deposits sediment faster than waves, tides and currents remove it. The river may divide into distributaries across the delta. Deltas require suitable sediment supply, shallow water and relatively low-energy conditions; not every river mouth develops one.

An estuary is a tidal river mouth where fresh water mixes with seawater. Strong tidal or wave action may prevent a large delta from forming.

## Worked explanation: ox-bow lake
1. Lateral erosion attacks the outer banks of a meander.
2. Deposition builds on inner banks and may narrow the neck.
3. A flood may cut through the narrow neck and create a straighter channel.
4. Deposition seals the ends of the abandoned loop.
5. The isolated loop becomes an ox-bow lake.

## Human significance and management
Floodplains provide fertile alluvial soils and relatively flat land for farming and settlement, but they are naturally flood-prone. Deltas may support agriculture, fisheries and ports, while also being vulnerable to subsidence, erosion and saltwater intrusion. Land-use zoning, flood forecasting, wetland protection and appropriate building standards reduce risk.

## WASSCE examination focus
Use a sequence of processes to explain each landform. For meanders, distinguish erosion on the outer bank from deposition on the inner bank. For deltas, explain why sediment accumulates at the river mouth and distinguish a delta from an estuary.

## Quick check
1. How does a waterfall retreat upstream?
2. Where does erosion occur in a meander?
3. Explain the formation of an ox-bow lake.
4. State two conditions favouring delta formation.

**Answers:** 1. Undercutting and collapse of the cap rock cause the waterfall step to retreat. 2. Mainly on the outer bank. 3. Neck narrows, a flood cuts through, and deposition seals the abandoned loop. 4. Large sediment supply, shallow low-energy water and limited removal by waves or tides.
