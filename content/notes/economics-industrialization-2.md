Many West African governments have tried to build factories, and many of those factories now stand idle. Industrialisation needs more than a building: power, skills, markets, capital and the right strategy. This lesson covers the strategies, the obstacles and the policies that make industry work.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- compare import substitution and export promotion as strategies of industrialisation
- explain the problems of industrialisation in West Africa
- suggest measures to promote industry
- discuss the role of small-scale industries and foreign investment
- explain the importance of mining to Sierra Leone's economy

## Strategies of industrialisation

### Import substitution

:::callout{type=definition}
**Import substitution industrialisation (ISI)** means producing at home goods that were previously imported, usually behind **tariffs or quotas** that protect the new industries from foreign competition.
:::

| Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| Saves foreign exchange | Protected firms may stay inefficient and costly |
| Creates local jobs | Consumers pay higher prices for poorer quality |
| Uses local raw materials | Many "local" factories still import machinery and inputs |
| Builds industrial skills | The home market may be too small for large-scale production |

### Export promotion

:::callout{type=definition}
**Export promotion (export-led industrialisation)** means encouraging industries that produce for **world markets**, for example by processing local raw materials for export.
:::

| Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| A large world market allows economies of scale | Strong foreign competition |
| Earns foreign exchange | Exposed to world price swings and trade barriers |
| Competition forces efficiency | Needs high quality standards and good infrastructure |

Many countries combine the two: protecting a few infant industries while helping exporters compete.

## Problems of industrialisation in West Africa

1. **Shortage of capital**: low incomes mean low savings; banks lend little for long-term projects.
2. **Unreliable power supply**: frequent outages force firms to run costly generators.
3. **Shortage of skilled labour and managers**, and the emigration of trained people.
4. **Small domestic markets**, because incomes are low.
5. **Poor infrastructure**: roads, ports, water and communications.
6. **Dependence on imported machinery, spare parts and inputs**, which become dear when the currency falls.
7. **Competition from cheap imports**, sometimes dumped below cost.
8. **Political instability and policy changes**, which discourage long-term investment.
9. **Inadequate raw materials** of the right quality and in steady supply.
10. **Corruption and red tape**, which raise the cost of doing business.

## Measures to promote industry

| Problem | Measure |
|---|---|
| Shortage of capital | Development banks, venture capital, incentives for foreign investment |
| Power | Investment in hydro, solar and grid supply |
| Skills | Technical and vocational education, apprenticeships |
| Small markets | Regional integration (ECOWAS) to widen the market |
| Infrastructure | Roads, ports, industrial estates with services |
| Competition from imports | Temporary protection for infant industries; anti-dumping duties |
| Instability | Consistent policies, rule of law, protection of property |
| Red tape | Simpler business registration and tax procedures |

## Small-scale industries

**Small-scale industries** (tailoring, carpentry, welding, soap making, block making, food processing) are very important in West Africa.

**Why they matter:** they need **little capital**; they are **labour-intensive** and create many jobs; they use **local raw materials** and skills; they spread development to **rural areas**; they train **entrepreneurs**; and they supply larger firms.

**Problems:** lack of credit, poor equipment, weak management and record keeping, competition from imports, and difficulty in marketing.

## Foreign investment

**Foreign direct investment (FDI)** is investment by foreign firms in factories, mines and businesses in the country.

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Brings capital the country lacks | Profits are sent abroad (repatriation) |
| Brings technology and management skills | Firms may exploit resources and damage the environment |
| Creates jobs and trains workers | Local firms may be crowded out |
| Earns foreign exchange through exports | Key sectors pass to foreign control |
| Pays taxes and royalties | Firms may seek special tax concessions |

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Mining** is Sierra Leone's largest source of export earnings. The country produces **iron ore, rutile, bauxite, diamonds and gold**, mostly through foreign-owned companies. Mining brings foreign exchange, royalties and jobs, but it employs relatively few people, is exposed to swings in world mineral prices, and can damage land and rivers. Processing more minerals at home would add value and jobs.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Industrialisation" does not just mean building factories. Without power, skilled labour, raw materials and a market, a factory cannot run profitably. In essays, link each problem to the condition it prevents.
:::

## Key points

- **Import substitution** produces imported goods at home behind protection; **export promotion** produces for world markets.
- Problems include **capital and skills shortages, unreliable power, small markets, poor infrastructure and import competition**.
- **Small-scale industries** create many jobs with little capital.
- **Foreign investment** brings capital and technology but also profit outflows and loss of control.
- **Mining** dominates Sierra Leone's exports.
