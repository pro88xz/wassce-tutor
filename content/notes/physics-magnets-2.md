The Earth itself is a giant magnet, and every compass on every ship relies on it. This lesson covers magnetic fields — how to map them, their patterns, and the Earth's own field.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a magnetic field and magnetic field lines
- plot magnetic field patterns with a compass or iron filings
- describe the fields of single magnets and pairs of magnets, including neutral points
- describe the Earth's magnetic field: declination and inclination (dip)

## Magnetic fields

:::callout{type=definition}
A **magnetic field** is the region around a magnet (or a current-carrying conductor) in which a magnetic force can be detected.

A **magnetic field line** (line of force) is the path along which a free north pole would move. Its direction at any point is the direction in which a compass needle's north pole points.
:::

![Magnetic field of a bar magnet: lines leave the north pole, curve round and enter the south pole, crowded together near the poles](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/bar-magnet-field.svg)

### Properties of field lines

1. They run from **north to south** outside a magnet.
2. They **never cross**.
3. They are **closest together** where the field is **strongest** (near the poles).
4. They behave as if they are stretched: they tend to shorten and they repel one another sideways.

## Plotting field lines

**Plotting compass method:**

1. Place the magnet on paper and mark its outline.
2. Put a small compass near the N pole; mark a dot at each end of the needle.
3. Move the compass so its tail is at the last dot; mark the new position of the head. Repeat until you reach the S pole.
4. Join the dots with a smooth curve and add arrows (N to S). Repeat from other starting points.

**Iron-filings method:** place a sheet of paper over the magnet, sprinkle iron filings and tap gently. The filings line up along the field lines, giving a quick picture of the pattern (but not its direction).

## Fields of pairs of magnets

- **Unlike poles facing (N–S):** lines run straight across from N to S — strong attraction.
- **Like poles facing (N–N):** lines from both poles curve away from each other. Midway between them is a **neutral point**.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **neutral point** is a point where the resultant magnetic field is **zero** — the fields of the magnets (and the Earth) cancel. A compass placed there does not point in any particular direction.
:::

## The Earth's magnetic field

The Earth behaves as if it contained a huge bar magnet, with its **south magnetic pole** near the **geographic North Pole** (which is why a compass's north pole is attracted towards it).

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Magnetic meridian**: the vertical plane containing the direction of the Earth's field (the direction a compass points).
- **Angle of declination (variation)**: the angle between the **geographic meridian** (true north) and the **magnetic meridian** at a place.
- **Angle of inclination (dip)**: the angle between the **Earth's magnetic field** and the **horizontal** at a place.
:::

| Location | Angle of dip |
|---|---|
| Magnetic poles | 90° (field vertical) |
| Magnetic equator | 0° (field horizontal) |
| Sierra Leone (near the magnetic equator) | small, roughly 0°–15° |

Declination is measured with a **declinometer**; dip is measured with a **dip circle** (a magnetic needle free to turn in a vertical plane set in the magnetic meridian).

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone lies close to the Earth's magnetic equator, so a dip needle here lies almost horizontal. Navigators correct their compass readings for the local declination when plotting a course along the coast.
:::

### Uses of the Earth's field

- **Navigation** with a magnetic compass (ships, aircraft, hikers).
- Many birds and sea turtles navigate using it.
- It shields the Earth from charged particles from the Sun; the **aurora** forms near the poles where these particles enter the atmosphere.

## Key points

- Field lines run N → S outside a magnet, never cross, and are densest where the field is strongest.
- Plot fields with a compass (gives direction) or iron filings (gives the pattern).
- A neutral point has zero resultant field.
- Declination: angle between true and magnetic north. Dip: angle between the Earth's field and the horizontal.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**Describe how to plot field lines** with a compass, **sketch field patterns** of single and pairs of magnets (marking neutral points), and **define declination and dip**. Objective items ask about properties of field lines and the angle of dip at the poles or equator.
:::

## Quick check

1. In which direction do magnetic field lines run outside a magnet?
2. What is a neutral point?
3. What is the angle of dip at the magnetic equator?

**Answers:** 1. From the north pole to the south pole. 2. A point where the resultant magnetic field is zero. 3. 0°.
