**Accounting concepts and conventions** are the basic assumptions and rules that guide how transactions are recorded and how financial statements are prepared, so that accounts are **consistent, reliable and comparable**. The most important are the **business entity**, **going concern**, **money measurement**, **historical cost**, **dual aspect**, **accruals (matching)**, **realisation**, **periodicity**, **consistency**, **prudence (conservatism)** and **materiality** concepts. This lesson explains each one with an example of how it is applied.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and purpose of accounting concepts and conventions
- explain each of the main concepts with an example
- apply the concepts to simple situations
- explain how the concepts affect the preparation of final accounts

## Why we need accounting concepts

Without agreed rules, two accountants could record the same transactions differently and produce different profits. Concepts make accounts:

- **consistent** from year to year,
- **comparable** between businesses,
- **reliable** and free from personal bias,
- **understandable** to users.

## The main concepts

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Business entity** | the business is treated as **separate from its owner** | the owner takes NLe 2,000 for a family expense: recorded as **drawings**, not a business expense |
| **Going concern** | the business is assumed to **continue operating** for the foreseeable future | equipment is shown at cost less depreciation, not at what it would fetch if sold off today |
| **Money measurement** | only items that can be **measured in money** are recorded | the skill of the staff or a good location is not recorded as an asset |
| **Historical cost** | assets are recorded at their **original cost** | land bought for NLe 80,000 stays at 80,000 even if now worth 120,000 |
| **Dual aspect** | every transaction has **two effects** | buying a van for cash: vehicles increase, cash decreases; the basis of double entry |
| **Accruals (matching)** | income and expenses are recorded when **earned or incurred**, not when cash moves; expenses are **matched** with the income they help to earn | December electricity unpaid at year end is still charged as an expense of that year |
| **Realisation** | revenue is recognised when goods or services are **delivered** and the customer is liable to pay, not when cash is received | goods sold on credit in March are sales of March |
| **Periodicity (accounting period)** | accounts are prepared for **regular periods**, usually a year | the year ending 31 December 2026 |
| **Consistency** | the same methods are used **year after year** | a business using straight-line depreciation does not switch each year to change its profit |
| **Prudence (conservatism)** | do not anticipate profits, but provide for **all known losses** | stock is valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value**; a provision is made for doubtful debts |
| **Materiality** | only items **significant** enough to affect users' decisions need separate or careful treatment | a stapler costing NLe 50 is written off as an expense, not treated as a non-current asset |
| **Objectivity** | records are based on **evidence**, not opinion | every entry is supported by a source document |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Name the concept applied in each case.**

1. A sole trader takes goods worth NLe 1,500 from the shop for his family. **Business entity:** recorded as drawings, because the owner and the business are separate.
2. Rent of NLe 3,000 for December is unpaid at 31 December but included as an expense for the year. **Accruals (matching).**
3. A customer is likely to be unable to pay NLe 4,000, so a provision is made for the loss. **Prudence.**
4. A building bought ten years ago for NLe 150,000 is still recorded at cost less depreciation. **Historical cost** (and **going concern**).
5. A waste bin costing NLe 80 is charged as an expense although it will last years. **Materiality.**
:::

## Concepts and the final accounts

- **Accruals and realisation** decide which income and expenses belong in this year's **income statement**, which is why we adjust for accruals and prepayments.
- **Prudence** explains provisions for **depreciation** and **doubtful debts** and the rule for valuing **stock (inventory)**.
- **Going concern** and **historical cost** decide how **non-current assets** are valued in the **statement of financial position**.
- **Business entity** explains why the owner's **capital** is shown as owed by the business to the owner.

:::callout{type=local-example}
A transport owner in Makeni uses his business minibus at weekends for family trips and pays for the fuel from the business till. Under the **business entity** concept, the fuel for private trips is **drawings**, not a business expense. If it were recorded as an expense, the business's profit, and its tax, would be wrongly stated.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Prudence does **not** mean deliberately understating profit or hiding assets. It means recognising **losses as soon as they are known** but **profits only when realised**, so that accounts are not over-optimistic.
:::

## Key points

- Concepts make accounts **consistent, comparable and reliable**.
- **Business entity:** owner and business separate. **Going concern:** business will continue.
- **Money measurement, historical cost, dual aspect, objectivity** govern what is recorded and at what value.
- **Accruals and realisation:** income when earned, expenses when incurred; match expenses to income.
- **Consistency, prudence, materiality, periodicity** govern how and when accounts are prepared.

## Quick check

1. What does the business entity concept state?
2. Which concept explains why an unpaid expense is included in this year's accounts?
3. Which concept explains why stock is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value?
4. A business changes its depreciation method every year. Which concept is broken?
5. Why is a pen costing NLe 20 not recorded as a non-current asset?

**Answers:**

1. The business is treated as separate from its owner, so the owner's private transactions are kept out of the business records (for example, as drawings).
2. The accruals (matching) concept.
3. The prudence (conservatism) concept.
4. The consistency concept.
5. Because of the materiality concept: the amount is too small to affect users' decisions.
