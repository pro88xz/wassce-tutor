A chemical equation is a recipe written in symbols: it shows what reacts, what forms, and in what proportions. Writing it correctly, balanced and with state symbols, is the first step in every calculation. This lesson covers writing and balancing equations, ionic equations, and the laws of chemical combination that equations obey.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- write word equations and balanced symbol equations with state symbols
- balance equations by inspection
- write ionic equations and identify spectator ions
- state and verify the laws of conservation of mass, constant composition and multiple proportions

## From words to symbols

**Word equation:** magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

**Symbol equation (unbalanced):** Mg + O₂ → MgO

**Balanced, with state symbols:**

$$2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{MgO(s)}$$

| State symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (s) | solid |
| (l) | liquid |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | aqueous: dissolved in water |

## Balancing equations

A balanced equation has the **same number of each kind of atom** on both sides, because atoms are neither created nor destroyed. Balance by changing the **coefficients** (the numbers in front), **never the subscripts** in a formula.

**Steps:**

1. Write the correct formula of every reactant and product.
2. Count the atoms of each element on each side.
3. Balance one element at a time, leaving hydrogen and oxygen (or a free element) until last.
4. Check every element again.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Balancing.**

1. Aluminium burning in oxygen:

$$4\text{Al(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)}$$

Check: Al 4 = 4; O 6 = 6.

2. Methane burning in oxygen:

$$\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

Check: C 1 = 1; H 4 = 4; O 4 = 2 + 2.

3. Propane (cooking gas) burning:

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Balance C first (3 CO₂), then H (4 H₂O), then O: $6 + 4 = 10$ atoms, so 5 O₂.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Never change a formula to balance an equation. Writing H₂O₂ instead of 2H₂O changes water into hydrogen peroxide, a different substance.
:::

## Ionic equations

Many reactions in solution involve only **some** of the ions present. The others, which appear unchanged on both sides, are **spectator ions** and are left out of the **ionic equation**.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Silver nitrate solution + sodium chloride solution.**

Full equation:

$$\text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)} + \text{NaNO}_3\text{(aq)}$$

Split the soluble ionic compounds into ions; Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ appear unchanged on both sides, so they are spectators. The ionic equation is:

$$\text{Ag}^+\text{(aq)} + \text{Cl}^-\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)}$$

A white precipitate of silver chloride forms. This is the test for chloride ions.
:::

| Reaction | Ionic equation |
|---|---|
| Any acid + alkali (neutralisation) | H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) |
| Barium ions + sulphate ions | Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) |
| Carbonate + acid | CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) |
| Zinc + copper(II) ions | Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) |

An ionic equation must balance **both atoms and charges**.

## Laws of chemical combination

### Law of conservation of mass

:::callout{type=definition}
**Matter is neither created nor destroyed** in a chemical reaction: the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**10.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated strongly in an open crucible.** It leaves 5.6 g of calcium oxide. How much carbon(IV) oxide escaped?

$$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$

By conservation of mass: $10.0 - 5.6 = 4.4$ g of CO₂ escaped. In a **closed** container the total mass would not change.
:::

### Law of constant (definite) composition

:::callout{type=definition}
**All pure samples of the same compound contain the same elements combined in the same proportion by mass**, however they are made.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Two samples of copper(II) oxide** were made by different methods.

- Sample A: 1.59 g of oxide gave 1.27 g of copper.
- Sample B: 3.18 g of oxide gave 2.54 g of copper.

% copper in A $= \dfrac{1.27}{1.59} \times 100 = 79.9\%$; % copper in B $= \dfrac{2.54}{3.18} \times 100 = 79.9\%$.

The percentages are equal, so the law is obeyed.
:::

### Law of multiple proportions

:::callout{type=definition}
If two elements form **more than one compound**, the different masses of one element that combine with a **fixed mass** of the other are in a **simple whole-number ratio**.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Carbon and oxygen.**

- In carbon(II) oxide, CO, 12 g of carbon combines with **16 g** of oxygen.
- In carbon(IV) oxide, CO₂, 12 g of carbon combines with **32 g** of oxygen.

The masses of oxygen combining with a fixed 12 g of carbon are in the ratio $16 : 32 = 1 : 2$, a simple whole-number ratio.
:::

The **law of reciprocal proportions** and **Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes** (gases) complete the laws of chemical combination. All of them are explained by Dalton's atomic theory: atoms combine in fixed whole numbers.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Cooking gas sold in Sierra Leone is mainly propane and butane. Balancing their combustion equations shows that each molecule needs a lot of oxygen. Burning gas in a closed room without air flow gives **incomplete combustion** and poisonous carbon(II) oxide:

$$2\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 7\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 6\text{CO} + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

This is why kitchens need ventilation.
:::

## Key points

- A balanced equation has equal numbers of each atom on both sides; balance with **coefficients, never subscripts**.
- **State symbols:** (s), (l), (g), (aq).
- **Ionic equations** leave out **spectator ions** and must balance atoms and charges; neutralisation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
- **Conservation of mass:** total mass is unchanged. **Constant composition:** fixed proportions by mass. **Multiple proportions:** simple whole-number ratios.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **balance equations** (including combustion), to **write ionic equations** for precipitation and neutralisation, and to **state and verify the laws of chemical combination** from data. In verification questions, show the percentage or ratio calculation clearly before drawing the conclusion.
:::

## Quick check

1. Balance: Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₃.
2. Write the ionic equation for the reaction of barium chloride solution with sodium sulphate solution.
3. State the law of constant composition.
4. Nitrogen forms N₂O and NO. Show that they obey the law of multiple proportions.

**Answers:**

1. 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃.
2. Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).
3. All pure samples of a compound contain the same elements combined in the same proportion by mass.
4. With a fixed 16 g of oxygen, N₂O has 28 g of N and NO has 14 g: ratio 28 : 14 = 2 : 1, a simple whole-number ratio.
