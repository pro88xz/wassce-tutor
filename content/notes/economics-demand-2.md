## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- list the factors that determine demand
- distinguish a change in quantity demanded from a change in demand
- explain exceptional demand curves and the types of demand

## Determinants of demand

Price is not the only thing that affects how much people buy. The other factors are:

| Factor | How it affects demand | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | Higher income usually raises demand for normal goods | Workers paid a bonus buy more meat and clothes |
| **Prices of substitutes** | If a substitute becomes dearer, demand for this good rises | Rice becomes dearer, so demand for gari rises |
| **Prices of complements** | If a complement becomes dearer, demand for this good falls | Fuel becomes dearer, so demand for motorbikes falls |
| **Tastes and fashion** | A good in fashion is demanded more | A new style of sandals becomes popular |
| **Population** | More people means more demand | A growing town needs more bread and water |
| **Expectations of future prices** | If prices are expected to rise, people buy more now | Families stock up on rice before an expected price rise |
| **Season and weather** | Some goods sell more at certain times | Umbrellas in the rainy season; uniforms before school opens |
| **Advertising** | Effective advertising raises demand | A drinks company's adverts before Christmas |

## Change in quantity demanded versus change in demand

This distinction is examined very often.

:::callout{type=definition}
A **change in quantity demanded** is caused **only by a change in the good's own price**. It is shown by a **movement along the same demand curve**: an *extension* (more demanded at a lower price) or a *contraction* (less demanded at a higher price).

A **change in demand** is caused by a change in **any other factor** (income, tastes, prices of other goods and so on). It is shown by a **shift of the whole demand curve**: to the **right** for an increase, to the **left** for a decrease.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Which is it?**

- The price of plantain falls from NLe 20 to NLe 15, so buyers take more. → **Extension** along the curve (change in quantity demanded).
- Civil servants receive a pay rise, so they buy more plantain at every price. → **Increase in demand**: the curve **shifts right**.
- The price of rice, a substitute, falls, so families buy less plantain at every price. → **Decrease in demand**: the curve **shifts left**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say "demand increased because the price fell". A fall in the good's own price increases the **quantity demanded** (a movement along the curve). "Demand increased" means the **whole curve shifted**, which needs some other cause.
:::

## Exceptional demand curves

Sometimes more is bought when the price **rises**, so the demand curve slopes **upwards**:

1. **Giffen goods**: very cheap staple foods eaten by very poor households. If the price of the staple rises, the family can no longer afford better foods and buys *more* of the staple to fill up.
2. **Goods of ostentation (Veblen goods)**: luxury items bought to show off wealth, such as expensive jewellery. A higher price makes them more attractive to some buyers.
3. **Expectations of further price rises**: when prices are rising and people expect them to keep rising, they may buy more now, even at the higher price.
4. **Ignorance**: some buyers believe a higher price means better quality.

## Types of demand

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint (complementary) demand** | Goods demanded together | Cars and fuel; tea and sugar |
| **Competitive demand** | Goods that are substitutes for each other | Rice and gari; Coca-Cola and Pepsi |
| **Derived demand** | Demand for something because of the demand for what it helps produce | Demand for bricklayers comes from the demand for houses |
| **Composite demand** | A good demanded for several different uses | Palm oil for cooking, soap and lamps |

## Key points

- Demand depends on **income**, **prices of related goods**, **tastes**, **population**, **expectations**, **season** and **advertising**.
- Own-price changes cause a **movement along** the curve; other factors cause a **shift** of the curve.
- **Exceptional demand curves** slope upwards: Giffen goods, goods of ostentation, expected price rises.
- Types of demand: **joint**, **competitive**, **derived**, **composite**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
A favourite question asks you to **distinguish between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded, with diagrams**. Draw two separate diagrams: one showing a movement along a single curve (label the points and arrows), the other showing the curve shifting from $D_1$ to $D_2$. Then give one cause of each.
:::

## Quick check

1. Petrol becomes much dearer. What happens to the demand for cars, and why?
2. Is "more bricklayers are needed because more houses are being built" joint, derived or composite demand?
3. Name one good that may have an exceptional (upward-sloping) demand curve.

**Answers:** 1. Demand for cars falls (the curve shifts left), because cars and petrol are complements (joint demand). 2. Derived demand. 3. For example: a Giffen good (a very cheap staple for the poorest households) or a good of ostentation (expensive jewellery).
