## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define a firm, a plant and an industry
- state the objectives of firms
- compare the main types of business organisation and their advantages and disadvantages

## Firm, plant and industry

:::callout{type=definition}
- A **firm** is a business unit that **organises factors of production** to produce goods or services, usually for profit. *Example: a bakery company.*
- A **plant** is a **single place of production**: a factory, workshop or mine. One firm may own several plants.
- An **industry** is a **group of firms producing similar goods**. *Example: all the bakeries in a country form the baking industry.*
:::

## Objectives of firms

1. **Profit maximisation**: the main aim in economic theory.
2. **Sales or revenue maximisation**: to win a bigger share of the market.
3. **Growth**: to expand and become more secure.
4. **Survival**: especially for new or small firms.
5. **Providing a service**: the main aim of public corporations and cooperatives.

## Types of business organisation

### Sole proprietorship

:::callout{type=definition}
A **sole proprietorship** (sole trader) is a business **owned and controlled by one person**, who takes all the profit and bears all the risk.
:::

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Easy and cheap to start | **Unlimited liability**: the owner's personal property can be taken to pay business debts |
| Owner makes quick decisions | Limited capital |
| Owner keeps all the profit | Long working hours, no one to share the work |
| Close contact with customers | The business may end when the owner dies or falls ill |

*Examples: a provision shop, a tailor, a barber, a market trader.*

### Partnership

:::callout{type=definition}
A **partnership** is a business owned by **two or more people (usually 2 to 20)** who agree to share capital, work, profits and losses, often under a written **deed of partnership**.
:::

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More capital than a sole trader | Unlimited liability for ordinary partners |
| Partners share work and bring different skills | Disagreements between partners |
| Losses are shared | Profits must be shared |
| | The partnership may end on the death or withdrawal of a partner |

*Examples: law firms, accounting firms, medical practices.* A **sleeping (dormant) partner** contributes capital but takes no part in running the business.

### Joint-stock (limited liability) companies

:::callout{type=definition}
A **joint-stock company** is a business owned by **shareholders**, who contribute capital by buying shares. It is a **separate legal entity** from its owners, and the shareholders have **limited liability**: they can lose only what they paid for their shares.
:::

| Private limited company (Ltd) | Public limited company (Plc) |
|---|---|
| Shares **cannot** be sold to the general public | Shares **can** be sold to the general public |
| Usually a small number of shareholders | Many shareholders |
| Less capital | Can raise very large capital |

**Advantages:** limited liability; large capital; **continuity** (the company continues even if shareholders die); professional managers.
**Disadvantages:** expensive and slow to form; accounts must be published; **separation of ownership and control** (managers may not act in shareholders' interests); decisions can be slow.

### Cooperatives

:::callout{type=definition}
A **cooperative society** is a business owned and run by its **members** for their **mutual benefit**, with each member having **one vote** regardless of shares held.
:::

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Producers' cooperative** | Farmers pool resources to buy inputs and market crops |
| **Consumers' cooperative** | Members buy goods in bulk and sell to themselves cheaply |
| **Credit and thrift (savings) cooperative** | Members save together and lend to each other |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Rice and cocoa farmers in rural Sierra Leone often form cooperatives to buy seed and fertiliser in bulk, share processing machines and sell their crops at better prices than each could get alone.
:::

### Public corporations (public enterprises)

:::callout{type=definition}
A **public corporation** is a business **owned and controlled by the government**, set up by law to provide an essential service. It is run by a board appointed by the government.
:::

*Examples in Sierra Leone: Guma Valley Water Company (water supply in Freetown) and EDSA, the Electricity Distribution and Supply Authority.*

**Reasons for public corporations:** to provide essential services, to avoid private monopolies exploiting consumers, to provide services private firms find unprofitable, and for national security.
**Problems:** inefficiency, political interference, losses that must be covered by taxpayers, and poor service.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **public limited company (Plc)** is **privately owned** by shareholders: "public" means its shares are sold to the public. A **public corporation** is **owned by the government**. Do not mix them up.
:::

## Key points

- **Firm** = business unit; **plant** = place of production; **industry** = group of firms making similar goods.
- Sole traders and partners have **unlimited liability**; company shareholders have **limited liability**.
- **Cooperatives**: one member, one vote, mutual benefit. **Public corporations**: government-owned, service-oriented.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **distinguish a firm from an industry**, to **compare two types of business organisation** (for example a sole proprietorship and a partnership) and to **explain limited liability**. In comparison questions, use a two-column table or matched points, so each difference is clear.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is the difference between a firm and an industry?
2. Why do shareholders of a limited company not lose their houses if the company fails?
3. Is a Plc owned by the government? Explain.

**Answers:** 1. A firm is a single business unit; an industry is all the firms producing similar goods. 2. They have limited liability: they can lose only the amount paid for their shares. 3. No. A Plc is owned by private shareholders; "public" means its shares can be bought by the public.
