:::callout{type=definition}
**Depreciation** is the part of the **cost of a non-current asset** that is used up in a period through **wear and tear, passage of time or becoming out of date**, and it is charged as an **expense** each year.
:::

The two main methods are the **straight-line method** (an equal charge each year) and the **reducing-balance method** (a fixed percentage of the falling carrying amount). Two related adjustments deal with customers who will not pay: **bad debts written off** and the **provision for doubtful debts**. This lesson explains how to calculate and record all three.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the meaning and causes of depreciation
- calculate depreciation by the straight-line and reducing-balance methods
- record depreciation using a provision for depreciation account
- record bad debts written off and recovered
- create, increase and decrease a provision for doubtful debts
- show these items in the final accounts

## Depreciation

**Causes:** wear and tear from use; passage of time (leases); obsolescence (new technology); depletion (mines, quarries).

| Method | Formula | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight line** | $\dfrac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual value}}{\text{Useful life}}$ | same charge every year |
| **Reducing balance** | rate % × **carrying amount** at the start of the year | high at first, then falling |

![Graph of the carrying amount of a NLe 100,000 machine over five years: straight line at 20% of cost falls evenly to zero; reducing balance at 30% falls quickly at first and reaches about 16,807 after five years](https://wasscetutor.com/img/accounting/depreciation-methods.svg)

*Figure 1. Straight-line and reducing-balance depreciation compared. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A machine cost NLe 100,000. Calculate depreciation for the first three years (a) at 20% a year straight line, (b) at 30% a year reducing balance.**

**(a) Straight line:** $20\% \times 100\,000 = 20\,000$ every year. Carrying amounts: 80,000; 60,000; 40,000.

**(b) Reducing balance:**

- Year 1: $30\% \times 100\,000 = 30\,000$; carrying amount $70\,000$
- Year 2: $30\% \times 70\,000 = 21\,000$; carrying amount $49\,000$
- Year 3: $30\% \times 49\,000 = 14\,700$; carrying amount $34\,300$
:::

**Which method?** Straight line suits assets used evenly (buildings, furniture). Reducing balance suits assets that lose value fast and need more repairs later (vehicles, machinery), so the total yearly cost of depreciation plus repairs stays more even.

### Recording depreciation

The asset stays at **cost** in its account; depreciation is accumulated in a **provision for depreciation** account:

- **Debit** income statement (depreciation expense); **credit** provision for depreciation.
- In the statement of financial position: **cost − accumulated depreciation = carrying amount (net book value)**.

| Machinery (statement of financial position) | NLe |
|---|---|
| Cost | 100,000 |
| Less accumulated depreciation (after year 2, reducing balance) | (51,000) |
| **Carrying amount** | **49,000** |

## Bad debts

:::callout{type=definition}
A **bad debt** is an amount owed by a customer that will **never be paid** (the customer is bankrupt, has died or disappeared).
:::

It is written off:

- **Debit** bad debts account; **credit** the customer's account.
- At the year end, the bad debts account is transferred to the **income statement** as an expense.

If a debt written off is later paid (**bad debt recovered**): reinstate the debt (debit customer, credit bad debts recovered), then record the cash received (debit cash, credit customer). Bad debts recovered is **income**.

## Provision for doubtful debts

Under **prudence**, a business estimates the part of its trade receivables that **may not be paid** and makes a **provision** for it, usually a percentage of receivables **after** writing off bad debts.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**At 31 December 2026 trade receivables were NLe 40,000, of which NLe 1,000 is to be written off as bad. A provision for doubtful debts of 5% is required. The existing provision is NLe 1,500.**

- Receivables after bad debts $= 40\,000 - 1\,000 = 39\,000$
- Required provision $= 5\% \times 39\,000 = 1\,950$
- **Increase** in provision $= 1\,950 - 1\,500 = 450$

**Income statement:** bad debts 1,000 and increase in provision 450 as expenses.

**Statement of financial position:** trade receivables $39\,000 - 1\,950 = 37\,050$.
:::

| Change in provision | Income statement | Entry |
|---|---|---|
| **Create or increase** | **expense** (the increase only) | Dr income statement, Cr provision |
| **Decrease** | **income** (the decrease only) | Dr provision, Cr income statement |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Only the **increase or decrease** in the provision for doubtful debts goes to the income statement, not the whole new provision. The **whole** provision is deducted from trade receivables in the statement of financial position.
:::

## Key points

- **Depreciation** spreads the cost of non-current assets over their useful lives.
- **Straight line:** (cost − residual) ÷ life. **Reducing balance:** rate × carrying amount.
- **Carrying amount = cost − accumulated depreciation.**
- **Bad debts** are written off as expenses; **recovered** bad debts are income.
- **Provision for doubtful debts:** only the **change** goes to the income statement; the **whole** provision is deducted from receivables.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **calculate depreciation by both methods**, to **prepare asset and provision for depreciation accounts**, and to **adjust for bad debts and the provision for doubtful debts** in final accounts. Read whether the rate is on **cost** (straight line) or on the **reducing balance**.
:::

## Quick check

1. State two causes of depreciation.
2. A van cost NLe 60,000, has a residual value of NLe 12,000 and a life of 4 years. Find the straight-line depreciation a year.
3. Equipment costing NLe 50,000 is depreciated at 20% reducing balance. Find the year 2 charge.
4. Receivables are NLe 20,000; provision required 5%; existing provision NLe 1,200. What goes to the income statement?
5. How are trade receivables shown in the statement of financial position in question 4?

**Answers:**

1. Any two: wear and tear; passage of time; obsolescence; depletion.
2. $\frac{60\,000 - 12\,000}{4} = 12\,000$: NLe 12,000.
3. Year 1: 10,000, carrying amount 40,000; year 2: $20\% \times 40\,000 = 8\,000$: NLe 8,000.
4. New provision $= 1\,000$, a decrease of NLe 200, shown as income.
5. $20\,000 - 1\,000 = 19\,000$: NLe 19,000.
