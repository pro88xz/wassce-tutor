Water boils at 100 °C, yet hydrogen sulphide, a similar but heavier molecule, is a gas at room temperature. Copper wire bends instead of shattering. Methane is a tetrahedron, not a flat cross. These facts come from metallic bonding, the forces between molecules, and the shapes molecules take. This lesson covers all three.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe metallic bonding and use it to explain the properties of metals
- describe van der Waals forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding
- explain the effects of hydrogen bonding on the properties of water
- predict the shapes and bond angles of simple molecules using electron-pair repulsion
- distinguish sigma and pi bonds and describe sp, sp² and sp³ hybridisation

## Metallic bonding

![Left: positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Right: hydrogen bonds between water molecules](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/metallic-hydrogen-bonds.svg)

*Figure 1. Metallic bonding and hydrogen bonding. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=definition}
**Metallic bonding** is the attraction between **positive metal ions** and a **sea of delocalised electrons** that move freely through the whole metal.
:::

| Property of metals | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Conduct electricity** | Delocalised electrons move through the metal when a voltage is applied |
| **Conduct heat** | Free electrons and vibrating ions pass on energy |
| **Malleable and ductile** | Layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the bond, because the electron sea moves with them |
| **High melting points** | Strong attraction between ions and electrons |
| **Lustrous (shiny)** | Free electrons reflect light |

The bond is stronger when ions have **more charge** and there are **more delocalised electrons**. Aluminium (3 electrons per atom) melts at 660 °C, much higher than sodium (1 electron) at 98 °C.

## Intermolecular forces

Forces **between** molecules are much weaker than covalent bonds **within** molecules. They decide melting points, boiling points and solubility of molecular substances.

| Force | Found between | Strength | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Van der Waals (dispersion) forces** | All molecules; caused by momentary uneven electron distribution | Weakest; increase with **molecular size** | Noble gases, I₂, CH₄; boiling points rise down the halogens |
| **Dipole–dipole forces** | Polar molecules (δ+ end attracts δ− end) | Stronger | HCl |
| **Hydrogen bonding** | Molecules with **H bonded to F, O or N** | Strongest of the three | H₂O, NH₃, HF, ethanol |

:::callout{type=definition}
A **hydrogen bond** is the attraction between a **hydrogen atom bonded to a very electronegative atom (F, O or N)** in one molecule and a **lone pair on F, O or N** in a neighbouring molecule.
:::

### Effects of hydrogen bonding

1. **High boiling point of water.** H₂O boils at 100 °C, while H₂S, with no hydrogen bonding, boils at −60 °C.
2. **Ice floats.** In ice, hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open lattice, so ice is **less dense** than liquid water.
3. **Solubility.** Ethanol, ammonia and sugar dissolve in water because they form hydrogen bonds with it.
4. **Surface tension and viscosity** of water are high.
5. **Biology.** Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands of DNA together and shape proteins.

## Shapes of molecules

**Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR):** electron pairs around a central atom **repel** each other and move **as far apart as possible**. **Lone pairs repel more strongly** than bonding pairs.

![Shapes of CO2 (linear, 180°), BF3 (trigonal planar, 120°), CH4 (tetrahedral, 109.5°), NH3 (pyramidal, 107°) and H2O (bent, 104.5°)](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/molecular-shapes.svg)

*Figure 2. Electron pairs spread out to give these shapes. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Molecule | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BeCl₂, CO₂ | 2 | 0 | **Linear**: atoms in a straight line | 180° |
| BF₃ | 3 | 0 | **Trigonal planar**: flat triangle | 120° |
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 | **Tetrahedral**: four corners in 3-D | 109.5° |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 | **Pyramidal**: three-sided pyramid | 107° |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 | **Bent**: V-shaped | 104.5° |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Why is the bond angle in water smaller than in methane?**

Both have **four electron pairs** around the central atom, arranged roughly tetrahedrally. Methane's four are all **bonding** pairs, giving 109.5°. Water has **two lone pairs**, which repel more strongly than bonding pairs and **squeeze** the H–O–H angle down to **104.5°**. Ammonia, with **one** lone pair, is in between at **107°**.
:::

## Sigma and pi bonds

| Sigma (σ) bond | Pi (π) bond |
|---|---|
| Formed by **head-on** overlap of orbitals | Formed by **sideways** overlap of p orbitals |
| Electron density on the line between the nuclei | Electron density above and below that line |
| Every single bond is one σ bond | Present only in **double** and **triple** bonds |
| Stronger; free rotation possible | Weaker; prevents rotation |

- A **double bond** = 1 σ + 1 π (C=C in ethene).
- A **triple bond** = 1 σ + 2 π (C≡C in ethyne, N≡N).

## Hybridisation (in brief)

Carbon's 2s and 2p orbitals **mix (hybridise)** to form equivalent orbitals for bonding.

| Hybridisation | Orbitals mixed | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sp³** | one s + three p | Tetrahedral, 109.5° | Methane, CH₄ |
| **sp²** | one s + two p (one p left for a π bond) | Trigonal planar, 120° | Ethene, C₂H₄ |
| **sp** | one s + one p (two p left for π bonds) | Linear, 180° | Ethyne, C₂H₂ |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Rainwater collected in drums behind houses in Freetown shows hydrogen bonding at work. Small insects walk on its surface because of **surface tension**. When water freezes in a cold box, the ice **floats** in a drink, because its hydrogen-bonded structure is more open than liquid water.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
When water boils, its **hydrogen bonds between molecules** break; the **O–H covalent bonds** inside each molecule do not. Steam is still H₂O.
:::

## Key points

- **Metallic bonding**: positive ions in a **sea of delocalised electrons**; explains conductivity, malleability and lustre.
- Intermolecular forces: **van der Waals** < **dipole–dipole** < **hydrogen bonding** (H bonded to F, O or N).
- Hydrogen bonding explains water's **high boiling point**, why **ice floats**, and the solubility of ethanol and sugar.
- **VSEPR**: pairs repel; lone pairs repel most. Shapes: **linear 180°, trigonal planar 120°, tetrahedral 109.5°, pyramidal 107°, bent 104.5°**.
- Double bond = **σ + π**; triple bond = **σ + 2π**; **sp³, sp², sp** hybridisation in alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **explain the properties of metals** using metallic bonding, to **define and explain hydrogen bonding** (especially water's boiling point and ice's density), and to **state the shapes and bond angles** of CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, BF₃ and CO₂ with reasons. Sigma and pi bonds and hybridisation appear in organic chemistry questions too.

## Quick check

1. Why are metals malleable?
2. Why does water have a much higher boiling point than hydrogen sulphide?
3. State the shape and bond angle of ammonia, and explain why the angle is less than 109.5°.
4. How many sigma and pi bonds are in a C≡C triple bond?

**Answers:**

1. Layers of metal ions can slide over each other while the delocalised electrons keep holding them together.
2. Water molecules are held by hydrogen bonds, which need more energy to break; H₂S has only weaker van der Waals and dipole forces.
3. Pyramidal, 107°: its lone pair repels the bonding pairs more strongly and squeezes the angle.
4. One sigma and two pi bonds.
