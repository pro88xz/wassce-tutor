Catching a fast ball, a car crash, a lift starting to move, two trolleys colliding: these are the problems where Newton's laws earn their keep. This lesson covers impulse, the conservation of momentum and the lift problems that appear year after year.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define impulse and relate it to change in momentum
- state and apply the principle of conservation of linear momentum
- distinguish elastic and inelastic collisions
- solve problems on lifts, recoil and connected bodies

## Impulse

:::callout{type=definition}
**Impulse** is the product of a force and the time for which it acts:

$$\text{impulse} = Ft = mv - mu$$

It equals the **change in momentum** and is measured in **N s** (= kg m s⁻¹).
:::

The same change in momentum can be produced by a large force for a short time or a small force for a long time. Making the time **longer** makes the force **smaller**:

- a cricketer or goalkeeper **draws the hands back** when catching
- car **crumple zones** and **airbags** lengthen the collision time
- you **bend your knees** when landing from a jump
- eggs are packed in soft trays; the ground under a high jump is padded

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 0.4 kg football moving at 15 m s⁻¹ is stopped by a goalkeeper in 0.05 s. Find the average force on his hands. If he draws his hands back so the catch takes 0.2 s, what is the force?**

Change in momentum $= 0.4 \times 15 = 6$ N s.

$$F = \dfrac{6}{0.05} = 120\ \text{N} \qquad F = \dfrac{6}{0.2} = 30\ \text{N}$$
:::

## Conservation of linear momentum

:::callout{type=definition}
**Principle of conservation of linear momentum:** when two or more bodies interact (collide or explode), their **total momentum remains constant**, provided **no external resultant force** acts on them.
:::

$$m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$$

![Before: a 2 kilogram trolley at 3 metres per second moves towards a 1 kilogram trolley at rest. After: they move off together at 2 metres per second. Total momentum is 6 kilogram metres per second before and after](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/collision-momentum.svg)

*Figure 1. Momentum before equals momentum after. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m s⁻¹ collides with a stationary 1 kg trolley and they stick together. Find their common velocity and the kinetic energy lost.**

$$2(3) + 1(0) = (2 + 1)v \Rightarrow v = 2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

KE before $= \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(3)^2 = 9$ J; KE after $= \tfrac{1}{2}(3)(2)^2 = 6$ J. Lost: **3 J** (to heat, sound and deformation).
:::

### Types of collision

| | Elastic collision | Inelastic collision |
|---|---|---|
| Momentum | conserved | conserved |
| Kinetic energy | **conserved** | **not** conserved (some lost) |
| Example | collisions of gas molecules; nearly, snooker balls | most real collisions; "perfectly inelastic" when bodies stick together |

### Explosions and recoil

Before an explosion the total momentum is often **zero**, so afterwards the momenta are equal and opposite.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A gun of mass 4 kg fires a 20 g bullet at 400 m s⁻¹. Find the recoil velocity of the gun.**

$$0 = 0.02 \times 400 + 4v \Rightarrow v = -\dfrac{8}{4} = -2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

The gun recoils at **2 m s⁻¹** opposite to the bullet. Holding the gun firmly against the shoulder adds the body's mass, reducing the recoil speed.
:::

## Lift problems

![A person standing in a lift: the normal reaction R acts upwards and the weight mg downwards; the lift accelerates upwards with acceleration a](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/lift-free-body.svg)

*Figure 2. Free-body diagram of a person in a lift. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

The reading on a bathroom scale in a lift equals the **normal reaction** $R$ on the person. Apply $F = ma$ with up positive:

| Motion of the lift | Equation | Scale reading |
|---|---|---|
| At rest or moving at **constant velocity** | $R - mg = 0$ | $R = mg$ (true weight) |
| **Accelerating upwards** (or slowing while moving down) | $R - mg = ma$ | $R = m(g + a)$, heavier |
| **Accelerating downwards** (or slowing while moving up) | $mg - R = ma$ | $R = m(g - a)$, lighter |
| **Falling freely** (cable broken) | $a = g$ | $R = 0$, "weightless" |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A 60 kg student stands on a scale in a lift. Find the reading when the lift (a) accelerates upwards at 2 m s⁻², (b) moves up at a steady 3 m s⁻¹, (c) accelerates downwards at 2 m s⁻². ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)**

(a) $R = 60(10 + 2) = 720$ N. (b) $R = 600$ N. (c) $R = 60(10 - 2) = 480$ N.
:::

## Connected bodies

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Two blocks of mass 3 kg and 2 kg on a smooth table are joined by a light string. A horizontal force of 15 N pulls the 3 kg block. Find the acceleration and the tension in the string.**

Treat both blocks together: $a = \dfrac{15}{3 + 2} = 3$ m s⁻².

The string alone pulls the 2 kg block: $T = 2 \times 3 = 6$ N.
:::

## Key points

- Impulse $Ft$ = change of momentum; longer contact time → smaller force.
- Total momentum is conserved when no external force acts: $m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$.
- Elastic: KE conserved; inelastic: KE lost; perfectly inelastic: bodies stick together.
- Lift: $R = m(g + a)$ going up faster, $R = m(g - a)$ going down faster.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
**State the principle of conservation of linear momentum** (include "no external force") and apply it to a collision or recoil; find the **kinetic energy lost** in an inelastic collision; explain **why a goalkeeper draws back his hands** or why vehicles have crumple zones using impulse. Lift problems appear frequently in objective papers.
:::

## Quick check

1. What is the SI unit of impulse?
2. A 1500 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ hits a wall and stops in 0.1 s. Find the average force.
3. A 50 kg woman stands in a lift accelerating downwards at 1 m s⁻². What does the scale read? ($g = 10$ m s⁻²)

**Answers:** 1. N s (equivalently kg m s⁻¹). 2. $\dfrac{1500 \times 20}{0.1} = 300\,000$ N. 3. $50(10 - 1) = 450$ N.
