How much copper is deposited when 2 A flows for an hour? Faraday answered this with two simple laws, and they let you calculate the mass of any element released by electrolysis — and even find the charge on a single electron.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state Faraday's first and second laws of electrolysis
- define the electrochemical equivalent and use $m = zIt$
- describe an experiment to find the electrochemical equivalent of copper
- describe primary and secondary cells

## Faraday's first law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Faraday's first law:** the **mass** of a substance liberated (deposited or dissolved) at an electrode is **directly proportional** to the **quantity of electricity** (charge) that passes:

$$m = zQ = zIt$$

where $z$ is the **electrochemical equivalent (e.c.e.)** of the substance: the mass liberated by **one coulomb** of charge. Its unit is **kg C⁻¹** (often given in g C⁻¹).
:::

| Element | e.c.e. (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen | $1.04 \times 10^{-8}$ kg C⁻¹ |
| Copper | $3.3 \times 10^{-7}$ kg C⁻¹ |
| Silver | $1.12 \times 10^{-6}$ kg C⁻¹ |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A current of 2.0 A flows through copper sulphate solution for 30 minutes. What mass of copper is deposited? (e.c.e. of copper = 3.3 × 10⁻⁷ kg C⁻¹)**

$$Q = It = 2.0 \times 1800 = 3600\ \text{C}$$

$$m = zQ = 3.3 \times 10^{-7} \times 3600 = 1.19 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg} = 1.19\ \text{g}$$
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How long must a current of 0.5 A flow to deposit 0.56 g of silver? (e.c.e. of silver = 1.12 × 10⁻⁶ kg C⁻¹)**

$$t = \dfrac{m}{zI} = \dfrac{0.56 \times 10^{-3}}{1.12 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.5} = 1000\ \text{s}$$
:::

## Faraday's second law

:::callout{type=definition}
**Faraday's second law:** when the **same quantity of electricity** passes through different electrolytes, the masses of the substances liberated are proportional to their **chemical equivalents** (relative atomic mass ÷ valency).
:::

The charge needed to liberate **one mole of a singly charged ion** is the **Faraday constant**, $F \approx 96\,500$ C mol⁻¹. For an ion of valency $n$, liberating one mole needs $nF$.

$$F = N_A e \Rightarrow e = \dfrac{96\,500}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$$

— which is how electrolysis gave one of the first values for the charge on an electron.

## Experiment: electrochemical equivalent of copper

1. Clean a copper cathode with sandpaper, wash, dry and weigh it ($m_1$).
2. Set up a copper voltameter in series with an ammeter, a rheostat, a switch and a battery.
3. Pass a **steady** current $I$ for a measured time $t$ (adjust the rheostat to keep $I$ constant).
4. Remove the cathode, wash it gently in water, dry it and weigh it again ($m_2$).
5. $z = \dfrac{m_2 - m_1}{It}$.

**Precautions:** keep the current constant and small (for an even, firmly attached deposit); clean the cathode before starting; dry the cathode carefully without rubbing off copper; ensure the cathode is connected to the negative terminal.

## Cells

A **cell** converts chemical energy into electrical energy.

| Primary cells | Secondary (rechargeable) cells |
|---|---|
| cannot be recharged; the chemical change is not reversible | can be recharged by passing a current backwards |
| e.g. dry Leclanché cell (torch batteries), simple voltaic cell | e.g. lead–acid accumulator (car batteries), nickel–cadmium, lithium-ion |

### The simple cell and its defects

A simple cell (copper and zinc plates in dilute sulphuric acid, e.m.f. about 1 V) quickly fails because of:

- **polarisation**: hydrogen bubbles collect on the copper plate, increasing internal resistance and setting up a back e.m.f. Cured by a **depolariser** (manganese(IV) oxide in the Leclanché cell).
- **local action**: impurities in the zinc form tiny cells that eat the zinc away even when no current is drawn. Cured by **amalgamating** the zinc with mercury.

### The lead–acid accumulator

- Plates of lead and lead(IV) oxide in dilute sulphuric acid; e.m.f. about **2 V** per cell (a 12 V car battery has six cells in series).
- It has a **very low internal resistance**, so it can deliver the large current needed by a car's starter motor.
- During charging, the **relative density** of the acid rises; it is checked with a **hydrometer** (about 1.25 when fully charged).
- Care: do not short-circuit it; keep the acid topped up with distilled water; recharge regularly.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Solar home systems, popular across Sierra Leone where the grid does not reach, store the day's solar energy in rechargeable batteries (lead–acid or lithium-ion) to run lights and phone chargers at night.
:::

## Key points

- Faraday's first law: $m = zIt$; e.c.e. $z$ in kg C⁻¹.
- Faraday's second law: equal charges liberate masses proportional to chemical equivalents; $F \approx 96\,500$ C mol⁻¹.
- Experiment: weigh the cathode before and after; $z = \Delta m / (It)$.
- Primary cells cannot be recharged; secondary cells (lead–acid) can, and have low internal resistance.

## Quick check

1. What is the unit of electrochemical equivalent?
2. Find the mass of copper deposited by 1 A in 1000 s (z = 3.3 × 10⁻⁷ kg C⁻¹).
3. Why can a car battery supply the very large current needed by a starter motor?

**Answers:** 1. kg C⁻¹. 2. $3.3 \times 10^{-7} \times 1000 = 3.3 \times 10^{-4}$ kg (0.33 g). 3. It has a very low internal resistance.
