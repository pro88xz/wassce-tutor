A family in Freetown can buy a plot outright and pass it to their children. A family in a village near Bo farms land that belongs to their lineage and was never bought or sold. A company that wants land for an oil palm plantation in the provinces must lease it from the landowning families through the chiefdom. Who may use land, for how long and on what terms is called **land tenure**, and it shapes what farmers are willing to invest in their land.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define land tenure and land tenure system
- describe the main types of land tenure system
- describe how land is held in Sierra Leone
- explain how land tenure affects agricultural production
- suggest ways of improving land tenure for farming

## What is land tenure?

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Land tenure**: the rules and relationships that decide how people obtain, use, control, transfer and inherit land.
- **Land tenure system**: the particular way land is owned or held in a society, such as communal, freehold or leasehold.
:::

Land tenure includes **rights** and **responsibilities**. A farmer may have the right to cultivate a plot without owning it, and an owner still has duties: to respect boundaries, neighbours and the law.

## The main types of land tenure system

| System | How land is obtained | Who holds the rights |
|---|---|---|
| **Communal (customary) tenure** | By being a member of the community or family | The community or lineage; members get user rights |
| **Inheritance** | Passed from parents to children | Family members, usually by custom |
| **Freehold (outright purchase)** | Buying the land | The owner, permanently |
| **Leasehold** | Agreement to use land for a fixed number of years | The lessee for the period; the owner keeps ownership |
| **Rent and tenancy** | Paying rent in money or produce, often yearly | The tenant uses the land; the landlord owns it |
| **Sharecropping** | Paying with a share of the harvest | The sharecropper uses the land for the season |
| **Gift** | Given freely by an owner | The person receiving the gift |
| **Pledging** | Land given to a lender as security for a loan, used until the loan is repaid | The lender uses it for a time; the owner keeps ownership |
| **State (government) ownership** | Land vested in or acquired by the government | The state, which may lease it out |

Each type has its own lesson in this topic.

## Land tenure in Sierra Leone

Sierra Leone has **two main systems**:

1. **The Western Area** (Freetown and the peninsula) follows English-style law. Land can be held **freehold** and bought and sold, and transfers are recorded at the Ministry of Lands.
2. **The provinces** follow **customary tenure**. Most land belongs to **landowning families**, with the **paramount chief** and chiefdom council as custodians. Members of the family farm it by right; others obtain land by **lease**, **rent**, **sharecropping** or permission.

:::callout{type=local-example}
A farmer from outside a chiefdom (a "stranger" farmer) who wants to grow rice or vegetables usually asks the landowning family or the chief for permission, and may give part of the harvest or a small payment in return. Investors who want large areas for plantations or mining must lease the land; under Sierra Leone's laws they cannot buy freehold land in the provinces. Land laws passed in 2022, including the Customary Land Rights Act, aim to make land deals more open and to give women equal rights to family land.
:::

## How land tenure affects agriculture

### Secure tenure helps farming

When a farmer knows the land will stay in their hands, they are willing to:

- plant **tree crops** (cocoa, coffee, oil palm, cashew) that take years to bear
- invest in **soil conservation**, fertiliser, irrigation and fencing
- use the land as **security** to borrow from a bank

### Insecure tenure holds farming back

- Farmers with short or uncertain rights avoid long-term improvements and may **exhaust the soil**.
- **Fragmentation**: inheritance divides land into ever smaller plots, too small for mechanisation.
- **Land disputes** over boundaries and inheritance take land out of use and waste money on court cases.
- Communal land usually **cannot be used as collateral** for a loan.
- Young people, women and settlers may find it **hard to get land** at all.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not confuse **owning** land with **having the right to use** it. A tenant, lessee or sharecropper has the right to use the land for a time, but the land still belongs to someone else.
:::

## Improving land tenure for farming

- **Survey and register** land so that boundaries and owners are clear.
- Give **written agreements** for leases, rents and sharecropping, with fair terms and long enough periods.
- Settle disputes quickly through chiefdom authorities or land tribunals.
- Protect the land rights of **women and young farmers**.
- Use **consolidation**: farmers exchange scattered plots to make larger, workable farms.
- Government can provide land for **farm settlements** and youth farming schemes.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Explaining an effect.** "Explain how insecure land tenure reduces agricultural production."

A farmer who may lose the land next season will not plant tree crops or spend on fertiliser and soil conservation, because the benefits come after they have left. They farm for the short term, the soil becomes exhausted and yields fall. Insecure tenure also means the land cannot be offered as collateral, so the farmer cannot borrow to buy inputs.
:::

## Key points

- **Land tenure** is the set of rules deciding how land is obtained, used, controlled, transferred and inherited.
- Types include **communal**, **inheritance**, **freehold**, **leasehold**, **rent and tenancy**, **sharecropping**, **gift**, **pledging** and **state ownership**.
- Sierra Leone: **freehold** in the Western Area; **customary** family land in the provinces, where others get land by lease, rent or permission.
- **Secure** tenure encourages long-term investment; **insecure** tenure leads to soil exhaustion, disputes, fragmentation and lack of credit.
