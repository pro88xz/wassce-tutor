# The kidney and urinary system: urine formation and water balance

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- explain ultrafiltration in the glomerulus
- describe selective reabsorption along the nephron

- describe the role of ADH in water balance
- distinguish normal urine from substances that should normally remain in the blood

## Overview of urine formation

Urine is formed in the nephrons through three linked processes: **ultrafiltration, selective reabsorption and tubular secretion**. The processes do not mean that the kidney removes everything from the blood. Useful substances are returned to the blood, while wastes and excess substances remain in the fluid that becomes urine.

### 1. Ultrafiltration

Blood enters the glomerulus under relatively high hydrostatic pressure. This pressure forces water and small dissolved substances through the filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule. These substances include water, glucose, amino acids, mineral ions and urea.

Blood cells and most large plasma proteins are normally retained in the capillaries because they are too large to pass through the filtration barrier. The fluid entering Bowman's capsule is called the **glomerular filtrate**. It resembles blood plasma but normally contains virtually no blood cells and very little protein.

Ultrafiltration is a pressure-driven physical process; it is not the same as selective reabsorption. The kidney first filters small substances, then selectively retrieves what the body needs.

### 2. Selective reabsorption

As the filtrate passes along the nephron, useful substances move from the tubule back into surrounding capillaries. This is called selective reabsorption.

The proximal convoluted tubule reabsorbs all the glucose and amino acids under normal conditions, together with most of the filtered water and salts. Glucose and amino acids are transported across the tubule wall by carrier proteins; sodium gradients help drive their uptake. Active transport uses energy indirectly or directly, so PCT cells contain many mitochondria. Water follows dissolved substances by osmosis.

Further adjustment occurs in the loop of Henle, DCT and collecting duct. The loop of Henle helps establish a high solute concentration in the medulla. This gradient allows water to leave the collecting duct when it is permeable to water. Mineral ions are also regulated along the tubule.

### 3. Tubular secretion

Some substances move from the blood in the surrounding capillaries into the nephron tubule. This is called tubular secretion. It helps remove certain drugs, hydrogen ions, potassium ions and other substances, and contributes to regulation of blood pH and ion balance.

## How the kidney concentrates urine

The body must conserve water when intake is low and remove more water when intake is high. The loop of Henle and the medullary concentration gradient help the kidney produce urine of different concentrations.

The descending limb of the loop of Henle is relatively permeable to water, so water leaves the filtrate into the increasingly concentrated medulla. The ascending limb is much less permeable to water and moves salts out of the filtrate. This contributes to the concentration gradient in the medulla. The collecting duct passes through this gradient.

**Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)**, released from the posterior pituitary gland, regulates the permeability of the collecting ducts to water. When the blood becomes too concentrated or water levels fall, more ADH is released. More water channels are inserted into collecting-duct cells, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of concentrated urine is produced. When the blood is more dilute, less ADH is released; less water is reabsorbed and a larger volume of dilute urine is produced.

ADH therefore regulates water reabsorption; it does not directly make the kidney produce urea.

## Composition of normal urine

Normal urine is mainly water, with dissolved urea, mineral salts and other small waste substances. It normally contains no glucose and no significant amount of protein or blood cells. Glucose in urine may indicate that blood glucose has exceeded the kidney's reabsorptive capacity, as can occur in diabetes mellitus. Protein or blood in urine may indicate damage or disease affecting the filtration barrier or urinary tract and requires medical assessment.

## Common urinary disorders

- **Kidney stones:** hard deposits of mineral salts that may obstruct urine flow and cause severe pain.
- **Urinary tract infection (UTI):** infection involving the urethra, bladder or, in more serious cases, kidneys.
- **Kidney failure:** loss of the kidneys' ability to regulate blood composition and remove wastes adequately. Severe cases may require dialysis or transplantation.

Dialysis uses a selectively permeable membrane and a carefully controlled fluid to remove wastes and excess salts from blood. It can replace some kidney functions but does not reproduce every regulatory and hormonal function of healthy kidneys.

4. What happens to urine volume when ADH secretion increases?
