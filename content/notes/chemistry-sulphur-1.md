Sulphur is the yellow element that gives matches their head, hardens rubber for tyres and is the raw material for sulphuric acid, the most widely made industrial chemical. Burned, it gives sulphur(IV) oxide, a choking gas that preserves fruit juice but also causes acid rain. This lesson covers sulphur, its allotropes and sulphur(IV) oxide.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- state the occurrence of sulphur and describe its extraction by the Frasch process
- describe the allotropes of sulphur and the transition temperature
- describe the effect of heat on sulphur
- state the chemical properties and uses of sulphur
- describe the preparation, test, properties and uses of sulphur(IV) oxide
- explain how sulphur(IV) oxide bleaches

## Occurrence and extraction

- **Free (native):** in underground deposits and volcanic regions.
- **Combined:** in **sulphide ores** (galena PbS, zinc blende ZnS, iron pyrites FeS₂) and **sulphates** (gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O); in **crude oil and natural gas** as hydrogen sulphide and organic sulphur compounds. Today most sulphur is **recovered from oil and gas** during refining.

**The Frasch process** (for underground deposits): three concentric pipes are sunk into the deposit.

1. **Superheated water** (about 170 °C, under pressure) is pumped down the outer pipe and **melts** the sulphur (melting point 115 °C).
2. **Hot compressed air** is blown down the innermost pipe.
3. A **froth of molten sulphur**, air and water is forced up the middle pipe and solidifies in large vats. The sulphur is about 99.5% pure.

## Allotropes of sulphur

:::callout{type=definition}
**Allotropy** is the existence of an element in **two or more forms in the same physical state**, with different arrangements of atoms. The forms are called **allotropes**.
:::

| Property | Rhombic (α) sulphur | Monoclinic (β) sulphur |
|---|---|---|
| Crystal shape | **Octahedral** (eight-faced) | **Needle-shaped** (prismatic) |
| Colour | Bright yellow | Pale yellow |
| Stable | **Below 95.5 °C** | Between **95.5 °C and 119 °C** |
| Density | 2.06 g/cm³ (denser) | 1.98 g/cm³ |
| Made by | Evaporating a solution of sulphur in carbon disulphide (CS₂) or methylbenzene | Cooling molten sulphur slowly; needles form as it solidifies |

The **transition temperature** is **95.5 °C**: below it rhombic sulphur is stable, and above it monoclinic. Left at room temperature, monoclinic sulphur slowly changes back into rhombic.

**Plastic (amorphous) sulphur** forms when nearly boiling sulphur is poured into cold water. It is a brown, rubbery, elastic solid of long tangled chains. It slowly hardens back into rhombic sulphur.

## Effect of heat on sulphur

| Temperature | Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| About 115 °C | Melts to a **pale amber, runny** liquid | S₈ rings move freely |
| About 180 °C | Becomes **dark brown and very viscous** (will not pour) | Rings break and join into long, tangled chains |
| Above about 250 °C | Becomes **runny** again | Long chains break into shorter ones |
| 444 °C | **Boils** to a brown vapour | |

## Chemical properties of sulphur

**1. Burns in air** with a **blue flame**, forming sulphur(IV) oxide:

$$\text{S(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$$

**2. Combines with metals** on heating, forming sulphides: Fe + S → FeS (the mixture glows); Cu + S → CuS.

**3. With hydrogen:** H₂ + S → H₂S.

**4. Oxidised by concentrated nitric or sulphuric acid** to sulphuric acid.

**Uses of sulphur:**

- making **sulphuric acid** (most important)
- **vulcanising rubber**: sulphur cross-links rubber chains, making car tyres hard and durable
- **matches and fireworks** (and gunpowder)
- **fungicides and insecticides** for crops
- medicines and **skin ointments** (for scabies and fungal infections)
- making carbon disulphide and dyes

## Sulphur(IV) oxide, SO₂

**Laboratory preparation:** add dilute hydrochloric acid to **sodium sulphite**, or heat **copper with concentrated sulphuric acid**:

$$\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{SO}_2$$

$$\text{Cu} + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(conc.)} \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{SO}_2$$

Dry it with concentrated sulphuric acid; collect by **downward delivery** (it is denser than air and very soluble in water).

**Test:** it turns **orange acidified potassium dichromate(VI)** paper **green**. It also decolourises purple acidified potassium manganate(VII).

**Properties:**

- colourless gas with a **choking**, irritating smell; poisonous
- denser than air; very soluble in water
- easily liquefied
- **acidic oxide**: dissolves in water to form **sulphurous acid** (sulphuric(IV) acid), which turns blue litmus red

$$\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3\text{(aq)}$$

- **reducing agent** (in the presence of water): it reduces dichromate (orange → green), manganate(VII) (purple → colourless), iron(III) to iron(II), and chlorine to chloride
- with **hydrogen sulphide** it acts as an **oxidising agent**: 2H₂S + SO₂ → 3S + 2H₂O

### How SO₂ bleaches

Sulphur(IV) oxide bleaches **by reduction**, in the presence of water. It removes oxygen from the coloured dye, leaving a colourless product. The bleaching is **temporary**: air slowly **re-oxidises** the dye, so the colour returns. This is why paper and straw bleached with SO₂ turn yellow with age. Chlorine, which bleaches by **oxidation**, gives **permanent** bleaching.

**Uses of SO₂:**

- making **sulphuric acid** (Contact process)
- **bleaching** wool, silk, straw and wood pulp, which chlorine would damage
- **preserving** fruit juices, dried fruit and wine (it kills microbes)
- **fumigating** stores and killing pests

:::callout{type=local-example}
Car and truck tyres used on Sierra Leone's roads are made from **vulcanised rubber**: raw rubber is heated with about 3% sulphur, which links the rubber chains together. Natural rubber was once tapped on plantations in Sierra Leone. Unvulcanised rubber becomes sticky in the heat and brittle in the cold; vulcanising makes it strong and elastic.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Sulphur(IV) oxide bleaches by **reduction** and the effect is **temporary**; chlorine bleaches by **oxidation** and the effect is **permanent**. Both need water.
:::

## Key points

- Sulphur occurs **native**, in **sulphide and sulphate ores** and in **crude oil and gas**; the **Frasch process** uses superheated water and hot air.
- Allotropes: **rhombic** (octahedral, stable below **95.5 °C**) and **monoclinic** (needles, 95.5–119 °C); **plastic sulphur** forms on quenching.
- Heating sulphur: runny amber → viscous dark brown (chains) → runny again → boils at 444 °C.
- Sulphur burns with a **blue flame** to SO₂; used for **H₂SO₄, vulcanising rubber, matches, fungicides**.
- **SO₂:** choking, acidic, a **reducing agent**; turns **acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ green**; bleaches by **reduction** (temporary); preserves food.

## Quick check

1. Define allotropy and name two allotropes of sulphur.
2. What is the transition temperature of sulphur?
3. Give the test for sulphur(IV) oxide.
4. Why does paper bleached with SO₂ turn yellow with time?

**Answers:**

1. The existence of an element in two or more forms in the same physical state; rhombic and monoclinic sulphur.
2. 95.5 °C.
3. It turns orange acidified potassium dichromate(VI) green.
4. SO₂ bleaches by reduction; oxygen in the air slowly re-oxidises the dye, so the colour returns.
