:::callout{type=definition title="Key figure"}
**Samori Toure (about 1830–1900)** was the Mandinka ruler who built a powerful empire in the Western Sudan and **resisted French conquest for about seventeen years**, from **1882 to 1898**, longer than almost any other West African leader.
:::

He succeeded for so long because of his **well-organised standing army**, his **own production and purchase of modern guns**, his **scorched-earth tactics**, his **mobility** in moving his whole empire eastwards, and his skilful **diplomacy**. He was finally captured at **Guelemou** on **29 September 1898** and exiled to **Gabon**, where he died in 1900. This lesson explains **Samori Toure's resistance**, why it lasted so long and why it failed, and compares it with other resistance to the **French in West Africa**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the rise of Samori Toure and the organisation of his empire
- outline the course of his resistance to the French
- explain why Samori resisted for so long
- explain why he was finally defeated
- describe other examples of resistance to French conquest, such as Lat Dior and Behanzin
- explain the different forms of African reaction to European conquest

## Forms of African reaction

African rulers reacted to European conquest in different ways:

| Reaction | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Armed (military) resistance** | fighting to defend independence | Samori Toure, Behanzin, Yaa Asantewaa, Bai Bureh |
| **Diplomatic resistance** | using treaties and negotiation to keep independence | Samori's treaties with France; Jaja of Opobo; Prempeh I of Asante |
| **Collaboration (alliance)** | cooperating with Europeans for advantage, often against rivals | some rulers who signed treaties of protection to gain help against enemies |
| **Passive resistance** | refusing to cooperate, migrating, avoiding tax or labour | people who fled across borders to avoid forced labour |

## Rise of Samori Toure

Samori was born about **1830** near **Sanankoro** in present-day **Guinea**, the son of a **Mandinka (Dyula) trader**. As a young man he was a trader, then a soldier. From the **1860s and 1870s** he built a large empire, sometimes called the **Wassoulou** or Mandinka empire, with its capital at **Bissandugu**. In **1884** he took the title **Almami** (religious and political leader) and used **Islam** to unite his people.

**Organisation of his empire:**

1. **Provinces** under governors, each with an army unit.
2. A **council** of advisers, and **qadis** (Islamic judges).
3. A **professional standing army** of **sofas** (infantry), with **cavalry**, trained in European methods by men who had served in French forces.
4. **Workshops** where blacksmiths **made and repaired guns** and ammunition, copying French rifles.
5. **Trade**: he sold **gold, ivory and captives** and bought **modern rifles**, especially through traders from **Freetown**.

## Why Samori resisted for so long

1. **A strong standing army** that was well trained, disciplined and loyal.
2. **Modern weapons**: he bought **repeating rifles** from Freetown traders and his blacksmiths copied and repaired guns.
3. **Scorched-earth policy**: as he retreated he **burned crops and villages** so the French could find no food.
4. **Mobility**: he **moved his whole empire eastwards** instead of defending one capital.
5. **Diplomacy**: he signed treaties to gain time and tried to play the **British against the French**.
6. **Islam and leadership**: as Almami he united his people with religion and personal authority.
7. **Good intelligence and organisation**, with supplies, spies and a clear chain of command.
8. **Knowledge of the terrain**, using forests, hills and rivers.

## Why Samori was defeated

1. **Superior French weapons**, including artillery and later the **Maxim gun**.
2. **The British refused to help** him or sell him arms after their agreements with France; the arms trade through Freetown was cut.
3. **Scorched-earth tactics** turned many people **against him**, and some conquered peoples **revolted** or helped the French.
4. His **second empire** brought him into conflict with neighbouring peoples and with the British in the Gold Coast hinterland.
5. **Famine** and exhaustion weakened his army and followers.
6. The French **surrounded** him from several directions and finally **surprised** his camp at Guelemou.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Samori had close links with **Sierra Leone**. His agents bought **rifles and ammunition in Freetown**, and he sent envoys to the colonial government asking for **British protection** against France. Britain refused, because it had agreed with France on spheres of influence. Some Sierra Leone peoples, especially in the north-east (Koinadugu and Kono), suffered from raids by Samori's sofas in the 1880s.
:::

## Other resistance to the French

| Leader | State | Resistance |
|---|---|---|
| **Lat Dior** | **Cayor** (Senegal) | opposed the French **Dakar–Saint-Louis railway** through his land; killed in battle at **Dekhelé, 1886** |
| **Behanzin** | **Dahomey** | fought the French in **1890** and **1892–94** with an army that included the famous women soldiers; surrendered in **1894** and was exiled to **Martinique** |
| **Ahmadu Seku** | **Tukulor empire** | resisted until the French took **Segou (1890)** and **Nioro (1891)** |
| **Mahmadou Lamine** | Senegal valley | led a Muslim rising against the French, **1885–1887** |

## Why African resistance generally failed

1. **Superior European weapons**: Maxim guns, artillery and repeating rifles.
2. **African disunity**: states did not combine against Europeans and often helped them against rivals.
3. **African soldiers in European armies**, such as the **Tirailleurs Sénégalais** and the **West India Regiment** and **Frontier Police**.
4. **European resources**: money, supplies and reinforcements from overseas.
5. **Divide-and-rule diplomacy** and treaties that tricked rulers.
6. **Internal problems**, such as revolts by conquered peoples and disputes over succession.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Do not say that Samori "failed because he was a poor leader". He was one of the ablest military leaders of his time. He was defeated mainly by **French military superiority**, the **loss of his arms supply** and **opposition among conquered peoples**.
:::

## Key points

- Reactions to conquest: **armed resistance, diplomacy, collaboration, passive resistance**.
- **Samori Toure** built the **Mandinka empire** (capital **Bissandugu**) and became **Almami** in **1884**.
- He resisted the French from **1882 to 1898**; captured at **Guelemou**, exiled to **Gabon**, died **1900**.
- Long resistance: **standing army, modern guns, scorched earth, mobility, diplomacy, Islam**.
- Failure: **French weapons, no British help, loss of arms supply, revolts, famine**.
- Other resisters: **Lat Dior**, **Behanzin**, **Ahmadu Seku**, **Mahmadou Lamine**.
