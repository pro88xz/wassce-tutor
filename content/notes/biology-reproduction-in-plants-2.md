# Reproduction in plants: fertilisation, seed formation and propagation

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- describe what happens after pollen reaches a stigma
- explain fertilisation in a flowering plant
- relate ovules and ovaries to seeds and fruits
- state the conditions required for seed germination
- compare sexual reproduction with vegetative propagation

## From pollination to fertilisation

After a compatible pollen grain lands on a stigma, it may absorb moisture and germinate. A pollen tube grows from the pollen grain through the stigma and down the style towards an ovule in the ovary. The male gametes travel through this tube.

In flowering plants, the pollen tube enters an ovule, usually through a small opening called the micropyle. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell to form a diploid zygote. The zygote develops into the embryo of the seed.

Flowering plants show **double fertilisation**. The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei in the embryo sac to form a usually triploid primary endosperm nucleus. The endosperm develops into nutritive tissue that supports the developing embryo. The term double fertilisation refers to these two fusion events within the same embryo sac.

## Formation of seeds and fruits

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed. The integuments of the ovule form the seed coat, which protects the embryo. The embryo contains an embryonic root called the radicle and an embryonic shoot called the plumule. One or two cotyledons are present, depending on the plant group. Food may be stored in the cotyledons or in the endosperm.

The ovary usually develops into the fruit. The ovary wall becomes the fruit wall, called the pericarp. Fruits protect seeds and often help disperse them. In some plants, other flower parts also contribute to the mature fruit structure.

A seed is therefore not the same as a fruit. The seed develops from an ovule; the fruit develops mainly from the ovary. This distinction is frequently tested.

## Seed dispersal

Dispersal moves seeds away from the parent plant. It reduces overcrowding and competition and may allow a species to colonise new areas.

Common methods include:

- **Wind:** very small or winged seeds can be carried by air currents.
- **Water:** floating fruits or seeds can be transported by water.
- **Animals:** hooks may attach to fur, while fleshy fruits may be eaten and seeds later deposited elsewhere.
- **Explosive mechanisms:** dry fruits split suddenly and scatter seeds.

A seed's structure often reflects its dispersal method. A wing increases the area exposed to air; hooks help a seed attach to an animal; a buoyant fruit can remain afloat.

## Conditions for germination

Germination is the resumption of growth of the embryo in a viable seed. Most seeds require:

- **Water:** activates enzymes, softens the seed coat and allows stored food to be mobilised.
- **Oxygen:** supports aerobic respiration, which releases energy for growth.
- **Suitable temperature:** allows enzyme-controlled reactions to proceed at an appropriate rate.

Some seeds also require particular light conditions or a period of dormancy to be broken. Not every seed requires light to germinate.

During germination, the radicle usually emerges first and develops into the primary root. The plumule develops into the shoot. The young seedling initially uses stored food until its leaves develop and photosynthesis can supply more organic material.

## Investigating germination

A fair investigation can compare seeds supplied with different conditions. For example, place equal numbers of similar viable seeds on moist cotton wool in separate containers. Give one group access to air at a suitable temperature, another group insufficient water, and another group very limited oxygen. Keep seed type, number, temperature and observation period as similar as possible.

A suitable control group receives water and oxygen at a suitable temperature. Record the number of seeds that germinate each day and calculate the percentage germination:

**Percentage germination = (number germinated ÷ total number tested) × 100**

Use several seeds in each group because individual seeds may differ in viability. A single seed is not enough for a reliable conclusion.

## Asexual reproduction and vegetative propagation

Vegetative propagation is a form of asexual reproduction in which new plants develop from vegetative organs such as stems, roots or leaves. It does not involve fertilisation or seed formation.

Examples include:

- **Stem cuttings:** a piece of stem is planted and develops roots and shoots.
- **Runners:** horizontal stems grow along the soil surface and form new plantlets at nodes.
- **Tubers:** underground swollen stems, such as Irish potato, contain buds called eyes that can grow into new shoots.
- **Bulbs:** short stems with fleshy storage leaves, as in onion, can produce new shoots.
- **Suckers:** shoots arising from the base or underground parts of a parent plant can be separated and replanted.

Cassava is commonly propagated by stem cuttings in West Africa. This method allows farmers to establish a crop without relying on seed germination. Vegetative propagation can preserve desirable characteristics and produce plants relatively quickly, but offspring usually have limited genetic variation. It can also transfer pests or diseases from the parent material if cuttings are unhealthy.

## Sexual and asexual reproduction compared

| Feature | Sexual reproduction | Asexual reproduction |
|---|---|---|
| Gamete fusion | Involves fertilisation | No fertilisation |
| Number of parents | Usually two gametes, which may come from one or two plants | One parent |
| Offspring variation | Usually greater genetic variation | Usually genetically very similar to parent |
| Propagules | Seeds in flowering plants | Vegetative parts such as stems, roots or leaves |
| Agricultural use | Produces seed and new combinations of traits | Rapidly multiplies plants with desired characteristics |

## WASSCE examination focus

Be able to distinguish pollination from fertilisation, and seed from fruit. Explain the two fusion events in double fertilisation if required. For germination questions, name water, oxygen and a suitable temperature and explain the role of each.

For vegetative propagation, name the plant part used and give a crop example. Do not claim that vegetative propagation produces seeds; it produces new plants from vegetative structures.

## Quick check

1. What grows from the zygote after fertilisation?
2. Which part of the flower usually develops into the fruit?
3. Name the three main conditions required for germination.
4. How is cassava commonly propagated?
5. Give one advantage and one limitation of vegetative propagation.

**Answers:** 1. The embryo. 2. The ovary. 3. Water, oxygen and a suitable temperature. 4. By stem cuttings. 5. It rapidly preserves desirable traits, but produces little genetic variation and may transmit diseases from the parent plant.
