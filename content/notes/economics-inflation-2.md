Inflation hurts a pensioner on a fixed income but may help a trader who borrowed to buy stock last year. It does not affect everyone in the same way. This lesson explains who gains and who loses, how governments fight inflation, and the opposite problem: deflation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the effects of inflation on different groups and on the economy
- describe monetary, fiscal and direct measures to control inflation
- define deflation and explain its causes, effects and remedies
- distinguish deflation, disinflation and stagflation

## Effects of inflation

### Who loses?

1. **Fixed-income earners**: pensioners and workers whose wages do not rise with prices; their **real income falls**.
2. **Savers**: money saved loses purchasing power if interest is lower than inflation.
3. **Creditors (lenders)**: they are repaid in money worth less than when they lent it.
4. **Consumers generally**, especially the poor, who spend most of their income on food and fuel.
5. **Exporters**: home prices rise, so exports become less competitive.

### Who gains?

1. **Debtors (borrowers)**: they repay with money worth less.
2. **Traders and producers** whose selling prices rise faster than their costs (holders of stock).
3. **Owners of real assets** such as land, houses and goods, whose value rises.
4. **The government**, as a major debtor, and because tax revenue rises with money incomes.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Savings and inflation.** Fatmata saves NLe 10,000 for a year at 8% interest, while inflation is 20%.

- At the end of the year she has $10000 \times 1.08 = $ NLe 10,800.
- Goods that cost NLe 10,000 now cost $10000 \times 1.20 = $ NLe 12,000.
- Her savings now buy only $\dfrac{10800}{12000} \times 100 = 90\%$ of what they bought before: a **real loss of about 10%**.

The **real interest rate** is roughly $8\% - 20\% = -12\%$: negative.
:::

### Effects on the economy

| Mild inflation | High inflation |
|---|---|
| Can encourage firms to produce (profits rise) | Discourages saving and long-term investment |
| Eases the burden of debts | Causes hoarding and speculation |
| | Makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, worsening the balance of payments |
| | Causes strikes and wage disputes |
| | Redistributes income unfairly |
| | Leads to a fall in the exchange rate and a loss of confidence in the currency |
| | Makes planning by firms and government difficult |

## Controlling inflation

### Monetary policy (by the central bank)

1. **Raise the policy rate** (interest rates), making borrowing dearer and saving more attractive.
2. **Sell government securities** (open market operations) to take money out of the banks.
3. **Raise the cash reserve ratio**, so banks can lend less.
4. **Selective credit controls** and moral suasion to restrict lending.

### Fiscal policy (by the government)

1. **Reduce government spending**, especially spending financed by borrowing.
2. **Raise direct taxes**, reducing people's disposable income and spending.
3. **Run a budget surplus** (or a smaller deficit).

### Direct and other measures

1. **Price controls** on essential goods (though these can cause shortages and black markets).
2. **Wage restraint** (an incomes policy): limiting pay rises to productivity growth.
3. **Increasing production**, especially of food, to cure supply shortages.
4. **Improving infrastructure** (roads, power, storage) to remove bottlenecks.
5. **Reducing import duties** on essential goods, or importing to fill shortages.
6. **Stabilising the exchange rate**, to reduce imported inflation.

:::callout{type=local-example}
To bring down the high inflation of 2022–2023, the **Bank of Sierra Leone raised its monetary policy rate** and tightened credit, while the government aimed to cut borrowing from the central bank. Supporting local rice production, through programmes such as **Feed Salone**, also targets one cause: dependence on imported food.
:::

## Deflation

:::callout{type=definition}
**Deflation** is a **persistent fall in the general price level**, which **raises the value of money**.
:::

**Causes:** a big fall in total demand (cuts in spending, high taxes, tight credit); a sharp fall in the money supply; a large rise in output with no matching rise in demand; falling world prices.

**Effects:** falling profits, so firms cut production and lay off workers (**unemployment**); people delay purchases expecting lower prices, so demand falls further; the real burden of **debts rises**; investment falls.

**Remedies:** lower interest rates and a lower reserve ratio; more government spending and lower taxes; buying back securities to add money to the economy; encouraging exports.

### Related terms

- **Disinflation**: the **rate** of inflation falls, but prices are still rising (for example from 30% to 15%).
- **Stagflation**: **high inflation together with stagnant output and high unemployment**, often caused by a sharp rise in costs such as fuel.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
"Inflation is falling" usually means **disinflation**, not deflation. Prices are still rising, only more slowly.
:::

## Key points

- Inflation **hurts** fixed-income earners, savers, creditors and exporters; it **helps** debtors, holders of goods and the government.
- High inflation discourages saving and investment and worsens the balance of payments.
- Control by **monetary policy** (higher interest rates, less credit), **fiscal policy** (less spending, more tax) and **direct measures** (price and wage controls, more output).
- **Deflation** is a persistent fall in prices; it causes unemployment and falling demand.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Very common: **explain the effects of inflation** (on named groups, or on the economy) and **suggest measures to control it**, sorted into monetary, fiscal and direct. Also asked: **define deflation** and its effects. For each measure, explain *how* it reduces demand or raises supply.
:::

## Quick check

1. Why do debtors gain from inflation?
2. Give two monetary measures the central bank can use to reduce inflation.
3. What is the difference between deflation and disinflation?
4. Interest on savings is 10% and inflation is 15%. Roughly what is the real interest rate?

**Answers:** 1. They repay loans with money that has lost purchasing power. 2. Any two: raise the policy rate, sell securities, raise the reserve ratio, selective credit controls, moral suasion. 3. Deflation is a fall in the price level; disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation while prices still rise. 4. About $10\% - 15\% = -5\%$.
