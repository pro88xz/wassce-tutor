Biology is learned with the hands and eyes as well as from books. WASSCE Biology has a **practical paper** in which you observe specimens, draw them, test foods, calculate magnification and interpret experiments, and practical skills also appear in the theory papers. Many candidates who know the facts lose marks here because they do not know the **conventions**: how to draw, how to label, what colour change a food test gives, why an experiment needs a control. This lesson teaches each skill and the rules examiners use to mark it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name the parts of a light microscope and use it correctly
- calculate magnification
- make a biological drawing that follows the rules
- carry out and interpret the food tests
- design a fair experiment with variables and a control
- describe methods of sampling organisms in the field
- observe and describe specimens in the practical examination

## The light microscope

![A compound light microscope with its parts labelled: eyepiece lens, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, coarse and fine focus knobs, stage with clips, slide, condenser, diaphragm, mirror or lamp, and base](https://wasscetutor.com/img/biology/microscope-parts.svg)

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **eyepiece** | the lens you look through; usually magnifies ×10 |
| **objective lenses** | magnify the specimen (e.g. ×4, ×10, ×40) |
| **revolving nosepiece** | turns to change the objective |
| **stage and clips** | hold the slide |
| **coarse focus knob** | moves the tube a lot; for **low power** only |
| **fine focus knob** | small movements for sharp focus, especially at **high power** |
| **condenser and diaphragm** | focus and control the amount of light |
| **mirror or lamp** | provides light |

### Using it correctly

1. Start with the **lowest power** objective.
2. Look from the **side** while lowering the objective close to the slide, so you do not crush it.
3. Look through the eyepiece and focus **upwards** with the coarse knob.
4. Move to a higher power and use **only the fine focus**.

### Magnification

:::callout{type=definition}
**Magnification of the microscope:**

$$\text{eyepiece} \times \text{objective}$$

**Magnification of a drawing:**

$$\frac{\text{length of the drawing}}{\text{actual length of the specimen}}$$

Use the **same units** for both lengths. $1$ mm $= 1000$ µm.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**(a) An eyepiece of ×10 is used with a ×40 objective. Find the magnification. (b) A drawing of a seed is 60 mm long; the seed is 15 mm long. Find the magnification of the drawing. (c) A cell measures 30 mm in a photograph magnified ×1500. Find its actual size in µm.**

**(a)** $10 \times 40 = $ **×400**

**(b)** $\dfrac{60}{15} = $ **×4**

**(c)** Actual size $= \dfrac{30}{1500} = 0.02$ mm $= 0.02 \times 1000 = $ **20 µm**
:::

## Biological drawing

:::callout{type=definition}
**The rules examiners use:**

1. Use a **sharp HB pencil**: never pen.
2. Draw with **clear, single, continuous lines**: no sketchy or broken lines.
3. **No shading** and no colouring.
4. Make the drawing **large**: usually at least half the available space.
5. Keep the **proportions** of the parts correct.
6. **Label lines** are ruled with a pencil, end exactly on the part, and **never cross** each other; keep labels to the side, preferably in a column.
7. Write a **title** (the name of the specimen and the view, e.g. "Transverse section of a dicot stem") and the **magnification** (e.g. ×3).
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Drawing what you expect instead of what you see.** The drawing is marked against the **specimen** in front of you. If the specimen leaf has five leaflets, do not draw seven from a textbook. Also, an arrowhead on a label line, or a label line that stops short of the part, can cost a mark.
:::

## Food tests

| Food | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| **starch** | add iodine solution | yellow-brown → **blue-black** |
| **reducing sugar** (e.g. glucose) | add Benedict's (or Fehling's) solution and **heat** in a water bath | blue → green → yellow → **brick-red** precipitate |
| **non-reducing sugar** (e.g. sucrose) | first boil with dilute HCl, neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate, then do the Benedict's test | **brick-red** (only after the acid step) |
| **protein** | add sodium hydroxide, then a few drops of copper(II) sulfate (**Biuret test**); or Millon's reagent and warm | **violet / purple** (Biuret); brick-red (Millon's) |
| **fat / oil** | rub on paper (**grease spot test**) | a **translucent** spot that does not dry |
| **fat / oil** | add **Sudan III** | a **red-stained** oily layer |
| **fat / oil** | shake with ethanol, pour into water (**emulsion test**) | a **cloudy white** emulsion |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A student tests a crushed groundnut. Iodine stays brown; Benedict's stays blue after heating; Biuret turns violet; the grease spot test gives a translucent spot. What does the groundnut contain?**

No starch and no reducing sugar; **protein** and **fat (oil)** are present.
:::

:::callout{type=tip}
**Always run a control.** Test water alongside the food. If water also changes colour, your reagent or tube is contaminated and the result means nothing.
:::

## Designing an experiment

:::callout{type=definition}
| Term | Meaning | Example: effect of temperature on enzyme activity |
|---|---|---|
| **hypothesis** | a testable prediction | "Amylase works fastest at about 37 °C" |
| **independent variable** | what you change | temperature |
| **dependent variable** | what you measure | time for starch to disappear |
| **controlled variables** | what you keep the same | volume and concentration of starch and amylase, pH |
| **control** | a set-up without the factor being tested, for comparison | starch with boiled (denatured) amylase |
| **replicates** | repeats, to check reliability | three tubes at each temperature |
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A student puts a potted plant in a dark cupboard for 48 hours, then covers part of one leaf with black paper and leaves the plant in sunlight. After six hours she tests the leaf for starch. Explain each step.**

- **48 hours in the dark** destarches the leaf, so any starch found later was made during the experiment.
- **Black paper** keeps light from part of the leaf: that part is the **control**.
- **Result:** only the uncovered part turns blue-black with iodine.
- **Conclusion:** **light is necessary for photosynthesis** (starch is made only where light reaches the leaf).
:::

## Sampling in the field

| Method | Used for |
|---|---|
| **quadrat** (a square frame, e.g. 1 m × 1 m) | plants and slow animals; count or estimate % cover in random positions |
| **line or belt transect** | changes across a habitat, e.g. from a swamp edge to dry land |
| **pitfall trap** | small crawling animals on the ground |
| **sweep net** | insects in grass |
| **pooter** | collecting small insects without harming them |
| **Tullgren funnel** | small animals in soil and leaf litter (they move away from the heat of a lamp) |
| **Baermann funnel** | small animals living in soil water, such as nematodes |
| **capture–recapture** | estimating the size of a population of mobile animals |

## Observing specimens

In the practical paper you are given specimens (such as a fruit, a seed, an insect, a bone or a leaf) and asked to observe, classify and compare them. A good approach:

1. **Look carefully** and use a hand lens. Note the **observable features**: shape, number of parts, colour, texture, type of symmetry.
2. **Classify**: name the group the specimen belongs to and give the features that show it. For example, an insect has **three body parts, three pairs of jointed legs and one pair of antennae**.
3. **Compare** two specimens in a table with matching rows: one feature per row, with both specimens described.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Specimens from home.** WASSCE specimens are usually everyday things: a mango, an orange, a groundnut, a maize grain, a cockroach, a grasshopper, a hibiscus flower, a cassava leaf. Practise at home: cut an orange across to see its **segments (carpels)** and **axile placentation**, split a soaked groundnut to see the **two cotyledons**, and draw each one following the rules above.
:::

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Eyepiece ×15, objective ×10. Find the total magnification."}
×150.
:::

:::reveal{title="2. A drawing of a grasshopper is 90 mm long; the insect is 30 mm long. What is the magnification?"}
$90 \div 30 = $ ×3.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. State the reagent and the positive result for (a) starch, (b) reducing sugar."}
(a) Iodine solution: blue-black. (b) Benedict's solution, heated: brick-red precipitate.
:::

:::reveal{title="4. Give three rules for labelling a biological drawing."}
Any three: label lines ruled in pencil; lines touch the part they name; lines do not cross; no arrowheads; labels placed to the side in a neat column; label words printed clearly.
:::

:::reveal{title="5. In an experiment on how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis, name the independent, dependent and one controlled variable."}
Independent: light intensity. Dependent: rate of photosynthesis (e.g. bubbles of oxygen per minute). Controlled: temperature, carbon dioxide concentration, the same plant or species.
:::

## Key points

- Microscope: start on **low power**, focus **upwards**, use **fine focus** at high power.
- **Magnification** = eyepiece × objective; drawing magnification = drawing length ÷ actual length (same units).
- Drawing: **pencil, clean lines, no shading, large, ruled labels that do not cross, title and magnification**.
- Food tests: **iodine** (starch), **Benedict's + heat** (reducing sugar), **Biuret** (protein), **grease spot / Sudan III / emulsion** (fat).
- Experiments: change **one** variable, measure another, control the rest, include a **control** and **replicates**.
- Sampling: **quadrats, transects, traps, nets, funnels**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
The practical paper (Paper 3) tests observation of specimens, biological drawings with labels and magnification, food tests and their results, and questions on experimental design. The theory papers also ask about the microscope, magnification calculations, food tests and controls. Follow the drawing rules exactly; they carry a large share of the practical marks.
:::
