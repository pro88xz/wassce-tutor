**Contours** are lines on a map that join places of **equal height above sea level**. They are the main way topographical maps show **relief**: contours close together mean a **steep slope**, contours far apart mean a **gentle slope**, and the **pattern** of the contours shows the shape of landforms such as hills, spurs, valleys, escarpments and plateaux. From contours you can draw a **cross-section** to show the shape of the land and calculate the **gradient** between two points. This lesson explains all three skills.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the methods of showing relief on maps
- explain contours, the contour interval, spot heights and trigonometrical stations
- identify slopes and landforms from contour patterns
- draw a cross-section and calculate its vertical exaggeration
- calculate the gradient between two points
- decide whether two points are intervisible

## Methods of showing relief

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Contours** | lines joining places of equal height; the most accurate method |
| **Spot heights** | dots with the exact height written beside them |
| **Trigonometrical stations** | triangles marking surveyed points, usually on hilltops |
| **Layer colouring (tints)** | different colours for height bands, from green (low) to brown and white (high) |
| **Hill shading** | shading on slopes as if lit from the north-west |
| **Hachures** | short lines drawn down the slope; thicker and closer on steep slopes |
| **Form lines** | dashed lines that show the general shape where heights are not surveyed |

:::callout{type=definition}
The **contour interval** (vertical interval) is the difference in height between neighbouring contours, for example **50 m** on many 1:50,000 maps.
:::

## Slopes

| Contour pattern | Slope |
|---|---|
| close together | **steep** |
| far apart | **gentle** |
| evenly spaced | **even (uniform)** |
| close at the bottom, far apart at the top | **convex** |
| far apart at the bottom, close at the top | **concave** |
| no contours over a wide area | **flat** land |

## Landforms from contour patterns

![Six contour patterns: a conical hill as rings with the highest in the middle; a spur with Vs pointing downhill; a valley with Vs pointing uphill and a river; an escarpment with close contours on the steep side and wide contours on the gentle side; a plateau with close contours round a large flat top; and a saddle between two hills](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/contour-landforms.svg)

*Figure 1. Contour patterns of common landforms. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Landform | Contour pattern |
|---|---|
| **Conical hill** | roughly circular contours, highest in the centre |
| **Ridge** | long, narrow, closed contours: an elongated hill |
| **Spur** | a tongue of high land; the contours form **V or U shapes pointing to lower ground** |
| **Valley** | the contours form **V shapes pointing upstream to higher ground**, usually with a river |
| **Escarpment** | **close** contours on the steep scarp slope, **wide** contours on the gentle dip slope |
| **Plateau** | close contours around the edges and a **large, flat top** |
| **Saddle (col)** | a low point between two hills on a ridge |
| **Gap** | a deep pass cut through a ridge, often used by a river, road or railway |
| **Cliff** | contours that run together or a special cliff symbol |

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Spurs and valleys look alike. Check the **heights**: in a **valley** the V points to **higher** ground (upstream) and often holds a river; on a **spur** the V points to **lower** ground.
:::

## Drawing a cross-section

![Top: a line A–B across contours from 100 to 400 metres with the crossing points marked. Bottom: the same points plotted on a graph at their heights and joined into a smooth profile of the hill](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/cross-section.svg)

*Figure 2. Drawing a cross-section from a contour map. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

1. Place the straight edge of a **strip of paper** along the line A–B.
2. Mark the ends (A and B) and every point where a **contour, river, road or other feature** crosses the edge, writing the **height** of each contour.
3. Draw the **base line** of the graph the same length as A–B and a **vertical scale** for height (for example, 1 cm represents 100 m).
4. Lay the strip on the base line and **plot each height** directly above its mark.
5. Join the points with a **smooth curve** (not straight lines); rivers are in the lowest parts.
6. Add a **title**, the **scales**, labels for features, and the **vertical exaggeration**.

### Vertical exaggeration

Cross-sections usually stretch the height so that the shape can be seen:

$$\text{Vertical exaggeration} = \frac{\text{horizontal scale (RF)}}{\text{vertical scale (RF)}}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A map is 1:50,000. The vertical scale of a cross-section is 1 cm to 100 m. Find the vertical exaggeration.**

Vertical scale: 1 cm represents 100 m $= 10\,000$ cm, so the vertical RF is 1:10,000.

Vertical exaggeration $= \dfrac{50\,000}{10\,000} = 5$ times.
:::

## Gradient

:::callout{type=definition}
The **gradient** is the steepness of a slope between two points: the **difference in height** (vertical interval) divided by the **horizontal distance** (horizontal equivalent), both in the **same units**. It is usually written as **1 in x**.
:::

$$\text{Gradient} = \frac{\text{vertical interval (VI)}}{\text{horizontal equivalent (HE)}}$$

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Point P is 450 m above sea level and point Q is 250 m. On a 1:50,000 map they are 8 cm apart. Find the gradient from P to Q.**

Vertical interval $= 450 - 250 = 200$ m.

Horizontal equivalent $= 8 \times 0.5 = 4$ km $= 4\,000$ m.

Gradient $= \dfrac{200}{4\,000} = \dfrac{1}{20}$, which is **1 in 20**.
:::

## Intervisibility

Two points are **intervisible** if they can be seen from each other. Draw a cross-section between them and join the two points with a straight line: if any higher land rises above the line, they are **not intervisible**. Convex slopes often block the view; concave slopes do not.

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Contour**: a line on a map joining places of equal height.
- **Spot height**: a dot on a map with the exact height written beside it.
- **Spur**: a tongue of high land; its contours form V or U shapes pointing to lower ground.
- **Ridge**: a long, narrow hill shown by long, closed contours.
- **Escarpment**: a steep slope on one side of a hill, with a gentle slope on the other.
- **Cross-section**: a side view of the land along a line, drawn from the contours.
:::

## Key points

- Relief is shown by **contours**, **spot heights**, **trig stations**, **layer colouring**, **hill shading**, **hachures**.
- **Close** contours: steep; **wide** contours: gentle; patterns show **hills, spurs, valleys, escarpments, plateaux, saddles**.
- In a **valley**, contour Vs point **upstream**; on a **spur**, Vs point **downhill**.
- **Cross-section:** paper strip, plot heights, smooth curve; **VE** $=$ horizontal RF ÷ vertical RF.
- **Gradient** $=$ VI ÷ HE in the same units, written **1 in x**.

## Quick check

1. What is a contour?
2. What do contours close together show?
3. How can you tell a valley from a spur on a map?
4. A map is 1:50,000 and the vertical scale is 1 cm to 50 m. Find the vertical exaggeration.
5. Two points differ in height by 150 m and are 3 km apart. Find the gradient.

**Answers:**

1. A line on a map joining places of equal height above sea level.
2. A steep slope.
3. In a valley the contour Vs point upstream to higher ground and there is often a river; on a spur the Vs point to lower ground.
4. Vertical RF is 1:5,000, so VE $= 50\,000 \div 5\,000 = 10$.
5. $\dfrac{150}{3\,000} = \dfrac{1}{20}$: 1 in 20.
