**Cash crops** are grown mainly to be **sold**, often for export, rather than eaten by the farm family. In Sierra Leone the most important are **cocoa** and **coffee** in the east, **oil palm** across the south and east, and **cashew**, **ginger**, **kola** and **sugarcane** in different areas. Many are **tree crops** grown for decades in plantations or smallholder farms. They earn farmers cash and the country foreign exchange, but they need patience, care and processing to get a good price. This lesson describes the main plantation and cash crops and the cultivation and processing of cocoa and oil palm in detail.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name the main cash and plantation crops of Sierra Leone and their uses
- describe the cultivation of cocoa, including fermentation and drying
- describe the cultivation of oil palm and explain the Dura, Pisifera and Tenera types
- describe the processing of palm fruit into palm oil and kernel oil
- state the importance and problems of cash crop production

## The main cash crops

| Crop | Scientific name | Main areas and notes | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cocoa** | *Theobroma cacao* | the east (Kailahun, Kenema, Kono); smallholder farms | cocoa beans → chocolate, cocoa butter |
| **coffee** (robusta) | *Coffea canephora* | the east, often alongside cocoa | coffee beans |
| **oil palm** | *Elaeis guineensis* | across the south and east; wild groves, smallholdings and estates | palm oil, palm kernel oil, palm wine |

| **coconut** | *Cocos nucifera* | coastal areas | copra, oil, coconut water |

## Cocoa

### Cultivation

1. **Climate and soil:** warm, humid, with well-distributed rainfall; deep, well-drained, fertile forest soil; **shade** when young.
2. **Nursery:** seeds from ripe pods of good parent trees (or improved hybrid seed) sown in **polybags** under shade; seedlings ready at **4–6 months**.
3. **Field:** clear forest partly, leaving some tall trees for permanent shade; plant **temporary shade** such as **plantain or banana**, which also gives food and income while the cocoa grows.
4. **Planting:** about **3 m × 3 m** (about 1100 trees per hectare), in holes filled with topsoil.
5. **Care:** weed regularly (ring weeding around each tree); prune to shape and remove diseased parts; fertilise; gradually thin the shade as trees grow.
6. **Bearing:** pods from about **3–5 years**; full yield at about 8–10 years; trees can bear for decades.

### Pests and diseases

- **Mirids (capsids):** sap-sucking bugs causing black patches and **dieback** of branches. Control: approved insecticides at the right time; good shade management.
- **Black pod** (fungus, *Phytophthora*): pods rot brown-black in wet weather. Control: harvest often, **remove and destroy infected pods**, prune to improve air flow, copper fungicides.
- **Cocoa swollen shoot** (virus, spread by mealybugs): a serious disease in parts of West Africa; infected trees must be cut out.

### Harvesting and processing

1. **Harvest** ripe pods (yellow or orange, depending on variety) every 2–4 weeks with a cutlass or a long-handled knife, without damaging the flower cushions.
2. **Break** the pods and remove the wet beans.
3. **Ferment** the beans for **5–7 days** in heaps or boxes covered with plantain or banana leaves, **turning** every 2 days.
4. **Dry** in the sun on mats or raised platforms for 1–2 weeks, turning often, until they rattle and the moisture is about 7%.
5. **Grade, bag and sell.**

:::callout{type=definition}
**Why ferment cocoa?** Fermentation kills the germ (embryo), removes the sticky pulp, and starts chemical changes that develop the **chocolate flavour and brown colour**. Unfermented or poorly fermented beans are purple and slaty, taste bitter, and fetch a **lower price**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Drying cocoa on bare ground or by the roadside.** Beans pick up dirt, smoke and smells, go mouldy if rained on, and are graded down. Use raised platforms or mats and cover them from rain.
:::

## Oil palm

### Types

![Cross-sections of oil palm fruits: Dura with a thick shell and thin pulp; Pisifera with no shell but mostly sterile; Tenera, the Dura × Pisifera hybrid, with a thin shell and thick oily pulp](https://wasscetutor.com/img/agriculture/oil-palm-types.svg)

| Type | Shell | Oil | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dura** (D) | thick | less (thin mesocarp) | common in wild groves |
| **Pisifera** (P) | none | — | female flowers usually **sterile**; used as the male parent |
| **Tenera** (D × P) | **thin** | **most** (thick mesocarp) | the hybrid planted on modern farms |

### Cultivation

1. **Nursery:** germinated seeds in a **pre-nursery** (small bags) for about 3 months, then a **main nursery** (large polybags) until about 12 months old.
2. **Planting:** in a **triangular** pattern, **9 m** apart, giving about **143 palms per hectare**; a legume cover crop protects the soil between them.
3. **Care:** ring weeding; fertilisers (especially potassium and magnesium); pruning old fronds.
4. **Harvesting:** first bunches at about **3 years** after planting; harvest a bunch when a few **loose fruits fall** from it; use a chisel on young palms and a sickle on a long pole for tall ones.

### Processing palm fruit

1. **Sterilise**: boil or steam the bunches or fruits (stops the enzyme that makes oil go sour, and loosens the fruits).
2. **Thresh**: separate the fruits from the bunch.
3. **Digest**: pound or mash the fruits to break the pulp.
4. **Press**: squeeze out the oil (by hand or in a screw press) and clarify by boiling and skimming → **crude palm oil** (red oil).
5. **Crack** the nuts left over to get **kernels** → **palm kernel oil**; the residue is **palm kernel cake** for animal feed.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Red oil in the kitchen and the market.** Palm oil is the cooking oil of most Sierra Leonean dishes, from cassava leaf to groundnut soup, and palm kernel oil is used for soap. Many women process palm fruit in small groups with hand-operated presses. Fresh, well-clarified oil fetches a better price than oil from over-ripe or stale fruit, which is high in free fatty acids and goes rancid.
:::

## Importance and problems of cash crops

**Importance:** income for farmers; **foreign exchange** from exports; employment in farming, processing and trade; raw materials for local industries (soap, oil, drinks); infrastructure such as roads in producing areas.

**Problems:**

- **Price fluctuation** on world markets.
- **Old, low-yielding trees** and slow replanting.
- **Pests and diseases** (mirids, black pod).
- **Long waiting period** before the first harvest; farmers need credit.
- Poor **roads**, storage and processing facilities; poor quality lowers prices.
- **Competition with food crops** for land and labour.
- Smuggling and weak marketing systems.

## Key terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key terms"}
- **Cash crop**: a crop grown mainly for sale, such as cocoa, coffee, oil palm and cashew.
- **Plantation**: a large estate growing one perennial cash crop, often with hired labour and modern methods.
- **Temporary shade**: fast-growing plants, such as plantain or banana, planted to shade young cocoa and give food and income while it grows.
- **Polybag**: a plastic bag of soil in which seedlings are raised in a nursery.
- **Tenera**: the hybrid oil palm with a thin shell and a thick, oil-rich fruit pulp.
- **Fermentation**: piling or boxing fresh cocoa beans for 5–7 days, which develops the chocolate flavour before drying.
:::

## Key points

- Main cash crops: **cocoa, coffee, oil palm, cashew, ginger, kola, sugarcane, rubber, coconut**.
- Cocoa: polybag nursery, **shade**, 3 × 3 m, pods from 3–5 years; **mirids** and **black pod**; **ferment 5–7 days**, then dry.
- Oil palm: **Dura × Pisifera = Tenera**; 9 m triangles (143/ha); harvest when loose fruits fall.
- Palm processing: **sterilise, thresh, digest, press, clarify**; crack nuts for kernels.
- Cash crops earn income and foreign exchange but face price swings, old trees and poor infrastructure.
