:::callout{type=definition}
**River landforms** are the features a river creates as it erodes and deposits along its course: **V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, rapids and gorges** in the upper course; **meanders** in the middle course; and **floodplains, levees, ox-bow lakes, deltas and estuaries** in the lower course.
:::

This lesson explains how each landform is formed, with diagrams and African examples, the way WASSCE asks you to describe them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the formation of V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, rapids and gorges
- explain the formation of meanders and ox-bow lakes
- explain the formation of floodplains and levees
- state the conditions for delta formation and describe the types of delta
- distinguish a delta from an estuary
- state the uses of rivers and river landforms

## Landforms of the upper course

**V-shaped valley:** strong **vertical erosion** deepens the channel, while weathering and mass movement wear back the valley sides, giving a steep, narrow V.

**Interlocking spurs:** the young river cannot cut through hard ridges, so it winds around them; the ridges (spurs) seem to lock together like the fingers of clasped hands.

**Waterfalls and gorges:**

1. A band of **hard rock** lies over **softer rock**.
2. The river erodes the soft rock more quickly, forming a step.
3. The falling water and swirling stones erode a deep **plunge pool** at the foot.
4. The soft rock beneath the hard cap is **undercut**, the overhang collapses, and the waterfall **retreats upstream**.
5. Over time this retreat leaves a steep-sided **gorge** downstream.

**Rapids** are stretches of fast, broken water where bands of hard and soft rock alternate.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**Victoria Falls** on the **Zambezi** (on the Zambia–Zimbabwe border) is one of the world's great waterfalls, about 1.7 km wide and over 100 m high. Below it the Zambezi flows through a zigzag series of **gorges**, cut as the falls retreated upstream along lines of weakness in the basalt.
:::

## Landforms of the middle course: meanders

:::callout{type=definition}
A **meander** is a large bend in a river.
:::

- On the **outside of the bend**, the water flows fastest and deepest; erosion undercuts the bank, forming a steep **river cliff**.
- On the **inside of the bend**, the water is slow and shallow; deposition builds a gentle **slip-off slope** (point bar) of sand and gravel.
- Over time, erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside make the bends larger and they migrate across the valley.

## Landforms of the lower course

![Four stages in the formation of an ox-bow lake: erosion on the outer bends, the neck narrows, a flood cuts through the neck, and deposition seals off the old bend](https://wasscetutor.com/img/geography/oxbow-formation.svg)

*Figure 1. How an ox-bow lake forms. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Ox-bow lake:**

1. Erosion on the outer bends of two meanders narrows the **neck** of land between them.
2. During a flood, the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter, straighter course.
3. Deposition seals off the ends of the old bend.
4. The abandoned bend becomes a crescent-shaped **ox-bow lake**, which may later dry into a swamp.

**Floodplain and levees:** when a river overflows in flood, it spreads out and slows down, depositing fine silt (**alluvium**) over the surrounding land. Repeated floods build a flat, fertile **floodplain**. The coarsest material is dropped first, right beside the channel, building raised banks called **levees**.

### Deltas

:::callout{type=definition}
A **delta** is a low, flat, often triangular area of deposited sediment at the mouth of a river, through which the river divides into many smaller channels called **distributaries**.
:::

**Conditions for a delta to form:**

1. The river carries a **large load** of sediment.
2. The sea or lake has **weak currents and a small tidal range**, so sediment is not carried away.
3. The coast is **sheltered** and the water near the shore is **shallow**.
4. The river's speed falls suddenly as it enters the sea.
5. Salt water helps fine clay particles clump together and sink (**flocculation**).

| Type of delta | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Arcuate** | fan-shaped, curved front | Niger Delta, Nile Delta |
| **Bird's-foot** | long distributaries like a bird's claws | Mississippi Delta |
| **Cuspate** | pointed, like a tooth | Tiber Delta (Italy) |

### Estuaries

:::callout{type=definition}
An **estuary** is the wide, funnel-shaped tidal mouth of a river, where fresh water mixes with sea water.
:::

Estuaries form where strong tides and currents carry sediment away, so no delta can build up.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The **Rokel River** enters the Atlantic through the **Sierra Leone River estuary**, one of the largest natural harbours in Africa. Its deep, sheltered water is why Freetown grew where it did, and why the **Queen Elizabeth II Quay** can handle ocean-going ships. By contrast, the **Niger** carries a huge load into a sea with weak currents and has built the vast **Niger Delta**, rich in oil and gas.
:::

## Uses of rivers and river landforms

1. **Water supply** for homes, farms and industry.
2. **Irrigation** and fertile **floodplain and delta soils** for farming (rice in inland valley swamps and riverine grasslands).
3. **Hydroelectric power** from waterfalls and dams (Bumbuna on the Rokel).
4. **Transport** by canoe and boat.
5. **Fishing.**
6. **Minerals** in alluvial deposits (diamonds, gold).
7. **Tourism** (waterfalls, gorges).

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
A **delta** forms by **deposition** where the sea is calm; an **estuary** forms where strong tides **remove** sediment. A river mouth has one or the other, depending on the balance between the load and the strength of the sea.
:::

## Key points

- **Upper course:** V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs, waterfalls (hard over soft rock, plunge pool, retreat), gorges, rapids.
- **Middle course:** meanders with **river cliffs** (outer bend, erosion) and **slip-off slopes** (inner bend, deposition).
- **Lower course:** **ox-bow lakes** (neck cut through in flood), **floodplains** and **levees**, **deltas** (large load, calm sea), **estuaries** (strong tides).
- Delta types: **arcuate** (Niger, Nile), **bird's-foot** (Mississippi), **cuspate** (Tiber).

## Quick check

1. Why does a waterfall retreat upstream?
2. Where is a river cliff found on a meander, and why?
3. How is an ox-bow lake formed?
4. State two conditions necessary for a delta to form.
5. What is the difference between a delta and an estuary?

**Answers:**

1. The soft rock beneath the hard cap is undercut, so the overhang collapses and the falls move back.
2. On the outside of the bend, where the fast, deep water erodes the bank.
3. Erosion narrows the neck of a meander; in a flood the river cuts through the neck; deposition seals off the old bend, leaving a lake.
4. Any two: a large load; weak currents and small tides; a sheltered, shallow coast; a sudden fall in speed; flocculation in salt water.
5. A delta is a deposit of sediment at a river mouth; an estuary is a wide tidal mouth where sediment is carried away.
