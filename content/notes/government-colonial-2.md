Britain ruled the vast Protectorate of Sierra Leone with only a handful of British officers. It could do so because it governed **through the chiefs**, who already had authority over their people. This method, called **indirect rule**, shaped chieftaincy and local politics in Sierra Leone for the whole colonial period and beyond. This lesson explains what indirect rule was, how it worked in Sierra Leone, and its merits, demerits and lasting effects.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define indirect rule and state the reasons for it
- describe how indirect rule worked in the Sierra Leone Protectorate
- explain the features of indirect rule
- state its merits and demerits
- explain its effects on Sierra Leone

## What is indirect rule?

:::callout{type=definition}
**Indirect rule** was the British method of governing colonies **through existing traditional rulers and institutions**, under the supervision of British officials. The best-known theorist was **Lord Lugard**, who developed it in Northern Nigeria and explained it in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (**1922**).
:::

## Reasons for indirect rule

1. **Shortage of British staff** to govern large areas directly.
2. **Lack of money**: it was cheap, since chiefs were paid little and collected taxes.
3. **Existing systems**: chiefs already had authority and their people obeyed them.
4. **Language barrier**: British officers did not speak local languages.
5. **To reduce resistance**: people were more likely to accept orders through their own rulers, especially after the Hut Tax War.
6. **Unhealthy climate** that discouraged British settlement.

## How indirect rule worked in Sierra Leone

![The chain of command: Secretary of State for the Colonies in London, Governor in Freetown, District Commissioners, Paramount Chiefs, and sub-chiefs and people](https://wasscetutor.com/img/government/indirect-rule-chain.svg)

*Figure 1. The chain of command under indirect rule. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

- The **Governor** in Freetown was responsible to the **Secretary of State for the Colonies** in London.
- The Protectorate was divided into **districts** (later grouped into **provinces**), each supervised by a **District Commissioner**.
- **Paramount Chiefs** ruled their chiefdoms, assisted by sub-chiefs and the **Tribal Authority** (chief and councillors).
- Chiefs **collected the hut tax**, kept **law and order**, ran **native courts** under customary law, recruited labour and passed on government orders.
- From **1937**, **Native Administration** reforms created **chiefdom treasuries**, so that part of the tax was spent on local services and chiefs were paid fixed salaries.
- In the 1940s, **District Councils** and a **Protectorate Assembly** gave chiefs and educated Protectorate leaders a wider forum.

## Features of indirect rule

1. Use of **traditional rulers** as agents of colonial government.
2. **Supervision by British officers** (District Commissioners), who could advise, warn or depose chiefs.
3. Retention of **customary law** and **native courts**.
4. **Native treasuries** funded by local taxes.
5. The **Governor** had the final say in recognising or removing chiefs.
6. **Separation** of the Protectorate from the Colony's system of government.

## Merits and demerits

| Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| Cheap and needed few British officials | Chiefs became **agents of the colonial power**, losing the trust of their people |
| Preserved traditional institutions and culture | Chiefs became **autocratic**, since the checks of elders and societies weakened and British backing protected them |
| Reduced resistance to colonial rule | **Educated Africans were excluded** from local government |
| Provided training in local administration | **Slowed the development** of modern, democratic institutions |
| Used local knowledge and languages | **Widened the gap** between Colony and Protectorate |
| Maintained order with little force | Abuses such as **forced labour** and **excessive levies** by some chiefs |

## Effects on Sierra Leone

- **Strengthened the power of Paramount Chiefs**, who remain important in politics today.
- **Colony–Protectorate divide** in law, education and political representation.
- **Political role of chiefs** in the nationalist period: the SLPP drew strong support from chiefs and the Protectorate elite.
- **Discontent with chiefs' abuses** led to the **1955–1956 riots** in the north against heavy taxes and levies.
- The chieftaincy system survived independence and is **guaranteed by the 1991 Constitution**.

:::callout{type=local-example}
After the **1955–1956 tax riots** in northern Sierra Leone, a commission of inquiry found that many people were protesting against **excessive levies and abuses by chiefs**, not only against government taxes. Some chiefs were removed. The riots showed how indirect rule had freed some chiefs from the traditional checks of elders and societies, leaving ordinary people with little protection.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Indirect rule did not mean the British left chiefs alone. The **District Commissioner** supervised the chiefs closely, and the **Governor** could recognise, suspend or depose them. Real power lay with the colonial government; the chiefs were its agents.
:::

## Key points

- **Indirect rule** governed through **traditional rulers** under British supervision (**Lord Lugard**, *The Dual Mandate*, 1922).
- Reasons: **few British staff, low cost, existing authority, language, less resistance**.
- In Sierra Leone: **Governor → District Commissioners → Paramount Chiefs → sub-chiefs**; **native courts** and **chiefdom treasuries (1937)**.
- Demerits: **autocratic chiefs, exclusion of the educated elite, Colony–Protectorate divide, slow democratisation**.
- Effects: **strong Paramount Chiefs**, **1955–56 riots**, chieftaincy **guaranteed by the 1991 Constitution**.
