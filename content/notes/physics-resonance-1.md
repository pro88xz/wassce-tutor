Pluck a guitar string and it vibrates at a frequency set by its length, its tension and its thickness. Tighten it, and the note rises. Shorten it with a finger on a fret, and the note rises again. This lesson explains why.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain how stationary waves form on a stretched string
- find the frequencies of the fundamental and harmonics of a string
- state the laws of vibration of strings and use $f = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$
- describe the sonometer and its use

## Stationary waves on a string

A string fixed at both ends (a guitar string, a sonometer wire) is plucked. Waves travel to each fixed end, reflect and superpose with the oncoming waves, setting up a **stationary wave**. The fixed ends cannot move, so they are always **nodes**.

![A string fixed at both ends vibrating in one loop for the fundamental and two loops for the second harmonic; nodes and antinodes are marked](https://wasscetutor.com/img/physics/stationary-waves-string.svg)

*Figure 1. The fundamental and the second harmonic of a stretched string. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

### Modes of vibration

| Mode | Pattern | Length $L$ | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental** (1st harmonic) | 1 loop | $L = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ | $f_0 = \dfrac{v}{2L}$ |
| 2nd harmonic (1st overtone) | 2 loops | $L = \lambda$ | $2f_0$ |
| 3rd harmonic (2nd overtone) | 3 loops | $L = \dfrac{3\lambda}{2}$ | $3f_0$ |

A string can vibrate at **all whole-number multiples** of its fundamental frequency, which is why a plucked string gives a rich sound.

## Speed of waves on a string

The speed of transverse waves on a string depends on the **tension** $T$ and the **mass per unit length** $\mu$ (kg m⁻¹):

$$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$$

So the fundamental frequency is

$$f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$$

### The laws of vibration of strings

For a stretched string vibrating in its fundamental mode:

1. **Length law:** $f \propto \dfrac{1}{L}$ (tension and mass per unit length constant) — shortening the string raises the pitch.
2. **Tension law:** $f \propto \sqrt{T}$ (length and mass per unit length constant) — tightening the string raises the pitch.
3. **Mass law:** $f \propto \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}$ (length and tension constant) — thicker, heavier strings give lower notes.

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A wire 0.80 m long with mass per unit length 0.002 kg m⁻¹ is stretched by a tension of 80 N. Find (a) the speed of waves on it, (b) its fundamental frequency, (c) the frequency of its third harmonic.**

(a) $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{80}{0.002}} = \sqrt{40\,000} = 200$ m s⁻¹.

(b) $f_0 = \dfrac{v}{2L} = \dfrac{200}{1.6} = 125$ Hz.

(c) $3f_0 = 375$ Hz.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A string sounds at 300 Hz. What is its frequency when the tension is made four times larger?**

$f \propto \sqrt{T}$, so the frequency is multiplied by $\sqrt{4} = 2$: **600 Hz**.
:::

## The sonometer

A **sonometer** is a hollow wooden box with a wire stretched over two movable bridges. The tension is set by hanging masses from one end of the wire (over a pulley). The box amplifies the sound.

**Using it to verify the length law:**

1. Keep the tension constant. Strike a tuning fork of known frequency and hold its stem on the box.
2. Place a small paper rider on the wire midway between the bridges. Move a bridge until the paper rider is thrown off: the wire is **resonating** with the fork (same frequency).
3. Measure the vibrating length $L$ between the bridges.
4. Repeat with forks of different frequencies $f$.
5. Plot $f$ against $\dfrac{1}{L}$: a straight line through the origin verifies $f \propto \dfrac{1}{L}$.

:::callout{type=local-example}
Players of the kondi (thumb piano) and the guitar in Sierra Leonean music tune by exactly these laws: tightening a string or shortening a metal key raises its note.
:::

## Key points

- A string fixed at both ends has nodes at the ends; fundamental $L = \lambda/2$, $f_0 = v/2L$.
- Harmonics are whole-number multiples of $f_0$.
- $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$; $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}$.
- Laws: $f \propto 1/L$, $f \propto \sqrt{T}$, $f \propto 1/\sqrt{\mu}$.

## Quick check

1. What is the wavelength of the fundamental of a string 0.5 m long?
2. If the length of a vibrating string is halved (same tension), what happens to its frequency?
3. Which harmonic has three loops?

**Answers:** 1. $\lambda = 2L = 1.0$ m. 2. It doubles. 3. The third harmonic.
