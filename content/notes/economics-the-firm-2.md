## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the scale of production and how firms grow
- distinguish internal from external economies of scale and give examples
- explain diseconomies of scale and why small firms survive

## Scale of production

:::callout{type=definition}
The **scale of production** is the **size of a firm's operations**: how much it produces and how many factors of production it uses. **Large-scale production** means producing on a big scale with many workers and much capital.
:::

### How firms grow

1. **Internal growth**: the firm ploughs back profit to buy more machines and open more branches.
2. **Mergers and amalgamation**: two or more firms join to form one.
3. **Takeovers**: one firm buys control of another.

| Type of integration | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | Firms at the **same stage** of production join | Two bakeries merge |
| **Vertical backward** | A firm joins with its **supplier** | A bakery buys a flour mill |
| **Vertical forward** | A firm joins with its **buyer or outlet** | A flour mill opens its own bakeries |
| **Lateral (conglomerate)** | Firms making **unrelated** products join | A drinks firm buys a transport company |

## Economies of scale

:::callout{type=definition}
**Economies of scale** are the **advantages, in the form of lower average cost**, that a firm gains as it increases its scale of production.
:::

### Internal economies of scale

These arise **inside the firm** as **it** grows.

| Internal economy | How it lowers average cost |
|---|---|
| **Technical** | Large firms can afford big, efficient machines and use them fully |
| **Managerial** | Specialist managers (finance, marketing, production) can be employed |
| **Marketing (commercial)** | Bulk buying earns discounts; advertising costs are spread over more units |
| **Financial** | Banks lend more easily and more cheaply to large firms |
| **Risk-bearing** | Large firms can produce many products and sell in many markets |
| **Research and welfare** | Large firms can pay for research and for staff housing, canteens and training |

### External economies of scale

These arise **outside the firm**, when the **whole industry** grows or concentrates in one area. **Every firm** in the industry benefits.

| External economy | Example |
|---|---|
| **Skilled labour** | Trained workers gather where the industry is located |
| **Infrastructure** | Better roads, power and water are provided to the area |
| **Ancillary firms** | Suppliers and repair firms set up nearby |
| **Information** | Trade associations and journals share research and market news |
| **Specialised institutions** | Banks, insurance firms and training colleges serving the industry |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Where many mining companies operate in one district, firms that supply equipment, repair vehicles, transport ore and train mechanics set up nearby. Each mining firm benefits from these services without paying to create them: an **external** economy of scale.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Internal economies come from the growth of **the firm itself**. External economies come from the growth of **the industry** or the area. If the advantage would disappear when the industry left the area, it is external.
:::

## Diseconomies of scale

:::callout{type=definition}
**Diseconomies of scale** are the **disadvantages** that cause **average cost to rise** when a firm grows too large.
:::

**Internal diseconomies:**

1. **Management problems**: too many levels of management, slow decisions.
2. **Poor communication** between departments and with workers.
3. **Low worker morale**: workers feel like "small cogs", leading to strikes and absenteeism.
4. **Wastage** and difficult supervision.

**External diseconomies** arise when an industry becomes too concentrated: traffic congestion, pollution, rising land rents and shortages of labour and raw materials.

## Why small firms survive

Despite the advantages of large scale, small firms remain common in West Africa:

1. **Small markets**: demand for some goods (tailoring, hairdressing) is too small for large firms.
2. **Personal services**: customers want individual attention.
3. **Limited capital** and difficulty borrowing.
4. **Perishable goods** must be sold quickly and locally.
5. **Desire for independence**: many people prefer to be their own boss.
6. **Government support** for small businesses.
7. **Flexibility**: small firms adapt quickly to changes in fashion and demand.
8. **Subcontracting**: large firms buy parts and services from small firms.

## Sources of finance for firms

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| Personal savings | The main source for sole traders |
| Ploughed-back profits | Retained earnings used for growth |
| Bank loans and overdrafts | Must be repaid with interest |
| Sale of shares | For limited companies |
| Debentures | Long-term loans to a company at a fixed interest rate |
| Trade credit | Suppliers allow payment later |
| Microfinance and cooperatives | Small loans for small businesses |

## Key points

- Firms grow by **internal growth**, **mergers** and **takeovers** (horizontal, vertical, lateral integration).
- **Internal economies**: technical, managerial, marketing, financial, risk-bearing.
- **External economies**: skilled labour, infrastructure, ancillary firms, information.
- **Diseconomies** raise average cost when a firm or industry grows too large.
- Small firms survive through **small markets**, **personal service**, **flexibility** and **independence**.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Very common: **"Distinguish between internal and external economies of scale and give two examples of each"**, and **"Give five reasons why small firms survive"**. Start with clear definitions, then give each example a heading and a sentence explaining how it lowers cost.
:::

## Quick check

1. A bakery buys a flour mill. What type of integration is this?
2. Is bulk buying an internal or an external economy of scale?
3. Give two causes of internal diseconomies of scale.

**Answers:** 1. Vertical backward integration. 2. Internal (a marketing economy). 3. For example: management and communication problems, and low worker morale.
