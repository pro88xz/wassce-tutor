The diamonds dug from the gravels of Kono and the charcoal burned in every coal pot are the same element: carbon. Its atoms can join in different ways, giving the hardest natural substance and a soft, black, slippery one. This lesson covers the allotropes of carbon and its two oxides, carbon(IV) oxide and the poisonous carbon(II) oxide.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the structures and properties of diamond and graphite and relate them to their uses
- describe the amorphous forms of carbon and their uses
- state the chemical properties of carbon
- describe the laboratory preparation, test, properties and uses of carbon(IV) oxide
- describe the preparation, properties and dangers of carbon(II) oxide
- compare carbon(IV) oxide and carbon(II) oxide

## Allotropes of carbon

![Diamond: each carbon bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional network. Graphite: flat layers of hexagons held by weak forces, with delocalised electrons](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/carbon-allotropes.svg)

*Figure 1. The structures of diamond and graphite. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

| Property | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Each C bonded to **4** others; **tetrahedral 3-D giant covalent** network | Each C bonded to **3** others in **flat hexagonal layers**; layers held by **weak** forces |
| **Hardness** | **Hardest** natural substance | **Soft and slippery**: layers slide |
| **Appearance** | Transparent, sparkling crystals | Black, shiny, greasy |
| **Electrical conductivity** | **Does not conduct**: all electrons in bonds | **Conducts**: one **delocalised electron** per atom moves along the layers |
| **Density** | 3.5 g/cm³ | 2.3 g/cm³ |
| **Melting point** | Very high (about 3550 °C) | Very high |
| **Uses** | Jewellery; **cutting, drilling and grinding** tools | **Pencil "lead"** (with clay); **lubricant**; **electrodes** in electrolysis and dry cells; moderator in nuclear reactors |

Both are **giant covalent structures**, so both have very high melting points and are insoluble in water. **Burning** either in excess oxygen gives only carbon(IV) oxide, which proves both are pure carbon.

**Newer allotropes:** **fullerenes** (such as C₆₀, "buckyballs") and **graphene** (a single graphite layer, very strong and highly conducting).

### Amorphous carbon

Forms of carbon with no regular crystal structure (actually tiny graphite crystals):

| Form | Made by | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Charcoal** (wood charcoal) | Heating wood with little air | Cooking fuel; **adsorbs** gases and colours |
| **Activated charcoal** | Charcoal treated to give a huge surface area | Water filters; gas masks; removing colour from sugar; treating poisoning |
| **Coke** | Heating coal without air | Fuel and **reducing agent** in the blast furnace |
| **Soot (lampblack)** | Incomplete burning of oils | Printing ink, shoe polish, car tyres |
| **Animal charcoal** | Heating bones | Decolourising raw sugar |

## Chemical properties of carbon

**1. Burns in oxygen:** in plenty of oxygen it forms carbon(IV) oxide; in limited oxygen, carbon(II) oxide.

$$\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 \qquad 2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}$$

**2. Reducing agent:** carbon removes oxygen from the oxides of metals **below it** in the reactivity series (zinc, iron, lead, copper). This is the basis of metal extraction:

$$2\text{CuO} + \text{C} \rightarrow 2\text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2 \qquad 2\text{ZnO} + \text{C} \rightarrow 2\text{Zn} + \text{CO}_2$$

**3. With steam** at white heat, it forms water gas: C + H₂O → CO + H₂.

**4. Oxidised by hot concentrated acids** (nitric and sulphuric) to CO₂.

## Carbon(IV) oxide, CO₂

![Dilute hydrochloric acid on marble chips in a flask with a thistle funnel; carbon(IV) oxide collected by downward delivery](https://wasscetutor.com/img/chemistry/co2-prep.svg)

*Figure 2. Preparing carbon(IV) oxide. WASSCE Tutor diagram.*

**Laboratory preparation:** dilute **hydrochloric acid** on **marble chips** (calcium carbonate):

$$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$

Dilute **sulphuric** acid is **not** used: it forms insoluble **calcium sulphate**, which coats the marble and stops the reaction.

**Collection:** by **downward delivery** (denser than air), or over water (it is only fairly soluble).

**Test:** it turns **lime water** (calcium hydroxide solution) **milky**, because insoluble calcium carbonate forms. With **excess** CO₂, the milkiness **clears**, because soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate forms:

$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

$$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{(aq)}$$

**Properties:**

- colourless, odourless gas, about **1.5 times denser** than air
- fairly soluble in water, forming weak **carbonic acid** (H₂CO₃), which turns blue litmus faintly red
- **acidic oxide**: reacts with alkalis, CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O
- does **not burn** and does **not support** combustion of most substances. But **burning magnesium continues to burn** in it, leaving black specks of carbon: 2Mg + CO₂ → 2MgO + C
- **solid CO₂** ("dry ice") **sublimes** at −78 °C

**Uses:**

- **fire extinguishers** (dense, does not support burning)
- **fizzy drinks** (dissolved under pressure)
- **dry ice** for refrigeration and stage "smoke"
- **baking**: CO₂ from baking powder or yeast makes bread and cakes rise
- **photosynthesis**: the source of carbon in all plant material

## Carbon(II) oxide, CO

**Preparation:** pass carbon(IV) oxide over **red-hot coke** (CO₂ + C → 2CO), or warm methanoic acid with concentrated sulphuric acid (which removes water).

**Properties:**

- colourless, **odourless**, **very poisonous** gas; about the same density as air
- almost insoluble in water; **neutral** oxide
- **burns** with a **blue flame**: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂
- **reducing agent**: reduces metal oxides, as in the blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

**Why it is so dangerous:** CO combines with **haemoglobin** in red blood cells about 200 times more strongly than oxygen does, forming carboxyhaemoglobin. Blood can then no longer carry oxygen. Because CO has **no smell**, victims do not notice, become drowsy and may die. It is produced by **incomplete combustion** in charcoal stoves, generators and car engines in enclosed spaces.

### CO₂ and CO compared

| Property | Carbon(IV) oxide, CO₂ | Carbon(II) oxide, CO |
|---|---|---|
| Smell | Odourless | Odourless |
| Toxicity | Not poisonous (suffocates only at high levels) | **Very poisonous** |
| Density compared with air | Denser | About the same |
| Solubility in water | Fairly soluble | Almost insoluble |
| Nature | **Acidic** oxide | **Neutral** oxide |
| Burning | Does not burn | **Burns** with a blue flame |
| Lime water | Turns milky | No effect |
| Reducing agent? | No | **Yes** |

:::callout{type=local-example}
Sierra Leone's **diamonds**, mined in Kono and along rivers in the east, formed from carbon under enormous pressure deep in the earth and were brought up in volcanic rock. The **charcoal** sold at roadsides is made by burning wood with limited air in earth-covered kilns. It burns hotter and with less smoke than wood, but its large-scale production cuts down forests.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Graphite conducts electricity because of its **delocalised electrons**, not because it is a metal. Diamond, with every electron held in a bond, does not conduct.
:::

## Key points

- **Diamond:** each C bonded to 4, 3-D network: **hardest**, non-conductor; used for jewellery and cutting.
- **Graphite:** layers of hexagons with weak forces and **delocalised electrons**: soft, slippery, **conducts**; used for pencils, lubricants and electrodes.
- Amorphous carbon: **charcoal, activated charcoal, coke, soot**.
- Carbon is a **reducing agent**, used to extract zinc, iron, lead and copper.
- **CO₂:** CaCO₃ + 2HCl; turns **lime water milky**; dense, acidic, does not support burning; extinguishers, drinks, baking.
- **CO:** **poisonous**, odourless, burns with a blue flame, a reducing agent; forms by incomplete combustion.

## Quick check

1. Why does graphite conduct electricity but diamond does not?
2. Why is dilute sulphuric acid not used with marble chips to prepare CO₂?
3. What happens when excess CO₂ is bubbled through lime water?
4. Why is carbon(II) oxide poisonous?

**Answers:**

1. Graphite has one delocalised electron per atom, free to move along its layers; in diamond all electrons are held in bonds.
2. It forms insoluble calcium sulphate, which coats the chips and stops the reaction.
3. It first turns milky (CaCO₃), then clears as soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate forms.
4. It combines with haemoglobin and stops the blood carrying oxygen.
