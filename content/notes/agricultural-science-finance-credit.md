Every farm needs money **before** it earns any: to buy seed and fertiliser at planting, pay labourers at weeding, buy day-old chicks and feed, or build a pig pen. The harvest comes months later. **Agricultural finance** is about where farmers get this money, how they use it, and how they repay it. Used well, credit lets a farmer buy the inputs that raise yields; used badly, it becomes a debt that takes the harvest. This lesson covers the sources and types of agricultural credit, how interest works, why farmers find credit hard to get, and how to manage credit wisely.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- define agricultural finance and credit and explain their importance
- describe the sources of agricultural credit, formal and informal
- classify credit by time and purpose
- calculate simple interest on a farm loan
- explain the problems farmers face in obtaining credit and suggest solutions
- describe good credit management

## Finance and credit

:::callout{type=definition}
- **Agricultural finance** is the provision and management of money (capital) for agricultural production, processing and marketing.
- **Credit** is money (or goods) obtained now on a promise to **repay later**, usually with **interest**.
- **Collateral (security)** is property pledged to the lender, which can be taken if the loan is not repaid.
:::

**Importance of credit:** buys inputs (improved seed, fertiliser, feed, chemicals); buys equipment and animals; pays labour at peak periods; allows expansion and new enterprises; lets farmers **store** produce and sell later at better prices instead of selling at harvest; supports processing and marketing.

## Sources of credit

### Informal sources

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| **personal savings** | the cheapest source: no interest or collateral |
| **family and friends** | quick and flexible; often little or no interest; amounts are small |
| **osusu** (rotating savings group) | members contribute a fixed amount regularly, and each in turn receives the whole pot; widely used in Sierra Leone |
| **village savings and loan associations** | members save weekly and lend to each other at interest set by the group |
| **moneylenders** and **traders** | quick, no paperwork, but **high interest**; traders may lend against the coming harvest at a low price |

### Formal sources

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| **commercial banks** | larger loans; need collateral, records and paperwork; few rural branches |
| **community banks** and **financial services associations** | rural, community-owned financial institutions offering savings and small loans |
| **microfinance institutions** | small loans to individuals and groups, often without collateral, using **group guarantee** |
| **cooperatives and credit unions** | members borrow from pooled savings at fair interest |
| **government schemes** and **NGO projects** | input loans, subsidised credit, credit in kind (seed, fertiliser) |
| **mobile money** services | savings, transfers and some small loans by phone |

## Types of credit

**By time:**

| Type | Period | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **short-term** | under 1 year | seasonal inputs: seed, fertiliser, feed, labour |
| **medium-term** | 1–5 years | animals, small machines (power tiller, sprayer), poultry houses |
| **long-term** | over 5 years | land development, swamp development, buildings, **tree crops** (cocoa, oil palm), large machines |

**By form:** **cash credit** (money) or **credit in kind** (inputs given now, repaid in cash or produce at harvest).

## Interest

Interest is the price of borrowing money. For a simple farm loan:

:::callout{type=definition}
$$\text{simple interest} = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$$

$P$ = principal (amount borrowed), $R$ = rate per year (%), $T$ = time in years.
:::

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**A farmer borrows NLe 6000 at 18% simple interest per year to buy fertiliser and seed. She repays after 9 months. How much does she repay?**

$T = \dfrac{9}{12} = 0.75$ years.

$$\text{interest} = \frac{6000 \times 18 \times 0.75}{100} = \text{NLe } 810$$

Total repayment $= 6000 + 810 = $ **NLe 6810**.

If the extra fertiliser and seed raise her harvest by 15 bags of rice worth NLe 500 each (NLe 7500), the loan **pays for itself** with NLe 690 to spare. If the rains fail, she still owes NLe 6810, which is why credit carries **risk**.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Using a production loan for a wedding or a phone.** Money borrowed for seed and fertiliser must be used for seed and fertiliser. If it is spent on something that earns no income, there is no extra harvest to repay it, and the farmer ends up worse off than before.
:::

## Problems of obtaining credit

- **Lack of collateral**: many farmers have no land title or other security.
- **High interest rates** and short repayment periods.
- **Small, scattered farms** and high administrative costs for lenders.
- **Risk** of crop failure from weather, pests and diseases.
- **Lack of records** and business plans; **illiteracy**.
- **Few banks** in rural areas; long distances and delays.
- **Poor repayment history** in some areas, making lenders cautious.

**Solutions:** **group lending** (members guarantee each other); cooperatives and credit unions; **village savings groups**; government guarantee schemes; crop and livestock **insurance**; training in record keeping; credit in kind tied to inputs; repayment timed to the harvest.

## Good credit management

1. Borrow only for a **productive** purpose with a clear expected return.
2. Work out whether the extra income will **cover** the loan and interest.
3. Use the loan **for its intended purpose**.
4. Keep **records** of the loan and how it was used.
5. **Repay on time**, building a good credit history for future loans.
6. Avoid borrowing from several lenders at once.

:::callout{type=local-example}
**Osusu for the planting season.** In many communities, women's groups run **osusu**: each member pays, for example, NLe 100 every week, and each week one member takes the whole pot. Members arrange the order so that farmers receive their turn just **before planting**, when they need cash for seed and labour. It costs no interest and needs no bank, but it depends entirely on **trust** and regular contributions.
:::

**Credit** is money or goods obtained now on a promise to repay later, usually with interest. **Collateral** is property pledged to the lender as security for the loan.
:::

Informal: personal savings, family and friends, osusu, village savings groups, moneylenders, traders (any three). Formal: commercial banks, community banks, microfinance institutions, cooperatives and credit unions, government schemes (any three).

Any four: no collateral; high interest; small scattered farms; risk of crop failure; no records; illiteracy; few rural banks; poor repayment history.
:::

## Key points

- **Credit** buys inputs, equipment and time; it must be repaid with **interest**.
- Sources: **informal** (savings, family, osusu, savings groups, moneylenders, traders) and **formal** (banks, community banks, microfinance, cooperatives, government, NGOs).
- Types: **short-term** (<1 year), **medium-term** (1–5 years), **long-term** (>5 years); cash or in kind.
- Simple interest $= \dfrac{PRT}{100}$.
- Problems: **collateral, interest, risk, records, distance**; solutions include **group lending, cooperatives, savings groups, insurance**.
- Manage credit: productive use, intended purpose, records, **repay on time**.
