Before a business opens its doors, the government is already involved: it registers the business, issues a tax number and a trade licence, and sets rules on safety and wages. It also helps businesses: it builds roads and ports, gives tax incentives to investors and promotes exports. This lesson explains why and how government regulates and supports business, and how its policies affect traders.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why government intervenes in business
- describe how government regulates business: registration, licensing, laws and taxation
- describe how government supports and promotes business
- explain the effects of government policies on business
- calculate the effect of a sales tax on prices

## Why government intervenes in business

1. **Protect consumers** from exploitation and unsafe goods.
2. **Protect workers**: wages, safety and working conditions.
3. **Raise revenue** through taxes to pay for public services.
4. **Prevent monopolies** and encourage fair competition.
5. **Promote economic growth** and **create jobs**.
6. **Protect local industries** from unfair foreign competition.
7. **Protect the environment** from pollution and over-exploitation.
8. **Provide essential services** that private firms may not provide profitably.
9. **Control inflation** and stabilise the economy.
10. **Ensure national security** and **fair distribution** of income and opportunities.

## How government regulates business

### Registration and licensing

- **Registration**: businesses must register. Companies register with the **Corporate Affairs Commission**, and sole traders and partnerships register a business name.
- **Tax registration**: businesses obtain a **Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN)** from the **National Revenue Authority (NRA)**.
- **Licences**: trade licences from **local councils**, and special licences for pharmacies, mining, banking, insurance, transport and the sale of alcohol.
- **Permits**: import and export licences, and environmental permits.

### Laws and regulations

| Area | Examples of rules |
|---|---|
| **Company law** | Formation, accounts, audits and the duties of directors |
| **Labour law** | Minimum wage, working hours, safety, social security (NASSIT) contributions |
| **Consumer protection** | Weights and measures, food and drugs, standards |
| **Environmental law** | Pollution, waste disposal, mining rehabilitation |
| **Competition law** | Monopolies and price-fixing |
| **Health and safety** | Fire safety and hygiene in food businesses |
| **Zoning and planning** | Where businesses may operate |

### Taxation

| Tax | On |
|---|---|
| **Income tax / corporation tax** | Profits of businesses and incomes of individuals |
| **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** | The value of most goods and services sold (15% in Sierra Leone) |
| **Customs (import and export) duties** | Goods entering or leaving the country |
| **Excise duty** | Locally made goods such as drinks and tobacco |
| **Payroll and property taxes, licence fees** | Employees, buildings and trading licences |

:::callout{type=worked-example}
**How GST affects the price.** A shop sells a pair of shoes for NLe 200 before tax. GST is 15%.

1. GST $= 15\% \times 200 = $ NLe 30
2. Price to the consumer $= 200 + 30 = $ **NLe 230**

If GST were raised to 20%, the price would become $200 + 40 = $ NLe 240. Higher sales taxes raise prices and may **reduce sales**. The shop collects the GST and **pays it to the NRA**, acting as an unpaid tax collector.
:::

### Economic policy tools

- **Monetary policy** (by the **Bank of Sierra Leone**): interest rates and credit. Higher rates make loans dearer and slow business borrowing.
- **Fiscal policy**: government **spending and taxation**. Spending on roads raises business activity; higher taxes reduce it.
- **Price control**: maximum prices for essential goods.
- **Trade policy**: tariffs, quotas and import bans, as well as trade agreements.

## How government supports and promotes business

1. **Infrastructure**: roads, ports, airports, electricity, water and telecommunications.
2. **Finance**: credit schemes, development banks, loan guarantees and grants for small businesses.
3. **Tax incentives**: tax holidays, duty-free import of machinery, investment allowances.
4. **Investment and export promotion**: the **Sierra Leone Investment and Export Promotion Agency (SLIEPA)** helps investors and finds markets for exporters.
5. **Protection** of infant industries with tariffs and quotas.
6. **Training and education**: technical and vocational institutions.
7. **Research** and **information** on markets and technology.
8. **Trade fairs** and trade missions abroad.
9. **Government purchases**: buying from local firms (local content).
10. **Trade agreements**: **ECOWAS** and the **African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)** open wider markets.
11. **Peace, security and the rule of law**: the basic conditions for business.

## Effects of government policies on business

| Policy | Possible effect on business |
|---|---|
| Higher company tax | Lower profits; less investment |
| Lower interest rates | Cheaper loans; more investment and spending |
| Tariffs on imports | Protects local producers; dearer imported inputs |
| Subsidies | Lower costs; lower prices; more output |
| Price control | Lower prices for consumers, but may cause shortages and black markets |
| Better roads and power | Lower costs; wider markets |
| Strict regulation | Safer products, but more costs and paperwork |
| Political instability | Loss of confidence; investors leave |

:::callout{type=local-example}
A small manufacturer in Freetown deals with government at every step:

- registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission
- a TIN from the NRA
- a licence from Freetown City Council
- product approval from the Standards Bureau
- GST collected on sales
- NASSIT contributions for workers

The same government may also give it duty-free import of machinery under investment incentives, and help it reach export markets through SLIEPA.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Government involvement is not only **control**. Questions often ask for both **regulation** (laws, licences, taxes) and **support** (infrastructure, finance, incentives, promotion). Answer both sides when the question says "role of government".
:::

## Key points

- Government intervenes to **protect consumers and workers, raise revenue, prevent monopoly, promote growth and protect the environment**.
- It **regulates** through **registration, licensing, laws, taxation** and monetary, fiscal, price and trade policy.
- It **supports** business with **infrastructure, finance, tax incentives, promotion (SLIEPA), protection, training and trade agreements**.
- Each policy has **effects** on costs, prices, profits and investment.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Expect to **explain the reasons for government intervention in business**, to **state ways government regulates and assists business**, and to **explain how a given policy** (tax, interest rate, tariff, subsidy, price control) **affects business**. Link each policy to a clear effect.
:::

## Quick check

1. Give three reasons why government intervenes in business.
2. State three ways the government assists business.
3. A radio costs NLe 400 before GST at 15%. What does the consumer pay?
4. How would a rise in interest rates affect businesses?

**Answers:** 1. Any three: protect consumers, protect workers, raise revenue, prevent monopolies, promote growth and jobs, protect local industry, protect the environment, provide essential services, control inflation. 2. Any three: infrastructure, finance and credit, tax incentives, investment and export promotion, protection, training, research and information, trade fairs, government purchases, trade agreements. 3. $400 + 15\% \times 400 = 400 + 60 = $ NLe 460. 4. Loans become dearer, so businesses borrow and invest less, and consumer spending may fall.
