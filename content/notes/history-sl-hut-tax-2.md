The **effects of the Hut Tax War of 1898** shaped colonial Sierra Leone for decades. The British government sent **Sir David Chalmers** to investigate, and his **Chalmers Report (1899)** blamed the **hut tax** and the **conduct of the Frontier Police**, and recommended ending the tax. But the Colonial Secretary **Joseph Chamberlain** supported **Governor Cardew**, and the tax was **kept**. The war ended armed resistance, **completed British control** of the Protectorate, turned chiefs into **agents of colonial rule**, and left deep **mistrust between the Colony and the Protectorate**. This lesson explains **the Chalmers Commission and the results of the Hut Tax War**.

## The Chalmers Commission

In **1898** the British government appointed **Sir David Chalmers**, a senior judge, as a **Royal Commissioner** to investigate the causes of the rising.

**Findings of the Chalmers Report (1899):**

1. The main cause was the **hut tax**, which the people saw as a threat to their **ownership of land** and their **independence**.
2. The tax had been introduced **too quickly**, without proper explanation.
3. The **Frontier Police** had acted **brutally**, humiliating chiefs and abusing people.
4. The chiefs had been treated with **disrespect**, and their powers reduced too sharply.
5. He recommended that the **hut tax be abolished** and the administration be made more sensitive to local customs.

**The government's response:** **Governor Cardew** rejected the findings, arguing that the rising was caused by chiefs' resentment of the ban on slave dealing and the loss of their powers, not by the tax. The Colonial Secretary, **Joseph Chamberlain**, **supported Cardew**, and the **hut tax was retained**. Some reforms followed, especially in the **Frontier Police**.

## Effects of the Hut Tax War

### Political effects

1. **End of armed resistance:** British rule over the Protectorate was **firmly established**; there was no further large rising.
2. **Punishment of leaders:** **Bai Bureh** was **exiled to the Gold Coast** (he returned in **1905** and died in **1908**); other chiefs were deposed, imprisoned or exiled, and many people were executed for murders committed in the Mende rising.
3. **Chiefs as agents:** chiefs who survived became **agents of the colonial government**, collecting tax and keeping order under the District Commissioners; **paramount chiefs** gained power over their people but lost independence.
4. **Military reorganisation:** the **West African Regiment** was raised in **1898**, and the **Frontier Police**, whose abuses had helped cause the war, was reformed and later reorganised.
5. **Hut tax continued**, becoming an accepted part of colonial rule.

### Social effects

1. **Loss of life:** hundreds killed in the fighting and in the Mende rising, including missionaries and Krio.
2. **Destruction** of towns, farms and property by both sides; **famine** in some areas.
3. **Colony–Protectorate mistrust:** the killing of **Krio traders** in the south left lasting **bitterness between the Krio and the peoples of the Protectorate**, which affected politics up to independence.
4. **Missionary work** was set back for a time, though missions later returned and expanded.

### Economic effects

1. Trade was **disrupted** during the war, and Krio traders lost property and confidence in the Protectorate.
2. After the war, peace and order allowed the **railway (begun in 1896)** to be extended and trade in **palm kernels** to grow.
3. **European and Lebanese** traders increasingly replaced the Krio in Protectorate trade.

## Importance of the Hut Tax War

1. It was the **last major armed resistance** to British rule in Sierra Leone.
2. It showed the **people's determination** to defend their land and independence.
3. It gave Sierra Leone a **national hero**, Bai Bureh.
4. It exposed the **injustices** of early colonial administration, as recognised by the Chalmers Report.
5. It shaped the **relationship between chiefs and the colonial state**, and the division between the **Colony and the Protectorate**.

:::callout{type=local-example}
The Colony–Protectorate divide that grew after 1898 remained a major issue until independence. When the **Stevenson Constitution (1951)** gave the Protectorate majority representation, many Krio leaders, such as **Dr Bankole-Bright**, opposed it, while Protectorate leaders such as **Dr Milton Margai** led the new **Sierra Leone People's Party (SLPP)**. The roots of that conflict lie partly in the events of 1898.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
The Chalmers Report did **not** lead to the end of the hut tax. Chalmers **recommended** abolishing it, but the Colonial Office **rejected** his advice and supported Governor Cardew; the tax was **kept**.
:::

## Key figures and terms

:::callout{type=definition title="Key figures and terms"}
- **Sir David Chalmers**: the commissioner whose 1899 report blamed the war on the hut tax and the Frontier Police and urged that the tax be abolished.
- **West African Regiment**: the army regiment raised by the British in 1898, during the Hut Tax War.
:::

## Key points

- **Sir David Chalmers** investigated; his **1899 report** blamed the **hut tax** and the **Frontier Police**, and urged abolition of the tax.
- **Cardew** and **Chamberlain** rejected this; the **tax was kept**, though the police were reformed.
- Political effects: **British control completed**, **Bai Bureh exiled (to 1905)**, chiefs became **colonial agents**, **West African Regiment (1898)**.
- Social and economic effects: **loss of life, destruction, Colony–Protectorate mistrust**, decline of Krio trade in the Protectorate.
- Importance: **last major armed resistance**, a **national hero**, and lasting effects on Sierra Leone politics.
