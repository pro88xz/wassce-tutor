A descriptive essay paints a picture in words: a person, a place, an event, a scene. The examiner should finish your essay feeling they have **met** that person or **stood** in that place. Descriptive questions look easy (*"Describe your favourite teacher"*, *"Describe a busy market in your town"*), but weak answers become flat lists of facts. This lesson shows you how to organise a description, choose details that matter, and write with the senses, using a complete model essay.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- organise a description of a place, a person or an event
- choose a **dominant impression** and select details that support it
- use the five senses, precise words and figurative language
- avoid the flat, list-like descriptions that score poorly

## The dominant impression

:::callout{type=definition}
The **dominant impression** is the main feeling or idea you want the reader to take away: *a market that never sleeps*, *a teacher who was strict but fair*, *a village that time has forgotten*. Every detail you choose should support it.
:::

Decide your dominant impression **before** you write. It gives the essay a purpose and stops it becoming a list.

| Topic | A flat approach | A dominant impression |
|---|---|---|
| Describe a market | lists the things on sale | *a market that is the beating heart of the town* |
| Describe your grandmother | her height, her dress, her age | *a small woman with the strength of a whole family* |
| Describe your school | the buildings, one by one | *a school that is old and tired, but full of hope* |

## Organising a description

### A place

Choose a **route** through it and keep to it: from outside to inside, from morning to night, from the gate to the far end, from a distance to close up.

### A person

Move from **outside to inside**:

1. **Appearance**: a few telling details, not a police report.
2. **Habits and actions**: what they do, and how.
3. **Speech**: a phrase they always say.
4. **Character**: what kind of person they are, **shown** by the details above.
5. **Your feelings** about them.

### An event

Follow **time order**: the preparation, the start, the high point, the end, and the feeling afterwards.

## Writing with the senses

| Sense | Flat | Vivid |
|---|---|---|
| Sight | *There were many colours.* | *Bolts of gara cloth, indigo and gold, hung from every stall like flags.* |
| Sound | *It was noisy.* | *Above the hum of a thousand voices rose the shriek of a whistle and the bleat of a frightened goat.* |
| Smell | *It smelt of food.* | *The sharp smoke of grilled fish mixed with the sweetness of ripe mangoes.* |
| Touch | *It was hot.* | *The sun pressed on my neck like a heavy hand.* |
| Taste | *The food was nice.* | *The pepper soup burned my tongue and made my eyes water, and I wanted more.* |

:::callout{type=tip}
You don't need all five senses in every paragraph. Two or three well-chosen details beat ten ordinary ones.
:::

### Precise words beat general ones

| General | Precise |
|---|---|
| walked | strolled, marched, shuffled, limped, hurried |
| said | whispered, shouted, muttered, announced, pleaded |
| big | huge, towering, sprawling, massive |
| old | ancient, worn, faded, crumbling, battered |

### Figurative language (in moderation)

Similes, metaphors and personification can make a picture sharper: *the lorry groaned up the hill*; *her laugh was a sudden burst of sunshine*. Use a few, well. Too many make writing feel forced.

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
**Describing everything and nothing.** *"The market is very big. There are many people. They sell many things like rice, oil, clothes, shoes, phones, pots, fish and pepper. It is very busy and noisy."* Every sentence is a general statement. Choose a **few** details and make each one **vivid**.
:::

## A complete model description

**Question:** *Describe a busy market in your town.*

::::model{title="Bo Main Market on a Saturday" label="Model descriptive essay"}
**BO MAIN MARKET ON A SATURDAY**

If you want to feel the heartbeat of Bo, do not go to the government offices or the big hotels. Go to the Main Market on a Saturday morning, when the whole town seems to pour through its gates at once.

The noise reaches you long before the gates come into view. It is the sound of a thousand conversations happening together: traders calling their prices in Mende and Krio, customers arguing them down, a radio playing Afropop from a stall that sells radios, and somewhere, always, a goat bleating as if it knows exactly where it is going. Okada riders weave through the crowd at the entrance, horns beeping, while porters with wooden carts shout "Way! Way!" to clear a path.

Inside, the market is divided into kingdoms. To the left is the kingdom of cloth, where bolts of gara, dyed indigo, orange and gold, hang from every stall like the flags of different nations. The cloth sellers are mostly women, and they sit as calmly as queens, unfolding a length of fabric with a single, practised flick of the wrist. Further in lies the kingdom of food: mountains of rice in open sacks, pyramids of tomatoes, onions and hot peppers so red they seem to glow. The smell here is a mixture of smoked fish, palm oil and ripe bananas, so thick you could almost taste it.

The heart of the market, though, is its people. There is Ya Fatu, who has sold groundnuts from the same corner for thirty years and remembers the name of every child who has ever bought from her. There is the young tailor who sits at a battered sewing machine under a blue umbrella, his foot pumping the pedal as fast as an athlete's, turning a customer's chosen cloth into a dress while she waits. There are schoolchildren carrying trays of puff-puff on their heads, students on Saturday duty who will be in class again on Monday.

By midday, the sun beats down on the zinc roofs and the market slows. Traders lean back in the shade, fanning themselves with cardboard. Some eat a plate of rice and cassava leaf brought by a cook who goes from stall to stall. The arguments over prices become gentler, and a few traders even doze, one eye half open in case a customer appears.

As the afternoon fades, the market begins to empty. Sacks are tied, cloths folded, tables stacked. The cart pushers make their last trips, now heavy with goods going home instead of coming in. And as I walk out through the gates with my mother's shopping in a plastic bag, the noise falls behind me, and Bo feels suddenly quieter, as if its heart has slowed down for the night, waiting for next Saturday to beat strongly again.
::::

### Why it scores

- **Dominant impression**: *the heartbeat of Bo*, introduced in the first line and returned to in the last.
- **Organisation**: a route in **time** (morning noise → inside → people → midday → evening) and in **space** (entrance → cloth → food).
- **Senses**: the radio and the goat (sound), indigo and gold cloth (sight), smoked fish and palm oil (smell), the sun on zinc roofs (touch).
- **People, not just things**: Ya Fatu, the tailor, the schoolchildren bring the place to life.
- **Figurative language used well**: *kingdoms*, *like flags*, *calmly as queens*, the heartbeat image.
- **Length**: about 470 words of fresh, specific detail, with no repetition.

## Practice

:::reveal{title="1. Make this sentence vivid with two senses: 'The kitchen was busy before the wedding.'"}
*In the kitchen, five aunts stirred pots the size of drums, the air thick with the smell of jollof and the clatter of spoons against metal.*
:::

:::reveal{title="2. Choose a dominant impression for 'Describe your favourite teacher', and three details that show it."}
**Impression:** *strict but fair, and secretly kind.*

- She never raised her voice, but the class fell silent when she lifted one eyebrow.
- She marked every exercise book in red ink, with a comment for each student.
- She paid the exam fees of a boy whose father had died, and told no one.
:::

:::reveal{title="3. Replace the general verbs: 'The old man walked slowly to his chair and said something to his grandson.'"}
*The old man **shuffled** to his chair and **muttered** something to his grandson.*
:::

## Key points

- Choose a **dominant impression** and let every detail support it.
- **Organise** a place by a route, a person from outside to inside, an event in time order.
- Write with the **senses**, **precise** words and **a little** figurative language.
- Choose **a few vivid details**, not a long list of general ones.

## WASSCE examination focus

:::callout{type=waec-tests}
Descriptive essays are rewarded above all for **expression** (20 marks): precise vocabulary, sensory detail and controlled figurative language. Examiners mark down descriptions that are lists of general statements, that drift into storytelling with no description, or that use memorised "flowery" phrases that don't fit the subject.
:::
