Drop a small piece of sodium into water and it fizzes, melts into a silver ball and races across the surface; potassium bursts into a lilac flame. The **alkali metals** of group 1 are the most reactive metals of all. This lesson covers their properties and trends, their reactions, flame tests and the extraction and uses of sodium.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- name the alkali metals and relate their properties to their electron configuration
- describe their physical properties and explain how they are stored
- describe and write equations for their reactions with water, oxygen and chlorine
- state and explain the trends in reactivity and melting point down the group
- describe flame tests for lithium, sodium and potassium
- describe the extraction and uses of sodium

## The alkali metals

Group 1: **lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K)**, rubidium (Rb), caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr).

| Element | Configuration | Ion |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium | 2, 1 | Li⁺ |
| Sodium | 2, 8, 1 | Na⁺ |
| Potassium | 2, 8, 8, 1 | K⁺ |

Each has **one outer electron**, which it loses easily to form a **+1 ion** with a noble-gas configuration. That is why they are so reactive. They never occur free in nature; they are found as compounds such as NaCl and KCl.

## Physical properties

| Property | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Soft** | Can be **cut with a knife** |
| **Low density** | Li, Na and K **float on water** |
| **Low melting points** | Na melts at 98 °C, K at 63 °C |
| **Shiny when freshly cut** | But **tarnish** quickly in air as an oxide layer forms |
| **Good conductors** | Of heat and electricity |

**Storage:** under **paraffin oil** (kerosene), to keep them away from **air and water**, with which they react.

## Chemical properties

### With water

The metal reacts to form an **alkaline hydroxide** and **hydrogen**:

$$2\text{Na(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

| Metal | Observations |
|---|---|
| **Lithium** | Floats; fizzes steadily; does not melt |
| **Sodium** | Floats; **melts into a silvery ball** (the reaction is exothermic and Na has a low melting point); **darts about** propelled by hydrogen; fizzes; may burn with a yellow flame; solution turns red litmus blue |
| **Potassium** | Floats; reacts violently; the hydrogen **ignites with a lilac flame**; may spit |

### With oxygen

They **tarnish** rapidly in air and **burn** when heated:

$$4\text{Na(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Na}_2\text{O(s)}$$

Sodium burns with a **yellow flame**. In excess oxygen it forms the peroxide, Na₂O₂. The oxides dissolve in water to form alkalis: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH.

### With chlorine

Sodium burns in chlorine, forming white **sodium chloride**:

$$2\text{Na(s)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl(s)}$$

## Trends down the group

| Property | Trend (Li → K → Cs) | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactivity** | **Increases** | The outer electron is **further from the nucleus** and more **shielded**, so it is lost more easily |
| Atomic radius | Increases | More shells |
| First ionisation energy | Decreases | Outer electron held less strongly |
| **Melting point** | **Decreases** | Larger atoms; weaker metallic bonding |
| Density | Generally increases | |

## Flame tests

Dip a clean nichrome or platinum wire (cleaned in concentrated HCl) into the sample, then hold it in a non-luminous Bunsen flame.

| Ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| **Lithium**, Li⁺ | **Crimson red** |
| **Sodium**, Na⁺ | **Golden yellow** (persistent) |
| **Potassium**, K⁺ | **Lilac** (best seen through blue cobalt glass) |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | Brick red |
| Copper, Cu²⁺ | Blue-green |

## Extraction of sodium: the Downs cell

Sodium is too reactive to be extracted by carbon reduction. It is obtained by the **electrolysis of molten sodium chloride**.

- **Calcium chloride** is added to **lower the melting point** from 801 °C to about 600 °C, saving energy.
- **Cathode (iron, −):** Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na. The molten sodium floats up and is collected.
- **Anode (graphite, +):** 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Chlorine is a valuable by-product.
- A steel gauze keeps the sodium and chlorine apart, because they would otherwise react.

## Uses of sodium

- **Sodium vapour street lamps**, which give an intense yellow light
- **Coolant** (liquid sodium) in some nuclear reactors, because it conducts heat very well
- Making sodium compounds such as sodium peroxide and sodium cyanide
- Extracting titanium from titanium(IV) chloride

:::callout{type=local-example}
The orange-yellow street lights seen on some roads and junctions are **sodium vapour lamps**. Electricity excites sodium atoms, which give out the same golden-yellow light you see in a sodium **flame test**. The colour is a "fingerprint" of sodium.
:::

:::callout{type=common-mistake}
Alkali metals become **more** reactive down the group: potassium is more reactive than sodium. Their **melting points** fall down the group: potassium melts at a lower temperature than sodium.
:::

## Key points

- Group 1 metals have **one outer electron** and form **M⁺ ions**; they are stored under **paraffin oil**.
- They are **soft, low-density** (float on water) and have **low melting points**.
- With **water**: hydroxide + hydrogen (Na melts into a ball and darts; K gives a lilac flame). With **oxygen**: oxides; with **chlorine**: chlorides.
- **Reactivity increases** and **melting point decreases** down the group.
- **Flame tests:** Li crimson, **Na yellow**, **K lilac**.
- Sodium is extracted by **electrolysis of molten NaCl** (with CaCl₂) in the **Downs cell**.

## Quick check

1. Why are alkali metals stored under paraffin oil?
2. Why does sodium melt into a ball when it reacts with water?
3. Explain why potassium is more reactive than sodium.
4. What is the flame colour of potassium ions?

**Answers:**

1. To keep them from reacting with air (oxygen and moisture) and water.
2. The reaction is exothermic and sodium has a low melting point.
3. Potassium's outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
4. Lilac.
